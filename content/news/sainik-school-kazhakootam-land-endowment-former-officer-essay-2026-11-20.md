## A new voice enters the Kazhakootam land row

The fight over 20 acres of Sainik School Kazhakootam has a new document in it — and it is not a press release. A former Administrative Officer of the school has published a long essay, dated November 2026, arguing that the whole argument about the campus land starts from a wrong idea: that the land is spare.

His point, in one line: the land was not a gift with nothing attached. It was an endowment, given so the school could earn its own keep. And the reason it looks unused today, he says, is that the school stopped using it the way it was meant to be used.

## What happened, in order

In a letter dated **6 June 2026**, Thiruvananthapuram Mayor V. V. Rajesh asked the Ministry of Defence to allot **20 acres** of the school campus for a waste-to-energy plant, reported as a compressed biogas (CBG) plant to be set up with Bharat Petroleum Corporation Ltd. The campus houses more than **600 cadets**, plus teachers, non-teaching staff and their families, and a primary school runs inside it.

On **3 November 2026**, the Ministry's Sainik Schools Cell replied that the matter had been examined and directed the school and the corporation to conduct a **joint field assessment** of the proposal's feasibility. A team led by the mayor visited the campus in the first week of November, and parents, alumni and residents protested.

Then came the U-turn. The corporation has dropped the proposal to set up the CBG plant on the school premises, the mayor told The Times of India, saying the civic body has "no such plans in Kazhakootam" and will continue its decentralised waste plan. But the mayor also said the **centralised waste-treatment proposal itself is not being dropped**. The plant at the school is off the table for now. The city's search for a centralised site goes on.

## The essay's argument: why 250 acres?

Most of the argument so far has been about a plant. His argument is about the land itself. Sainik School Kazhakootam was established on **26 January 1962**, he writes, on about **300 acres** of undulating land provided by the state. In the early 2000s, about **75 acres were handed over to KINFRA on lease**, for compensation of approximately ₹3 crore meant for building a boundary wall. The campus today is about **225 acres**.

Why was so much land given at all? The Sainik Schools Society's Rules, he says, require the state to provide "around 250 acres of land, part of which should be arable land", expressly including land for a farm, piggery, poultry, fishery and dairy farm. The land was meant to earn: produce for the school mess, surpluses into a reserve fund, until "after a period of time, the school becomes fully self supporting".

That, he argues, is the point everyone forgets when they call the land spare. The original schools were given land *in place of* funding — newer schools run on far smaller campuses, but their owners pay for salaries, pensions and upkeep.

He also connects the unused land to the fee parents pay. In 1997, he writes, a cadet paid **₹11,000 a year**; today the annual fee is well above **₹1.5 lakh** and rises by about 10% every year. Scholarships for SC/ST cadets cover the full fee, but income-based scholarships for others have been capped for years — a "full" scholarship now amounts to only about **₹20,000**. A scheme created "to bring public school education within the reach of the common man" is drifting out of his reach, the essay says — while the land given to ease the cost of running the schools has been used less and less.

## Where this leaves parents

For parents of Kazhakootam cadets, the practical position is this: **no land has been allotted, and the corporation says it has no immediate plans at the school.** But the campus has faced claims on its land before, and the corporation's centralised waste-treatment search continues. The author's answer is to put the land to work — vacation camps and NDA/SSB coaching in empty hostels, leadership retreats for nearby Technopark companies, training areas for defence units and the NCC, and modern farms under a full-time Estate Manager, as the Rules already provide for. His defence, in short, is not a protest but a use: a campus that earns from its land, visibly, is much harder to describe as spare.

## FAQs

**Is the waste plant being built on Sainik School Kazhakootam's campus?**

No. The Thiruvananthapuram Corporation has said it has no immediate plans to set up the compressed biogas plant at the school, after protests from parents, alumni and residents. The mayor has said the city's separate centralised waste-treatment proposal is still open, so the wider issue is not closed.

**How much land did the corporation ask for?**

Twenty acres of the school campus, for a waste-to-energy plant reported as a compressed biogas plant to be run with Bharat Petroleum Corporation Ltd. The mayor's letter of 6 June 2026 asked the Ministry of Defence to allot the land.

**Who owns the land of Sainik School Kazhakootam?**

The Government of Kerala owns the land, according to the former Administrative Officer's essay. The school was set up in 1962 on about 300 acres provided by the state, and the essay argues no decision on any part of it can properly be taken without the state government at the table.

**Why was so much land given to a Sainik School in the first place?**

The Sainik Schools Society's rules require the state to provide around 250 acres, part of it arable, for playgrounds, farms, a dairy, poultry, piggery and fishery. The land was meant to earn income for the school — produce for the mess, surpluses into a reserve fund — so the school could become self-supporting and fees could stay low.
