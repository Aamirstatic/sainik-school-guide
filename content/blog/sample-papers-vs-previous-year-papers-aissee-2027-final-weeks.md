Walk into any bookshop in November and the AISSEE shelf tells you a story: twenty books, all promising the same thing, all full of "sample papers." Meanwhile the previous year papers — the actual papers NTA set in real exams — sit in a thin corner, sometimes just a PDF link a teacher shares on WhatsApp.

Parents ask me the same question every year: which one actually helps more? The honest answer is that they do different jobs, and with 79 days left to the 31 January 2027 exam, using them in the wrong order wastes the one thing you cannot buy back — time.

## What each one is actually good for

A previous year paper is a real NTA paper. The questions were set by the people who set this year's paper, in the same style, at the same difficulty, with the same traps. It tells your child three things no sample paper can: how hard the real paper feels, which topics NTA actually repeats, and how the 150 minutes (Class 6) or 180 minutes (Class 9) really disappear.

A sample paper is written by a publisher or a coaching institute. It is a guess — sometimes a good guess, sometimes not — at what NTA might ask. Its real value is volume: there are dozens of them, so your child can practise a weak section again and again without running out of material.

Here is the comparison, plainly:

|  | Previous year papers | Sample papers |
|---|---|---|
| **What it is** | Real NTA papers from past AISSEE exams | Publisher or coaching-made practice papers |
| **Best for** | Real difficulty, real timing, real question style | Volume — repeated practice of weak sections |
| **How many exist** | Few — a handful of real papers | Dozens — easy to find in books and online |
| **Biggest risk** | Running out of them too early | Difficulty may be easier or harder than the real exam |
| **Use it when** | Full-paper Sundays, timed, OMR-style | Weekday section practice, 30–45 minutes |
| **Never use it for** | Daily drilling (too precious to waste) | Judging your child's real exam readiness |

## The mistake I see every November

A parent buys five sample-paper books. The child solves paper after paper, scores 240 out of 300, and everyone relaxes. Then the real exam arrives and the paper feels different — the maths is wordier, the language section uses trickier synonyms, the clock runs faster. The child was practising against a mirror that flattered them.

The opposite mistake is just as common: a family guards the previous year papers like treasure, saving them "for the last month," and then the last month arrives and there is no time to analyse them properly.

## The 11-week plan: which paper, when

Today is 13 November. The exam is 31 January. That is 11 Sundays — 11 full-paper slots. Here is how to spend them.

| Week | Sunday full paper | Weekday practice |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 (13–26 Nov) | 1 previous year paper, timed | Sample papers, weakest section only |
| 3–5 (27 Nov–17 Dec) | 1 previous year paper every Sunday | Sample papers, two weak sections, 45 min a day |
| 6–8 (18 Dec–7 Jan) | Alternate: sample paper, then previous year paper | Sample papers + error-log revision |
| 9–10 (8–21 Jan) | 1 previous year paper each Sunday | Only revision of logged mistakes |
| 11 (22–30 Jan) | 1 light sample paper, mid-week only | No new papers — revision and rest |

A note on the numbers: three to five full previous year papers, solved properly and analysed honestly, is enough. If you have more, save one for the final Sunday before the exam — a familiar, real paper is the best confidence-builder there is. If you have fewer, even one or two real papers will calibrate your child better than a shelf of sample books.

## How to solve a paper so it actually teaches

Solving the paper is the easy half. This is the half that matters:

**Solve it like the real exam.** Same start time if you can — 2:00 PM, the actual exam slot. Same 150 minutes for Class 6, 180 for Class 9. Phone in another room. OMR-style bubbling on a separate sheet, not ticks in the book.

**Take a break, then review the same day.** Budget 45 minutes to an hour after every full paper. Go through every wrong answer and every guessed answer — the guesses matter as much as the mistakes, because a lucky guess hides a gap.

**Keep an error log.** One line per mistake: the topic, what went wrong (concept, silly error, time pressure, misread question), and the date. After three papers, the log will show you exactly which two or three topics are eating your child's marks. That is your revision list.

**Re-solve the wrong questions one week later.** Without looking at the solution. If your child gets it right, the concept is fixed. If not, it goes back into the queue. This single habit is worth more than ten extra sample papers.

## What about Class 9?

Everything above applies, with one difference: the Class 9 paper is longer and heavier — 180 minutes, 150 questions, 400 marks, with maths alone worth 200. That makes the previous year paper even more valuable for Class 9 children, because the real paper's time pressure in the maths section is something no sample paper reproduces well.

And remember the qualifying rule for both classes: 25% in each section and 40% aggregate. A paper is not just about the total — check the section scores every single time. A child scoring 260 out of 300 but failing one section has still failed.

## The short version

Previous year papers tell your child what the real exam is. Sample papers give them the reps to get better at it. Start with a real paper to find out where you stand, use sample papers to fix what it exposes, and finish with a real paper to prove the fix worked.

Eleven Sundays left. Make each one count.

## FAQs

**How many AISSEE previous year papers should a student solve before the exam?**

Three to five full previous year papers under timed conditions is enough. What matters more is the analysis afterwards — logging every mistake and fixing the weak topic before the next paper.

**Are sample papers enough if previous year papers are hard to find?**

Sample papers give useful practice volume, but they cannot replace previous year papers for understanding NTA's real difficulty and question style. Even one or two real papers teach more about the actual exam than five generic sample papers.

**When should a Class 6 student start solving full-length papers?**

Once the syllabus is broadly covered. With 79 days left, that moment is now for most children: one full paper every Sunday, plus section-wise sample-paper practice on weekdays.

**Should my child solve a paper and check answers the same day?**

Yes, but not immediately. Take a short break, then spend 45 minutes to an hour reviewing every wrong and guessed answer. A paper reviewed the same day teaches twice as much as one checked a week later.

**Can solving too many papers hurt preparation?**

Yes, if papers replace revision. A child who solves a paper a day but never revisits the mistakes is just rehearsing the same errors. One well-analysed paper a week beats five unreviewed ones.

**Which paper should my child solve first — previous year or sample?**

Start with a previous year paper. It sets the honest baseline: real difficulty, real timing, real section balance. Use sample papers after that for targeted practice on the weak sections it exposes.
