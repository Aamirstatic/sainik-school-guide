## The final stretch is a different game

The advice that worked in June — "build a routine, stay consistent" — is not the advice you need in October. Early preparation is about building habits. The final 100 days are about protecting morale. The syllabus is mostly covered or nearly covered; what decides the outcome now is whether your child walks into the exam hall believing they are ready.

And this is exactly when motivation wobbles. The notification still hasn't dropped, which makes the whole thing feel unreal. Mock scores plateau. Festivals arrive with their noise and sweets and cousins. Winter mornings make the bed very attractive. If your home feels like a low-grade negotiation every evening, you are not failing as a parent — you are experiencing the most normal phase of the AISSEE year.

The trick is that each part of the final stretch needs a different kind of parenting.

## The final 100 days in three phases

| Phase | Dates (approx.) | Your child's state | Your job as a parent |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Consolidation** | Mid-Oct to Diwali (8 Nov) | Syllabus nearly done; first full mocks; notification anxiety | Protect the routine; don't add new material; handle the notification wait calmly |
| **Grind** | Diwali to 31 Dec | Festival break, then the longest study block; mock scores wobble | Manage energy, not hours; keep sleep sacred; celebrate error-pattern fixes, not scores |
| **Tempering** | January | Revision only; nerves peak; nothing new can be learned | Reduce pressure visibly; protect confidence; logistics over lectures (admit card, centre, sleep) |

Notice what changes: in phase one you guard the *plan*, in phase two you guard the *child's energy*, in phase three you guard the *child's confidence*. Parents who apply phase-one tactics (more chapters, more hours) in phase three usually get tears, not marks.

## Handling the Diwali break without guilt or collapse

Diwali falls on 8 November this year, right in the middle of the consolidation phase. Every year, two mistakes repeat. Some families ban the festival entirely — the child studies through it, resentful, and burns out by December. Others surrender completely — ten days of nothing, and restarting feels like pushing a boulder uphill.

The middle path, agreed *in advance* with your child:

- **Fix the break dates together.** Two or three full festival days off, marked on the calendar. A break the child chose feels like a reward; a break imposed feels like a theft, and a break stolen feels like guilt.
- **Keep one tiny daily anchor.** Twenty minutes of formula revision or one reading passage. Not real study — just a thread connecting the days, so restarting takes an evening, not a week.
- **Resume the day after, no "easing in".** The longer the runway back, the harder it gets. First day back is a normal full day.

## Mock-test season: read the errors, not the score

December and January will bring mock tests — school mocks, coaching mocks, previous papers at home. Here is the conversation to avoid: "Only 212 out of 300? Sharma's son got 240." Here is the conversation to have: "Show me the 15 you got wrong. How many were silly mistakes — misread question, wrong bubble, calculation slip?"

In my experience watching students, the final-100-days score jump almost never comes from learning new chapters. It comes from killing repeat errors. A child who fixes their OMR bubbling habit and their habit of skipping the last line of word problems can gain more in January than in all of November. Make error-fixing the visible victory: "This week you cut silly mistakes from 9 to 4" is the most motivating sentence in this house right now.

If scores *fall* for two weeks straight, that is information, not failure. It usually means fatigue, not falling ability. The answer is more sleep and one lighter week — not more hours. ## Sentences for the final stretch

Children in the final 100 days hear everything at double volume. A few swaps that cost nothing:

- Instead of "Only 100 days left, you should be studying right now" → "What's the one thing you're finishing today?"
- Instead of "Your cousin scored 95%, why can't you?" → "Your mock score is up 18 marks from last month. That's the trend that matters."
- Instead of "If you don't clear this, the whole year is wasted" → "We're proud of how hard you've worked. The paper is one morning; your effort is the whole year."
- Instead of silence about the notification → "The form isn't out yet; the day it drops, we'll fill it together. Nothing to worry about till then."

The pattern: pressure on *process* (today's task, this week's errors), warmth on *outcome* (the result, the rank, the future). Children can control the first and cannot control the second. Motivating someone means aiming your words at what they can actually do.

## When to push and when to back off: a quick checklist

Push harder on:
- [ ] Wake-up time and study slots — routine is non-negotiable
- [ ] The mock schedule — skipped mocks are skipped feedback
- [ ] Error-log review — the single highest-value habit of January
- [ ] Sleep timing — a tired brain cannot consolidate memory

Back off on:
- [ ] Comparing scores with other children — it converts motivation into anxiety
- [ ] Adding new books or material after December — the syllabus is closed
- [ ] Discussing "what if you don't clear it" as a threat — discuss it once, calmly, as a plan, and move on
- [ ] Studying past 10 PM in January — the exam is a morning paper; the brain should be trained for mornings

One last thing, and I say this as a principal who has watched hundreds of Class 6 children through this season: your child will remember how home *felt* during these 100 days long after they forget their mock scores.

## FAQs

**How many days are left for AISSEE 2027?**

As of 14 October 2026, roughly 109 days remain to the tentative exam date of 31 January 2027, per the NTA examination calendar. The exact date will be confirmed only in the official NTA bulletin.

**My child has lost interest in studying with 100 days left. Is this normal?**

Completely normal. Motivation dips when the exam feels both far away and impossibly close. Short, visible goals — one timed drill, one weak chapter — restore momentum faster than lectures about working harder.

**Should my child study through the Diwali holidays?**

A full stop for 2–3 festival days is fine and often restorative; a 10-day collapse is not. Agree the break dates in advance, keep one light daily habit like 20 minutes of revision, and resume the routine the day after.

**My child's mock scores have plateaued. Should I be worried?**

A plateau in December is typical and not a verdict. Track error patterns instead of the score: the same silly mistakes repeating means the child needs process fixes, not more hours.

**When should a parent push harder and when should they back off?**

Push on routine and consistency — wake time, study slots, mock schedule. Back off on outcomes — scores, ranks, comparisons. Pressure on what the child controls builds confidence; pressure on results builds fear.
