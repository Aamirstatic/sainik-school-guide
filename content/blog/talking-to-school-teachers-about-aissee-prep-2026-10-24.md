## The adult your child spends the most time with

Here's a thought most AISSEE parents miss. You are planning coaching, mock tests, guidebooks and timetables — and meanwhile, the person who watches your child solve maths problems five days a week, who already knows whether they rush through word problems or freeze on geometry, has heard nothing about the plan. That person is the school teacher. And honestly, most teachers are not opposed to AISSEE prep. They are opposed to being surprised by it.

Last year, a parent told us their version: their son's Class 5 teacher only learned about AISSEE when the boy started skipping homework to finish a mock test. The teacher, quite reasonably, assumed he was slacking. One short meeting earlier would have turned that teacher from an obstacle into an ally.

## Why teachers matter more than tutors for this

A tutor sees your child a few hours a week. A school teacher sees the unguarded version: who copies homework, who understands fractions versus who memorised the steps, who goes quiet when a new chapter starts. That daily evidence beats any diagnostic test you buy.

Teachers can also do three things no tutor can: ease school workload around your big prep days, tell you chapter by chapter where the school syllabus already covers AISSEE ground (Class 6 maths leans heavily on Class 5 NCERT work, and a teacher knows which chapters your child has actually mastered), and spot burnout — a child quiet in class for two weeks is telling you something no mock-test score will.

None of this happens if the teacher doesn't know. So the first job is a good first conversation.

## The first meeting: what to say, word for word

Book a normal parent-teacher slot — don't ambush a teacher at the school gate. Ten focused minutes beat thirty rambling ones. Go in with one clear message:

> "My child is preparing for the AISSEE entrance exam for Sainik School admission — the exam is expected on 31 January 2027. I'm not asking you to teach extra syllabus or change anything for the whole class. I'd just like your honest view of where he stands in maths and language, and whether we can adjust one or two homework clashes when his mock tests fall on school-test days."

Notice what that script does: it names the exam and date, says you're not asking for special treatment for the whole class, and asks for the teacher's judgment, not their labour.

Then ask three specific questions, and write the answers down in front of the teacher — it signals you actually value them:

1. **"Which two chapters does my child struggle with most in your subject?"** Not "is he weak in maths" — that question gets a shrug. Two chapters gets a real answer.
2. **"Is there anything in class behaviour I should know — rushing, not asking doubts, copying?"** Teachers notice behaviour parents never see.
3. **"If a school test and his practice test clash, who should I inform, and how early?"** This one question prevents most future friction.

## Requests that work — and ones that don't

Teachers respond to small, bounded asks. They quietly file away the big, vague ones. Here's the difference:

| Ask this (works) | Don't ask this (backfires) |
|---|---|
| "Can he skip the repeated practice worksheet on Sundays when he has a full mock test?" | "Please reduce his homework overall" |
| "Could you tell us a week early when a unit test is scheduled?" | "Please don't give him tests in January" |
| "Which chapters should we revise first at home?" | "Please coach him for AISSEE in class" |
| "If he's absent for a school event, may he get the notes the next day?" | "Please excuse him from school regularly for coaching" |
| "Would you mark his classwork a little strictly, so we see real gaps?" | "Please give him extra marks to build confidence" |

The pattern is simple: you're asking for information and small scheduling flexibility, never for grades or exemptions. A teacher who feels respected will volunteer more than you asked — the extra worksheet, the quiet word after class.

## If the teacher pushes back

It happens. Some teachers worry that entrance-exam pressure harms young children, and they say so bluntly. Some schools discourage outside exams on principle. Here's how to handle it without burning the relationship: don't defend the dream in the meeting. You will not win a philosophy debate in ten minutes, and you don't need to — admission to Sainik School is decided by the AISSEE written exam, qualifying marks and counselling merit, not by any teacher's recommendation.

Instead, take the useful part. Say: "I hear you — and I don't want him stressed either. What academic gaps do you see that we should fix first?" Almost every sceptical teacher will answer that question honestly, and their answer is exactly what you came for. Thank them, leave gracefully, and keep the door open. A teacher who disagreed with you in October can still be the one who flags a weak chapter in December.

One boundary: if discouragement turns into active obstruction — refusing notes after genuine absence, say — that's a school-management conversation, in writing, through the proper channel. Most cases never get there.

## The 4-week plan to get teachers on your side

Don't do all of this in one meeting — spread it over a month:

| Week | What you do | What it achieves |
|---|---|---|
| Week 1 | Book the meeting; use the script above; ask the three questions | Teacher knows the plan and feels respected |
| Week 2 | Share a one-page note: mock-test Sundays, the two weak chapters, your home routine | Teacher can connect classwork to prep without extra effort |
| Week 3 | Short check-in: "You flagged fractions — we're working on it; anything new?" | Teacher sees you acted on their advice — the strongest trust-builder there is |
| Week 4 | Agree on the clash protocol: who to inform, how early, for January's exam run-up | No surprises in the final, high-pressure month |

Then keep the rhythm light: a brief check-in every three to four weeks. The parent teachers help most is prepared, brief, grateful — and actually follows through. When your child improves in the flagged chapter, say so.

## What teachers can't do — so don't expect it

Let's be honest about the limits: a teacher cannot change the syllabus for one child, guarantee admission, or replace a study plan. Their job is the classroom; yours is everything outside it. When both sides stay in their lane and talk across the boundary, the child gets the best of both.

So this weekend, if you haven't already, book that meeting. Ten minutes, one honest script, three specific questions. It's the cheapest, highest-return move in your whole AISSEE plan — and it costs nothing at all.

## FAQs

**Should I tell my child's teacher about AISSEE preparation?**

Yes. Teachers who know can adjust homework load around mock tests, flag weak chapters they already see in class, and excuse or reschedule clashes. A teacher who finds out by accident is far less helpful than one you told early.

**What exactly should I ask a teacher for?**

Ask for specific, small things: which chapters your child is weakest in, permission to skip one repetitive homework set on mock-test Sundays, and a heads-up before unit tests that clash with the AISSEE timetable. Vague requests like "please help my child" get ignored; specific ones get done.

**What if the teacher discourages AISSEE or Sainik School?**

Stay calm and do not argue the dream in the meeting. Thank them, ask what academic gaps they see, and take that feedback home. You do not need the teacher's permission for your child's goal, but you do need their observations about maths or language basics.

**How often should parents meet teachers during AISSEE prep?**

One focused meeting to start, then a short check-in every three to four weeks is plenty. Teachers handle dozens of children; a brief, prepared visit beats frequent long ones. Save urgent contact for genuine clashes like a test on a mock-test day.

**Can a teacher's recommendation help in Sainik School admission?**

No. AISSEE admission is decided by the written exam, qualifying marks and counselling merit, not by school recommendations. A teacher's real value is academic: spotting gaps early and keeping schoolwork and entrance prep from fighting each other.
