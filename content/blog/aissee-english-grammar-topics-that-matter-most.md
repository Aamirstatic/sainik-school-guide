NTA does **not** publish topic-wise weightage — the priority rankings below are 🔶 **EXPECTED**, drawn from the pattern of previous years' papers, not from any official syllabus breakup.

English is the section parents underestimate and children mishandle. Here's the truth most people won't tell you: grammar in the AISSEE isn't about knowing every rule in a fat grammar book. It's about a small set of topics that show up **again and again**, and a few that look important but barely matter. Study the first group hard, touch the second group lightly, and your child picks up easy marks that most candidates leave on the table.

## The honest starting point

NTA has never released a topic-wise breakup of the English paper. Anyone quoting exact percentages — "tenses carry 12%!" — is inventing numbers. What we *can* do is look at the last few AISSEE papers and see which question types repeat. The table below is built from that: which grammar topics genuinely dominate, and how much of your effort each deserves. Treat it as a teacher's map, not NTA's blueprint.

## The priority table: where the marks live

| Priority | Grammar topic | Why it matters | Suggested effort share |
|----------|---------------|----------------|------------------------|
| 🔴 High | **Tenses** (all forms, sequence of tenses) | The single most recurring topic — direct questions plus the foundation for voice and narration | ~20% |
| 🔴 High | **Articles** (a/an/the) | 2–4 quick questions almost every year; fast marks if practised | ~10% |
| 🔴 High | **Prepositions** | Constantly tested, especially with verbs (phrasal use); mistakes are costly | ~10% |
| 🔴 High | **Subject-verb agreement** | Error-spotting favourite; one rule repeated in many disguises | ~10% |
| 🟡 Medium | **Active–passive voice** | Regular appearance, particularly for Class 9; formula-driven, so it rewards drilling | ~10% |
| 🟡 Medium | **Direct–indirect narration** | Regular for Class 9; a bit of tense-sequence logic and it's yours | ~10% |
| 🟡 Medium | **Error spotting / sentence improvement** | Not a separate topic — it's the *format* the high-priority topics appear in. Practise topics through this format | ~10% |
| 🟢 Lower | **Synonyms, antonyms, one-word substitution** | Appear, but are vocabulary, not grammar — prepare separately | ~5% |
| 🟢 Lower | **Idioms and phrases** | A question or two, unpredictable; learn-as-you-go | ~5% |
| 🟢 Lower | **Conjunctions, degrees of comparison** | Tested occasionally; cover once, revise once | ~5% |
| 🟢 Lower | **Comprehension passages** | Usually included in the paper; needs reading practice, not rules | rest |

Notice the asymmetry: five topics carry the overwhelming share. If your child is short on time, drill those five and you'll have covered most of what grammar asks.

## The mistakes I see every year

After decades of watching students take this exam, the same three errors show up:

1. **Learning rules, not sentences.** A child who can recite "the past perfect is formed with had + past participle" but freezes on *She had left before I arrived* has memorised, not learned. Rule first, then twenty sentences with it. Every time.
2. **Ignoring articles and prepositions.** They're boring, they're tiny, and they carry real marks. Children skip them for "bigger" topics and lose 6–8 easy marks. Don't.
3. **Preparing comprehension like it's grammar.** Reading passages don't test rules — they test attention. The only preparation that works is reading: ten minutes of a storybook or children's newspaper daily, for months. No shortcut.

## A 4-week grammar routine that fits real life

Assume your child gives English **45 minutes a day, 5 days a week**. Here's how those five weeks break down (and no, I'm not selling a 30-day miracle — this is one cycle; repeat it with harder material):

**Week 1 — Tenses.** One tense family per day (present, past, future, perfects), each ending with 20 mixed-tense sentences written from scratch. Not MCQs — *writing*. If they can write it correctly, the MCQ is easy.

**Week 2 — Articles + Prepositions.** These pair naturally. 30 fill-in-the-blank questions daily, then a correction pass explaining *why*. The explanations are where learning happens, not the solving.

**Week 3 — Subject-verb agreement + Voice + Narration.** Teach the formula, drill 15–20 conversions daily. Voice and narration share the tense logic — children who got Week 1 right find these two weeks click fast.

**Week 4 — Mixed mock sets.** Full English sections from previous years' papers, timed. Every wrong answer gets rewritten correctly in a notebook with the rule stated aloud. That notebook is the real textbook now.

For Class 9 specifically, give narration and voice a heavier share — the paper has historically asked more of these at the senior level. And remember Class 9 English is tested in English only (no Hindi version of the paper).

## How grammar connects to everything else

Here's something parents miss: grammar isn't just the English section. The intelligence section's verbal reasoning — analogies, series, statement questions — is easier when a child parses sentences cleanly. Even Maths word problems reward precise reading. [Strong English fundamentals lift the whole paper]({{< relref "aissee-intelligence-section-complete-guide.md" >}}), not just 50 marks.

And while there's **no negative marking** in the AISSEE — [attempt everything, always]({{< relref "aissee-negative-marking-honest-answer.md" >}}) — grammar questions are exactly the kind where eliminating two wrong options and guessing between the remaining two pays off. A child with decent grammar instinct can manufacture marks this way. A child without it just guesses blind.

## FAQs

**Which grammar topics are most important for AISSEE Class 6 English?**
Tenses, articles, prepositions, and subject-verb agreement — these recur most across past papers. Class 6 papers also lean on simpler formats like fill-in-the-blanks and sentence rearrangement.

**Is there a difference between Class 6 and Class 9 grammar in AISSEE?**
Yes. Both have 25 questions worth 50 marks, but Class 9 goes deeper: active–passive voice and direct–indirect narration appear more regularly at Class 9, while Class 6 stays closer to tenses, articles, and basic usage. Class 9 English is also English-medium only.

**How many grammar questions come in the AISSEE English paper?**
The paper has 25 English questions (50 marks), but NTA doesn't specify how many are grammar versus vocabulary versus comprehension. Based on past-paper patterns, grammar-type questions form the majority — exact numbers vary year to year.

**Should my child use a grammar workbook or NCERT books?**
Both. NCERT builds the base understanding; a good practice workbook (or previous papers) supplies the reps. [Here's an honest breakdown of how to use each]({{< relref "aissee-how-many-study-hours-class-6.md" >}}). The winning formula: understand from NCERT, drill from workbooks, verify with past papers.

**Can grammar be prepared in the last month?**
Honestly? Only if the base exists. Grammar is a skill built over months, not a syllabus to cram. In the final month, switch to error revision — past mistakes, mixed mock sets, and timed English sections. [A structured daily plan helps here]({{< relref "aissee-daily-timetable-class-6.md" >}}).

**Do examiners deduct marks for wrong answers in the English section?**
No — the AISSEE has no negative marking anywhere in the paper. Every question deserves an answer.
