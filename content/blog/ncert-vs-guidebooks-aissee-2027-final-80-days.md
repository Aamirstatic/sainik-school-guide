## The question changes when the exam is 81 days away

Ask a parent in June whether NCERT or guidebooks are better for AISSEE, and you get a syllabus debate. Ask the same parent today, 11 November, with the exam on **31 January 2027** — 81 days away — and it becomes a time-management question instead.

The honest answer has always been that both have a job to do. NCERT builds the concept. A guidebook drills the question type. Previous-year papers test both under pressure. What changes in the final 80 days is the balance: the time for building concepts from scratch is nearly over, and the time for timed practice has begun. Buying a new thick guidebook now is the single most common waste of the final phase — it feels like preparation, but it eats the revision hours that actually move scores.

## What each source is actually for

| Source | What it does well | What it cannot do | Use in the final 80 days |
|---|---|---|---|
| NCERT textbooks (Class 5 for Class 6; Class 8 for Class 9) | Concepts in the exact language the paper draws on; Maths foundations, English grammar, EVS/Science basics | No exam pattern, no timed practice, no Intelligence section coverage | Keep as the reference — dip in to fix a weak concept, do not re-read cover to cover |
| Guidebooks / practice books | Large banks of Intelligence and Maths question types; chapter-wise drills | Often padded with off-pattern questions; some carry errors and outdated patterns | Use only for timed section drills in your weakest section; drop the rest |
| Previous-year AISSEE papers | The real pattern, real difficulty, real time pressure | Limited in number; cannot teach a concept from zero | The core of the final phase — at least 5 full papers under timed conditions |
| Mock tests | Exam-hall simulation, OMR bubbling practice, stamina | Quality varies; a bad mock teaches bad habits | One full mock a week, reviewed the same day with an error log |

The pattern to notice: NCERT and previous-year papers are the two sources that never mislead. A guidebook can — a question type that never appears in AISSEE, an answer key with an error, a "guaranteed questions" list that is really a guess. That is why the rule for the final phase is simple: **NCERT to fix, papers to test, guidebooks only to drill a known weakness.**

## Class 6: what to use, section by section

The Class 6 paper is 150 minutes, 125 questions, 300 marks — Language 25 questions for 50 marks, Mathematics 50 for 150, Intelligence 25 for 50, General Knowledge 25 for 50, with no negative marking.

- **Mathematics (150 of 300 marks — half the paper).** Class 5 NCERT Maths is the base: fractions, decimals, percentages, geometry, mensuration. If a concept is weak, go back to the NCERT chapter and work its examples. Then drill that topic from a practice book in timed sets of 20 questions. Maths is where guidebooks earn their keep — but only as a drill bank, never as the first place a concept is met.
- **Language (50 marks).** Class 5 NCERT English — comprehension passages, grammar, vocabulary in context. Guidebooks add little here beyond extra passages; previous-year papers show the passage length and question style better than any guidebook chapter.
- **Intelligence (50 marks).** NCERT has no Intelligence section, so this is the one place a practice book is genuinely needed: series, analogy, classification, coding. Drill 15 minutes a day, every day, from one source — and stop collecting new question types after December.
- **General Knowledge (50 marks).** Class 5 EVS plus daily reading of simple current affairs. Guidebook GK lists are the least reliable material in the market — long lists of facts that rarely match the paper. Previous-year papers show which GK themes actually repeat: defence, sports, awards, national symbols.

## Class 9: the same logic, heavier books

The Class 9 paper is 180 minutes, 150 questions, 400 marks — Mathematics 50 questions for 200 marks, and Intelligence, English, General Science and Social Science at 25 questions and 50 marks each, all in English, with no negative marking.

Here the NCERT base is Class 8: Maths (algebra, geometry, mensuration), Science and Social Science. The same rule applies — NCERT to fix a concept, previous-year papers to test it, guidebooks only for timed drills in the weakest section. The one difference: Class 9 Maths carries 200 of 400 marks, so a weak Maths chapter costs twice as much as in Class 6. If there is one place to spend guidebook drill time, it is here.

## The final-80-days split

| Phase | Dates | NCERT | Guidebook / drills | Papers & mocks |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Now – 30 Nov | 11 – 30 Nov (20 days) | Fix the last weak concepts, chapter by chapter | Timed drills only in the weakest section | 2 full previous-year papers |
| December | 1 – 31 Dec (31 days) | Reference only — no new chapters | 15 min/day Intelligence; Maths drill sets | 2 full papers + 2 full mocks |
| January | 1 – 30 Jan (30 days) | Closed, except the error log | None — stop new material entirely | 1 paper + 1 mock per week, then taper |
| Exam week | 25 – 31 Jan | Nothing new | Nothing new | Admit card check, OMR practice, rest |

The qualifying bar is worth remembering while planning: 25% in each section and 40% in aggregate for General, OBC (NCL) and defence categories in the existing Sainik Schools (SC/ST candidates are exempt from the minimum marks there). A section ignored for 80 days is a section that can fail the whole paper, however strong the rest is.

## Three mistakes to avoid now

**Buying a new guidebook in November.** It will not be finished, and the unfinished chapters will create anxiety without adding marks. Use what you already own.

**Re-reading NCERT cover to cover.** At 81 days out, re-reading is the comfort activity, not the scoring one. Open NCERT only to fix a concept the error log has flagged — then close it and drill.

**Collecting PDFs.** Ten downloaded paper sets that are never solved score zero. Five solved papers, reviewed properly, beat fifty downloaded ones. If your child has not solved a full paper under timed conditions yet, that is the first job this week — before any new book.

The NCERT-versus-guidebook debate has a boring, correct answer: NCERT is the syllabus, guidebooks are the gym, and previous-year papers are the match. With 81 days left, spend the most time on the match.

## FAQs

**Is NCERT enough for AISSEE 2027?**

NCERT is enough for the concepts, especially in Class 6 Maths, English and GK foundations — but it is not enough on its own for exam practice. You still need previous-year papers and timed mock practice to learn the question pattern, speed and OMR bubbling.

**Should I buy a new guidebook now, 80 days before the exam?**

No. With 81 days left, a new thick guidebook is the wrong purchase. Use the NCERT books your child already has, plus previous-year papers and one practice source you already own. New material this late costs revision time.

**Which NCERT books matter most for AISSEE Class 6?**

Class 5 Maths and English, and Class 5 EVS for the General Knowledge and science foundations. For Class 9, Class 8 Maths, Science and Social Science carry the weight, with Class 8 English for the language section.

**How many previous-year papers should my child solve before 31 January?**

Aim for at least 5 full previous-year papers under timed conditions, plus section-wise practice from older papers. Quality matters more than count: every paper should be followed by an error-log review the same day.

**Are guidebooks bad for AISSEE preparation?**

No — guidebooks are useful as practice banks, especially for Intelligence and Maths question types. The mistake is treating a guidebook as the syllabus. NCERT builds the concept; the guidebook drills it; previous-year papers test it.

**What should we stop doing in the final 30 days?**

Stop starting new chapters and new books. The last 30 days are for revision, mock tests, error-log review and OMR practice. Anything your child has not met by 1 January is better left than crammed.
