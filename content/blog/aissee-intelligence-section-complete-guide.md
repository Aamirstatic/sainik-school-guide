**The Intelligence section is the most under-prepared — and the most scoreable — part of the AISSEE paper.** Maths gets the tuition classes. English gets the grammar books. GK gets the current-affairs apps. And Intelligence? Most children meet it for the first time on exam day, stare at a series like 3, 6, 11, 18, 27… and freeze.

That is a tragedy, because these 50 marks are the cheapest marks on the paper. No syllabus to memorise. No formulas to revise. Just the ability to spot patterns — a skill you can train like a muscle in 15 minutes a day. I have seen average students pull 40+ out of 50 here with three months of the right routine. Here's how.

## What the section actually tests

The Intelligence (mental ability/reasoning) section asks your child to think, not to recall. Every year the same families of questions appear. Learn the families, and the exam stops feeling like a surprise.

**The seven families that matter:**

1. **Number and letter series** — "2, 5, 10, 17, 26, ?" (add 3, 5, 7, 9 — next is +11 = 37). The single most common question type. Always attempt first.
2. **Coding-decoding** — "If CAT is written as DBU, how is DOG written?" (each letter +1: EPH). Children love these once they get the trick.
3. **Odd one out** — find the word, number, or figure that doesn't belong. Teaches categorisation.
4. **Analogies** — "Book : Pages :: Wall : ?" (Bricks). Vocabulary plus logic.
5. **Direction sense** — "Ravi walks 5 km north, then 3 km east…" Draw the diagram. Always draw the diagram.
6. **Blood relations** — "A is B's brother's wife's son…" Painful in words, trivial once you sketch a family tree.
7. **Mirror/water images and embedded figures** — spatial reasoning; the questions children find hardest to visualise and easiest to master with practice sheets.

## The 15-minute daily system

Here's the thing: reasoning is a perishable skill. A child who drills series for a week and then stops loses the edge in ten days. What works is a short, daily, never-missed routine. Fifteen minutes. That's it — less time than one round of a mobile game.

**The weekly drill cycle** (repeat every week until the exam):

| Day | Focus (15 min) | How |
|-----|----------------|-----|
| Monday | Number series | 20 series questions, timed — spot the operation |
| Tuesday | Coding-decoding | 15 questions; write the A–Z number grid once, reuse it |
| Wednesday | Odd one out + analogies | 20 mixed questions, say the reason aloud |
| Thursday | Direction sense + blood relations | 10 questions, **always with a rough diagram** |
| Friday | Mirror/water images | 15 questions from a figure sheet |
| Saturday | Mixed revision | 25 questions from the week's weakest family |
| Sunday | Timed sprint | 25 questions in 20 minutes, then error log |

The error log is the whole point. After every session, your child writes down *why* each wrong answer was wrong in one line: "didn't check alternate positions in series." Within three weeks, the same mistakes stop appearing. That's the system working.

## Three rules that win marks

**Rule 1: Attempt every single question.** There is no negative marking in AISSEE — CONFIRMED from the 2026 bulletin. An unattempted Intelligence question is a gift you refused to open. Even a 25% guess has positive expected value. (Read our full take on [why you should attempt everything](/blog/aissee-negative-marking-honest-answer/).)

**Rule 2: Speed comes from recognition, not calculation.** Nobody "solves" a series by deep thought in 60 seconds — they *recognise* it. Recognition comes from volume: 500+ questions seen before exam day. Start with untimed accuracy, then compress. A good target: 25 questions in 20 minutes by December.

**Rule 3: Draw, don't stare.** Direction sense, blood relations, and embedded figures punish children who try to hold everything in their head. A 10-second diagram turns a "hard" question into a trivial one. Teach your child that rough work is not cheating — it's the method.

## What not to do

Honestly? Don't buy a 400-page "mental ability" encyclopedia and assign chapters. Those books are built for SSC and banking exams — the difficulty is wrong, the question styles are wrong, and they demoralise 11-year-olds. For AISSEE Class 6, a good Class 5–6 reasoning workbook plus previous-year AISSEE papers is enough. For Class 9, add RIMC/RMS reasoning papers — same difficulty band.

And don't let your child practise Intelligence only on weekends. The section rewards frequency, not marathon sessions. Fifteen minutes daily beats two hours on Sunday. Every time.

## A quick self-check before December

By mid-December, your child should be able to: (a) finish 25 mixed reasoning questions in 20 minutes, (b) score 18+ consistently, and (c) explain the trick behind every question they got right — not just the answer, the *trick*. If they can teach it, they own it. If they can't, the weak family gets the next Saturday session.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many marks is the Intelligence section in AISSEE?**
In both Class 6 and Class 9, the Intelligence section carries 25 questions × 2 marks = 50 marks. That's one-sixth of the Class 6 paper (300 marks) and one-eighth of the Class 9 paper (400 marks).

**Is the Intelligence section difficult for Class 6 students?**
It's unfamiliar, not difficult. The questions need no memorised syllabus — only pattern recognition, which improves fast with daily 15-minute practice. Most children find it becomes their favourite section within a month.

**Which book is best for AISSEE Intelligence preparation?**
A Class 5–6 mental ability/reasoning workbook plus previous-year AISSEE papers covers Class 6. Avoid books written for SSC/banking exams — the difficulty level is wrong for 10–12-year-olds.

**How much time should my child spend on Intelligence daily?**
Fifteen focused minutes a day, following a weekly cycle of question families, beats long weekend sessions. Frequency matters more than duration for reasoning skills.

**Should my child attempt all Intelligence questions in the exam?**
Yes. AISSEE has no negative marking, so every unattempted question is a wasted opportunity. Attempt all 25, starting with series and coding questions where confidence is highest.

**Does the Class 9 Intelligence section differ from Class 6?**
The format is the same (25 questions, 50 marks) but Class 9 questions are set at a higher difficulty — more complex series, multi-step coding, and trickier spatial reasoning. Class 9 students should practise with RIMC and RMS reasoning papers too.
