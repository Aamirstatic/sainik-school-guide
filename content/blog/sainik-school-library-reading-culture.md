## The quietest room in a loud school

A Sainik School day is loud: PT commands at dawn, assembly, classes, games, house competitions. The library is the one room that runs on a different energy. Cadets go there for library periods, for prep, for newspaper reading before a debate, and — the part parents rarely hear about — simply to read something that is not a textbook.

That matters more than it sounds. AISSEE does not only test what a child has memorised from a syllabus. The Class 6 and Class 9 papers both reward a child who can read an unseen passage and actually understand it, who recognises a current-affairs name, who can reason through an unfamiliar question instead of freezing. Those are reading skills, and they are built in exactly the way a library builds them: slowly, daily, without an exam at the end of every chapter.

## How reading fits into a cadet's day

Reading at a Sainik School is not a single activity; it shows up in several places across the day:

- **Library periods** in the academic timetable, where cadets browse, borrow and read under a teacher's supervision.
- **Newspaper reading**, often tied to morning assembly news or house current-affairs discussions, where cadets learn to summarise a story in a few lines.
- **Prep periods**, where reading beyond the textbook — a reference book, a magazine, a biography — is a normal use of study time.
- **Debates, quizzes and declamation**, where the cadet who reads widely has something to say and the cadet who only reads textbooks often does not.
- **Free reading before lights-out**, the habit senior cadets pass down to juniors: a few pages a night, no test attached.

None of this replaces syllabus study. But it explains something parents notice after a year or two: the cadets who sound most confident in interviews and group discussions are rarely the ones who studied the most hours. They are usually the ones who read the most widely.

## What cadets actually read

Forget the image of a library full of exam guides. A typical Sainik School library shelf, and a typical cadet's reading week, covers far more ground:

| Reading type | Why it helps | Good starting point |
|---|---|---|
| Children's newspapers and magazines | Current affairs, vocabulary, summarising skill | One children's newspaper or magazine, read daily |
| NCERT textbooks, read properly | The AISSEE syllabus itself, understood not skimmed | The child's own Class 5 or Class 8 books |
| Biographies of officers and freedom fighters | GK, values, and interview answers with substance | One short, age-appropriate biography at a time |
| Science and geography non-fiction | The "why" behind textbook facts | Short illustrated non-fiction, not heavy reference books |
| Storybooks and novels | Comprehension speed, vocabulary, love of reading | Whatever the child enjoys — enjoyment is the point |
| Previous years' question papers | Exam familiarity, not reading for pleasure | Used as practice, kept separate from free reading |

The last row matters: exam practice is not reading. A child who only ever reads to be tested will stop reading the day the test ends. The library habit survives because it was never only about the test.

## A reading plan parents can start at home

You do not need to wait for admission to build this habit. A child preparing for AISSEE can start now, in twenty to thirty minutes a day. Here is a simple weekly plan that fits around school and AISSEE preparation:

| Day | Reading slot (20–30 minutes) |
|---|---|
| Monday | Children's newspaper: read two stories, retell each in three sentences |
| Tuesday | NCERT chapter, read aloud slowly — understanding, not speed |
| Wednesday | Storybook or novel of the child's choice, purely for pleasure |
| Thursday | Children's newspaper again, plus one short science or geography piece |
| Friday | Biography or non-fiction: one chapter, one thing learned, told to a parent |
| Saturday | Free choice — magazine, comic, storybook, anything the child picks |
| Sunday | Rest, or finish the week's unfinished book |

Two rules make this work. First, the slot is fixed — after homework, or before bed — not "whenever there is time", because there never is. Second, the child talks about what they read, even for two minutes. A book nobody talks about is a book half-read.

## The parent's checklist: building a reader before admission

- [ ] Fix one daily reading slot of 20–30 minutes and protect it for a month.
- [ ] Keep one children's newspaper or magazine coming into the house regularly.
- [ ] Read the child's NCERT chapters with them sometimes — it shows reading is valued, not just assigned.
- [ ] Ask "what did you read today?" the way you ask "what did you study today?"
- [ ] Let the child choose at least one book a week purely for pleasure, with no test attached.
- [ ] Visit a library together at least once before admission, so the building feels familiar, not formal.
- [ ] Do not buy a shelf of books at once — add one good book at a time, and finish it before buying the next.

## What reading does not do

Honesty matters here. Reading will not, on its own, get a child into Sainik School. The written exam still needs syllabus study, practice papers, and timed mocks. A child who reads novels all evening and never opens a maths book will not clear AISSEE, and any guide that suggests otherwise is selling comfort, not preparation.

What reading does is change the quality of the preparation that does happen. Passages feel easier. GK questions feel familiar rather than foreign. Interviews feel like conversations rather than interrogations. That edge is real, but it is an edge — not a substitute.

## The habit that outlasts the exam

Long after AISSEE, the cadets who keep reading are the ones who keep learning: officers who read beyond their branch, teachers, doctors, civil servants. Sainik Schools know this, which is why the library survives in a timetable that could easily have filled every hour with drill and classes. It is the school's bet that a cadet who reads becomes an officer who thinks.

Parents can place the same bet at home, starting tonight, with one book and twenty minutes.

## FAQs

**Do Sainik Schools have libraries?**
Yes. Sainik Schools are residential schools with library facilities as part of their campus infrastructure, and library periods are part of the school routine. The size and collection vary by school, so check your child's school handbook for its exact library timings.

**Does reading beyond textbooks help in AISSEE?**
Yes, indirectly. AISSEE tests general knowledge, English comprehension and reasoning, and all three improve with regular reading. Reading does not replace syllabus study, but a child who reads daily usually handles unseen passages and GK questions with more confidence.

**What should a Class 5 or Class 8 child read for AISSEE preparation?**
Age-appropriate newspapers for children, NCERT textbooks read properly rather than skimmed, simple biographies of armed forces officers and freedom fighters, and short non-fiction on science and geography. The goal is understanding, not memorising facts.

**How much daily reading is enough before AISSEE?**
Twenty to thirty minutes of focused reading a day, five or six days a week, is a realistic habit for a school-going child. It works best as a fixed slot — after homework or before bed — rather than as extra work piled on top of an already long day.

**Should parents buy lots of books before admission?**
No. Start with what you already have at home: the child's school textbooks, a children's newspaper or magazine, and one or two good general books. A reading habit is built by daily practice, not by a shelf full of unread books.

**Do cadets get free time to read at Sainik School?**
Yes, within the structured day. Between classes, prep periods, library periods and the evening routine there are reading windows, and senior cadets often read newspapers and magazines to prepare for debates, quizzes and current-affairs discussions.
