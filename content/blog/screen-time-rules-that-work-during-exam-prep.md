## The real problem: why bans fail

Here's the thing about screen time during exam preparation. Every parent has tried the full ban at least once — phone confiscated, Wi-Fi password changed. It works for about six days.

Then one of three things happens. The child finds a workaround (a friend's old phone, the tablet nobody remembered). Or the household turns into a war zone and nobody studies anyway. Or — the worst one — the child starts sneaking, and now you've added lying to the list of problems. The distraction didn't leave. It just went underground.

So this article isn't about banning screens. It's about a household system where the phone stops being the default thing your child reaches for — without turning your home into a prison during an already stressful AISSEE season. The exam is on **31 January 2027** (CONFIRMED tentative) — you need rules that survive four months, not four days.

## The 7 rules that actually hold up

**1. The drawer rule: the phone lives in one place, always.**

Buy a drawer. Or designate one — the living-room drawer, a box on the dining table, anything visible and neutral. During every study block, the phone goes in the drawer. Not facedown next to the notebook, not in the child's pocket "just for the timer". In the drawer, in another room, full stop.

The magic of this rule is that it's a place, not a person: the phone *lives* in the drawer during study hours, so nobody ever has to be the bad guy.

**2. No screens in the first hour after school and the last hour before bed.**

The hour after school is when the "just five minutes of YouTube" that becomes two hours begins. The hour before bed matters even more: every paediatric guideline agrees on this one. A child scrolling shorts at 10:30 PM sleeps badly, wakes up groggy, and studies badly the next day. It's a cycle you can simply refuse to start.

**3. One fixed entertainment slot per day. Non-negotiable, both ways.**

This is the rule parents usually skip — and it's the one that makes everything else work. Give the child a genuine, guilt-free entertainment slot — one hour in the evening. No commentary, no hovering.

Two conditions: same time every day, and it ends when it ends. Fixed timing removes the negotiation. And *you don't get to cancel it* — the slot is not a reward you take away for a bad mock test. The moment it becomes a bargaining chip, the whole system becomes a bribe economy.

On weekends, make it two slots or one longer one — fighting a weekday system on a Sunday is how rules die.

**4. The study room is a no-phone zone, even for "study apps".**

"But I need it for the timer / the calculator / the doubt-solving app." This is where good rules go to die. Get a ₹200 wall clock and a Casio calculator. Doubts go into a notebook and get resolved in the evening slot — good practice for AISSEE's pen-and-paper OMR format anyway. The exam hall will have no phone; practising with one in the room trains the wrong reflexes.

If a video wanders into unrelated recommendations within two minutes — and it always does — it wasn't study. Real AISSEE prep is in books, notes, and timed mock papers. [Our homework-balance guide](/blog/balancing-homework-and-aissee-prep/) covers how to structure study hours so the phone isn't missed in the first place.

**5. Weekday rules and weekend rules are written down separately.**

Most household systems collapse on Saturdays because the rules were written for school days and nobody specified what changes. Write two columns — weekday and weekend — and stick them on the fridge. The table below is ready to print.

**6. When the child sneaks the phone, fix the cause before the punishment.**

You will catch them — probably at night under the blanket. Your reaction decides whether this becomes a lesson or a lifestyle.

First, the logistics: the phone charges in *your* room overnight, every night. Scrolling memes at midnight is usually boredom, anxiety about the exam they can't voice, or fear of missing out on friends' chats.

Punishment teaches a child to hide better. A conversation about *why* teaches them to talk to you when exam pressure builds. If anxiety is the cause, read our piece on [motivating without pressure](/blog/motivate-child-aissee-without-pressure/).

**7. Parents follow the same rules. Visibly.**

During study hours, your phone goes in the drawer too. At dinner, face-down for everyone. In the last hour before bed, read a book where your child can see you. When the rule applies to the whole family, it stops being punishment and starts being culture.

## Screen time during exam preparation: the weekday vs weekend schedule

A schedule you can adapt — shift the times, but keep the structure.

| Time block | Weekday rule | Weekend rule |
|---|---|---|
| After school, first hour | No screens — snack | No screens — play, outdoor time |
| Study blocks (evening) | Phone in the drawer, no exceptions | Same as weekday |
| Entertainment slot | 1 fixed hour, same time daily | 2 slots or one 90-minute slot |
| Dinner | No phones at the table, whole family | Same as weekday |
| Last hour before bed | No screens for anyone | No screens for anyone |
| Overnight | Phone charges in parents' room | Phone charges in parents' room |
| Morning before school | No screens before school | No screens before the room is tidied |

Print it and stick it on the fridge. When your child asks "why can't I—" you point at the paper. It's the bad guy, not you.

One last honest note: the problem was never the screen. It was the *unbounded* screen — no start time, no end time. A child who finishes their entertainment hour and goes back to revision willingly beats a child who was never allowed a phone but thinks about one all day. ## FAQs

**Should I take away my child's phone completely during AISSEE preparation?**

A total ban almost never survives the first week, and the secrecy it creates is worse than the distraction. A controlled system — fixed entertainment slot, phone parked in a drawer during study hours, no screens in the last hour before bed — works better and lasts longer.

**My child sneaks the phone at night. What should I do?**

Make night-time sneaking physically impossible — the phone charges in the parents' room overnight. Then ask why they are sneaking it: boredom, anxiety, or FOMO among friends. Fix the cause separately; punishment alone teaches better hiding, not better habits.

**Are YouTube study channels helpful or distracting for AISSEE prep?**

Mostly distracting. Genuine study material for AISSEE is in books, notes, and mock papers — a video that wanders off-topic within two minutes is entertainment, not revision.

**Should parents also follow screen-time rules during exam prep?**

Yes — this is the rule that makes all the others stick. A child cannot respect a no-phone-during-dinner rule that a parent breaks every night. When the phone lives in the drawer for everyone during study hours, the rule stops feeling like punishment and starts feeling like the family's normal.
