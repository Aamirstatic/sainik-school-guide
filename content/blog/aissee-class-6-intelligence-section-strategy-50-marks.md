## The section, in numbers

Parents obsess over Maths, and they are right to — it is half the paper. But look at the other three sections again. They are not equal in difficulty, even though they are equal in marks.

| Section | Questions | Marks each | Total marks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mathematics | 50 | 3 | 150 |
| Language | 25 | 2 | 50 |
| **Intelligence** | **25** | **2** | **50** |
| General Knowledge | 25 | 2 | 50 |
| **Total** | **125** | — | **300** |

Here is the thing about Intelligence: it is the only section where your child does not need to *know* anything new. There is no syllabus to memorise, no formula to recall, no fact from a textbook. Every question gives you everything you need — the pattern is sitting right there in the question, waiting to be spotted. That is why it is the quickest section to improve with practice, and why it is the section most children leave marks in through sheer neglect. They spend months on Maths and GK and treat Intelligence as something you are either "good at" or not. That is wrong. Pattern-spotting is a skill, and skills respond to practice faster than knowledge does.

The rest of the pattern is straightforward. The exam is on **Sunday, 31 January 2027, 2:00 PM to 4:30 PM** — 150 minutes, pen-and-paper, OMR sheet, multiple-choice questions, **no negative marking**. And there is a floor: under the 2026 pattern, expected to repeat, a candidate needs at least **25% in each section** — that is 12.5 of the 50 Intelligence marks — and **40% in aggregate**, 120 of 300. The floor does not apply to SC/ST candidates. Notice what that floor means: a child cannot "skip" Intelligence and pass on Maths alone. The paper will not let them.

## What the 25 questions actually test

The official syllabus lists the Intelligence topics. They fall into six families, and once your child can name the family a question belongs to, half the work is done — because each family has only one or two tricks.

| Family | What it covers | How to practise it |
|---|---|---|
| **Series** | Number series, letter series, missing number or term | Look for the difference first, then the pattern in the differences |
| **Coding–decoding** | Alphabet coding, number coding, letter coding | Write A=1 to Z=26 on rough paper before starting |
| **Analogy** | Word analogy, number analogy, alphabet analogy | Say the relationship out loud before looking at the options |
| **Classification** | Odd one out — numbers, words, pictures | Test each option against one rule; the odd one breaks it |
| **Spatial & visual** | Mirror images, water images, counting figures, non-verbal reasoning | Hold the question at eye level; trace the figure with a finger |
| **Everyday logic** | Direction sense, blood relations, Venn diagrams, clock, calendar, ranking, arranging words, mathematical operations | Draw it — a small diagram beats thinking it in your head |

A word of honesty here. The bulletin does not publish how many questions come from each family, and anyone who tells you "six questions always come from coding" is guessing. The mix changes every year. What does not change is the *type* of thinking each family needs — and that is what the practice below builds.

## The 25-minute split: how to spend the exam

Intelligence questions are short, so the temptation is to rush them. Don't. A rushed reasoning question is a wrong reasoning question. Here is the split:

| Block | Minutes | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| First pass | 18 | Attempt every question you can solve in under a minute. Circle the rest and move on. |
| Second pass | 5 | Return to the circled questions with fresh eyes — the pattern often appears on the second look. |
| OMR + check | 2 | Bubble check: every answer on the sheet, no skipped rows, no double bubbles. |

That is 25 minutes for Intelligence, leaving 60 for Maths, 25 for Language, 20 for GK, and 15 for a final review. One timing warning: Intelligence is the section where children most often *lose* time without noticing, because a single stubborn series question can eat four minutes while feeling like one. The circle-and-move-on rule matters here more than anywhere else. If the pattern has not appeared in 45 seconds, circle it and come back — the second look works far more often than the fourth minute does.

And remember the one rule that makes this section free marks: there is no negative marking, so in the last minute of the Intelligence block, your child should answer every remaining question — even by guessing. A guess costs nothing. A blank scores nothing.

## Three worked examples — try these tonight

Sit with your child and try these. They are the exact style the section uses: simple ideas, careful reading. Cover the answer, let them work, then talk through it.

**Example 1 — number series.** *3, 6, 11, 18, 27, ?*
The differences are 3, 5, 7, 9 — odd numbers going up by 2. The next difference is 11, so the answer is 27 + 11 = **38**. The trap is looking for a multiplication pattern when the answer is in the differences. Teach your child: *differences first, always.*

**Example 2 — letter coding.** *If CAT is written as 3120, how is DOG written?*
Write A=1 to Z=26. C=3, A=1, T=20, so CAT becomes 3-1-20. The same rule gives D=4, O=15, G=7, so DOG is **4157**. The trap is overthinking — children hunt for a clever shift when the code is just the alphabet positions, side by side. Write the alphabet with numbers on the rough sheet before the section starts, and coding questions become free marks.

**Example 3 — odd one out.** *121, 144, 169, 190 — which is different?*
121 is 11 × 11, 144 is 12 × 12, 169 is 13 × 13 — all perfect squares. 190 is not a square at all. The answer is **190**. The trap is checking each number alone instead of asking *what do three of these share?* That one question — "what do three of them share?" — is the whole trick of the odd-one-out family.

## Five habits that build reasoning speed

1. **Fifteen minutes a day, every day.** One page of mixed reasoning questions — series, coding, odd one out — done daily beats a two-hour weekend session. Reasoning is a muscle; it responds to frequency, not volume.
2. **The alphabet strip.** Stick A=1 to Z=26 on the study wall and on the inside of the rough-work habit: your child writes it out at the start of every practice set, until it is automatic. Half of all coding questions are lost to slow letter-counting, not to wrong logic.
3. **Say the rule before choosing.** For every analogy and series question, your child says the rule out loud — "add 3, add 5, add 7" — *before* looking at the options. Options are designed to distract; a rule stated first cannot be shaken by a tempting wrong answer.
4. **The error notebook, reasoning edition.** Every wrong reasoning question goes in the same notebook as the Maths errors, with the *rule they missed* written beside it — not just the right answer. "Missed that 190 is not a square" is a lesson; "answer was 190" is nothing.
5. **One timed 25-question set every week.** Twenty-five minutes, on the clock, on an OMR sheet if you have one. The point is not the score; it is learning that 25 questions in 25 minutes means one minute per question — and that circling and moving on is a skill, not a failure.

## Three mistakes that cost these 50 marks

- **Treating Intelligence as a "talent" section and never practising it.** The children who score 45+ in this section are not born pattern-spotters. They have simply seen the question types before. Familiarity is the whole advantage, and it is free.
- **Wrestling with one question for four minutes.** The paper rewards the child who collects the easy 2-mark questions first. A circled question is not a lost question — it is a question you will meet again in five minutes with fresher eyes.
- **Leaving questions blank.** No negative marking means a blank is the only guaranteed zero. Guess. Every time. Our [OMR mistakes guide](/blog/silly-omr-mistakes-cost-sainik-school-seat/) covers the bubbling side of this — one skipped row can shift every answer after it.

## A weekly rhythm, not a dated plan

Our [11-week plan](/blog/aissee-2027-last-11-weeks-week-by-week-plan-2026-11-16/) gives the dated schedule; for Intelligence, the rhythm inside each week is what matters: fifteen minutes of mixed reasoning every day, one timed 25-question set on Sunday, and one error-notebook review on Saturday evening. That is roughly two and a half hours a week for 50 marks — and, unlike GK, every hour of it shows up directly in the score. In the final three days before the exam, drop the timed sets and keep only the error notebook and the alphabet strip. Confidence matters more than new practice at that point.

## FAQs

**How many marks is Intelligence in AISSEE Class 6?**

Intelligence is 25 questions for 50 marks — each question carries 2 marks. It is one of three 50-mark sections, alongside Language and General Knowledge; Maths is 50 questions for 150 marks.

**How much time should my child spend on Intelligence in the AISSEE Class 6 exam?**

About 25 minutes of the 150-minute paper: roughly 18 minutes on a first pass, 5 minutes returning to circled questions, and 2 minutes for the OMR check. That leaves 60 minutes for Maths, 25 for Language, 20 for GK and a final review.

**What topics are in the AISSEE Class 6 Intelligence syllabus?**

The official syllabus lists coding-decoding, word/number/alphabet analogy, number and letter series, classification (odd one out), direction sense, blood relations, Venn diagrams, clock and calendar, mirror and water images, and non-verbal reasoning.

**What is the minimum score needed in Intelligence to qualify?**

Under the 2026 bulletin pattern, expected to repeat in 2027, a candidate needs at least 25 percent in each section — 12.5 of the 50 Intelligence marks — and 40 percent (120 of 300) in aggregate. The rule does not apply to SC/ST candidates.

**Is there negative marking in the Intelligence section?**

No — wrong answers and blanks both score zero; nothing is deducted. Your child should attempt every Intelligence question, even the ones they are unsure about, because a guess costs nothing and a blank scores nothing.

**Can my child improve in Intelligence quickly?**

Yes — it is the quickest section to improve with practice, because the question types repeat. Fifteen minutes a day on series, coding and odd-one-out questions for three weeks is usually enough to lift a child from guessing to solving most of them confidently.
