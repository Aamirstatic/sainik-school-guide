Here is a number that should change how you study: the AISSEE Class 9 English section is worth **50 marks across 25 questions**, sitting inside a 150-question, 400-mark paper you attempt in **180 minutes**. Mathematics alone carries 200 of those marks, so most Class 9 aspirants pour their hours into maths — and then bleed easy marks in English, the one section where technique beats talent.

Reading comprehension is where those marks leak. Passages look friendly, so students read casually, pick the answer that "feels right," and move on. The fix is not more vocabulary lists. It is a repeatable method for attacking any passage under time pressure.

## Why comprehension is the highest-leverage English skill

Grammar and vocabulary questions reward what you already know. Comprehension rewards what you *do* in the exam hall — and a method can be learned in weeks. Every passage is a closed system: the answers are in front of you. Students who lose marks here usually commit one of three sins:

1. **Reading without a mission** — they read the passage like a story, then start the questions, then re-read the whole thing.
2. **Answering from memory** — they trust what they remember instead of verifying against the text. Memory lies; the passage doesn't.
3. **Falling for the "almost right" option** — the wrong options are designed to contain passage words. Familiar words feel true.

## The 4-pass method

This is the method. Practise it on every passage until it is automatic:

| Pass | What you do | Time target |
|---|---|---|
| **Pass 1 — Hunt** | Read the *questions* first, not the passage. Underline the exact thing each question asks. | 60–90 seconds |
| **Pass 2 — Sweep** | Read the passage once, at speed, marking (mentally or with a pencil tick) the zones where each question's answer lives. Do not slow down for hard words. | 2–3 minutes |
| **Pass 3 — Strike** | Answer each question by going back to its marked zone and finding the *sentence* that decides it. If you cannot point to the sentence, you are guessing. | 1–2 minutes |
| **Pass 4 — Verify** | Re-read only the questions you flagged as uncertain. Everything else stays as marked. Never change an answer without textual evidence. | 30 seconds |

Two rules make this work: **never answer from memory** (Pass 3 forces you back to the text), and **never re-read the entire passage** (Pass 4 is surgical, not a second reading).

## The five traps setters use

- **The word-match trap.** The option repeats passage words in a different order, changing the meaning. Check the sentence, not the vocabulary.
- **The extreme trap.** Options with words like "always," "never," "all," "none" are wrong more often than not. Passages rarely deal in absolutes.
- **The beyond-the-passage trap.** An option that is true in real life but not stated or implied in the passage. If it is not in the text, it is not the answer.
- **The half-true trap.** An option that is correct for the first half of its claim and wrong for the second. Read the whole option.
- **The tone question.** "What is the author's attitude?" questions are decided by adjectives and adverbs in the passage — circle them during the Sweep pass.

## The weekly drill (8 weeks to exam-level)

You do not need more material; you need a rhythm. With roughly 14 weeks to 31 January 2027, run this cycle:

- **Daily (20 minutes):** one unseen passage with a timer, using the 4-pass method. Mark your time and score in a notebook.
- **Twice a week:** 10 minutes of "zone marking" practice — take an old passage, read the questions, and just mark where each answer lives without answering. This trains Pass 2 in isolation.
- **Weekly:** one review session. Revisit every question you got wrong that week and write, in one line, which trap caught you. If the same trap appears twice, that is your study topic for the next week.
- **Every second week:** one timed English section simulation — all 25 questions, 25 minutes, OMR-style bubbling. The section never feels long until you do it against a clock.

Pair this with steady vocabulary work — unknown words slow every pass down. ## A note on the rest of the paper

English is 50 of 400 marks. Do not let a perfect passage method tempt you into over-investing: a practical exam-hall budget is roughly **20–25 minutes for English** out of the 180, since every minute past that is stolen from Mathematics, which is worth four times as much. Comprehension should feel *efficient*, not comfortable — if you finish a passage with time to spare, you read well; if you finish the section with time to spare, you read well *and* managed well.

And remember the one rule that changes everything about guessing: there is **no negative marking** in AISSEE. An attempted comprehension question can only score zero or more. Never leave one blank because you ran out of certainty.

## FAQs

**How many marks is the AISSEE Class 9 English section worth?**
50 marks across 25 questions, inside a 150-question, 400-mark paper attempted in 180 minutes. Mathematics carries the other 200 marks; Intelligence, Science and Social Science carry 50 each.

**What does the AISSEE Class 9 English section usually test?**
Reading comprehension passages, grammar, and vocabulary. Comprehension is the highest-leverage area because it rewards a repeatable reading method rather than memorisation.

**How much time should I give English in the AISSEE Class 9 paper?**
There is no official per-section time split. A practical budget is roughly 20 to 25 minutes of the 180, since English carries 50 of the 400 marks. Practise finishing passages inside that window.

**Is there negative marking in AISSEE 2027?**
No. Wrong answers and blank answers both score zero, so attempting every comprehension question is always the right call.

**How do I improve reading speed for AISSEE comprehension?**
Read one unfamiliar passage daily with a timer, force yourself to answer before re-reading, and track your words-per-minute and accuracy weekly. Speed without accuracy is worthless.

**Should I read the questions before the passage?**
Yes. Reading the questions first gives your brain targets to hunt for during the first read, which cuts re-reading time dramatically. This is the core of the 4-pass method explained in this article.
