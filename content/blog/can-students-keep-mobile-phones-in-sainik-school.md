> **Last verified: 6 October 2026.** This article is based on official joining instructions and parent advisories published by Sainik Schools themselves, linked under Sources.

If you are preparing for AISSEE 2027, this is probably the question your child is too nervous to ask and you are too practical not to: *can they keep their phone?*

The short answer is no. The longer answer — what happens instead, how families actually stay in touch, and how to prepare your child — matters more than the ban itself. Because the phone question is rarely about the phone. It is about whether your 11-year-old will feel cut off from home. And that worry deserves an honest answer.

## What the schools actually say

This is not a rumour or a "they say" — the prohibition is written into official school documents:

- **Sainik School Chandrapur** (official advisory to parents): "Use of mobile phones, electrical appliances and other electronic gadgets are STRICTLY PROHIBITED inside the school campus. In case any cadet is found in possession of such items, appropriate penal action would be taken as per school orders."
- **Sainik School Kapurthala** (joining instructions): "The cadets are not allowed to carry mobile phones anywhere in the campus."
- **Sainik School Korukonda** (joining instructions): parents are told not to provide mobile phones to their wards; confiscated items "will be confiscated (never to be returned)."
- One school's hostel rules go further: cadets "not permitted to keep mobile phones or any other electronic items," with strict disciplinary action "including withdrawal from the school" for defaulters.

Notice the escalation: prohibited, confiscated, never returned, possibly withdrawn. Schools make the consequence deliberately severe because the temptation is constant. A phone sneaked in during the first term usually surfaces within weeks — dormitories are shared, routines are regimented, and there is nowhere private to hide one.

## Why the ban exists (and why it won't soften)

Parents sometimes hope the rule is a formality that relaxes in practice. It doesn't, and the reasoning is consistent across schools:

1. **Focus.** The entire Sainik School model — prep, games, roll call, lights out — depends on cadets being present where they are. A phone in the dormitory quietly defeats every study hour.
2. **Equity.** Not every family can afford a smartphone. In a shared dormitory, one glowing screen creates a visible divide the school would rather never allow.
3. **Safety and discipline.** Unsupervised internet access, late-night messaging, and social media drama are problems day schools battle daily; boarding schools simply remove the vector.
4. **The homesickness argument backfires.** Schools find that constant contact *prolongs* settling-in distress rather than easing it. A child who calls home crying every evening takes months to adjust; one who writes a letter on Sunday is usually playing football with dorm mates by week three. This matches what we found when we looked at [how homesickness actually plays out](/blog/homesickness-in-sainik-school-how-parents-can-help/) — the adjustment is real, and it passes.

## How families actually stay in touch

No phone does not mean no contact. Here is what the official documents and standard practice indicate:

| What parents imagine | What actually happens |
|---|---|
| Daily WhatsApp calls with their child | Scheduled contact organised by the school — call windows, letter writing, and PTM days |
| Child calls whenever upset | Child speaks to the house master or housemistress, who handles welfare day to day |
| Parents message the child directly | Schools require parents to keep their own mobile numbers updated with the administration, so the *school* can reach *you* when needed |
| Emergencies go unanswered | Schools maintain their own communication lines for genuine emergencies; the chain runs through the school office |

The exact mechanics — which day calls happen, how often letters go home, whether there is a supervised phone booth — **vary by school**. Some details that circulate on parent forums ("Sunday calls at 4 pm") are school-specific and change year to year. Treat any such claim as unverified unless it is in your child's school's current prospectus or joining instructions. What *is* verified: the channel always runs through the school, never directly to the child.

Parents can also [visit on scheduled visiting days](/blog/how-often-can-parents-visit-sainik-school/) — which, combined with letters and organised calls, is how generations of cadets stayed connected long before smartphones existed.

## The conversation to have before admission

Do not let the phone ban be a surprise discovered at the school gate. If your child is preparing for AISSEE 2027 — now [117 days from the expected exam date](/news/aissee-2027-117-days-to-exam-what-to-finish-2026-10-06/) — have this talk *now*, while it's hypothetical:

- **Frame it as the point, not the punishment.** "The school takes your phone because your time there is fully scheduled — you won't have hours to scroll anyway." True: between drill, classes, games, prep and roll call, free screen time barely exists.
- **Agree on a letter habit.** Promise to reply to every letter. A child who knows a reply is coming writes more — and writing is where the settling-in happens.
- **Practice the discomfort early.** If your child has never spent a night without the phone beside the bed, start now. Small steps: phone-free study hours, phone-free Sundays.
- **Don't smuggle.** It sounds obvious, but the most common confiscation story starts with a parent who "just wanted them to have it for the journey." If the school finds out on day one — and it usually does — your child's first school memory is a disciplinary entry.

## A note on new Sainik Schools

The prohibition documents above come from the established 33 Sainik Schools. The newer PPP-mode Sainik Schools run their own administrations, but their joining instructions follow the same Society framework — do not assume a newer school is more relaxed about gadgets. Check the specific school's prospectus; assume the ban applies until you see otherwise in writing.

The phone rule feels harsh from the outside. From the inside — ask any old boy — it is one of the things they end up grateful for. Six years of undivided attention is exactly what the school is selling, and the no-phone rule is how it delivers.

## FAQs

**Are mobile phones allowed in Sainik School hostels?**

No. Official school documents state the ban explicitly — Chandrapur calls it "strictly prohibited" inside the campus, Kapurthala bars cadets from carrying phones anywhere on campus. Expect the same rule at every Sainik School.

**What happens if a cadet is caught with a mobile phone?**

The phone is confiscated and disciplinary action follows. Korukonda warns confiscated items are never returned; other schools' rules mention withdrawal as a possible consequence. The severity is deliberate.

**How do parents contact their child in a Sainik School?**

Through the school, not directly. Schools require parents to keep their mobile numbers updated with the administration, and contact happens through scheduled windows, letters, and PTM days. Exact procedures vary by school — check the joining instructions.

**Can my child take a phone on the journey to school at admission time?**

Contact the school office first rather than assuming. The ban applies inside the campus, and schools explicitly advise parents against providing phones to their wards. Gate-handover is the most common first-week confiscation story.

**Do senior students get phone privileges?**

No documented exemption exists for senior cadets in the official rules we reviewed — the prohibition is campus-wide. Do not assume relaxations; check the specific school's current prospectus.
