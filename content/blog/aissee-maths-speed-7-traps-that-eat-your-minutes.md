## The real maths problem is not maths

Ask a parent what worries them about AISSEE maths and they will say fractions, or percentages, or "my child is slow". Ask the child after a mock test and you get the truer answer: "I knew how to do it, but I ran out of time."

That is the whole article in one line. Most children losing maths marks in AISSEE are not losing them to sums they cannot do. They are losing them to minutes that leak away in seven predictable places — and with 73 days left until 31 January 2027, there is still time to plug every one of them. Speed in this exam is not a talent. It is a set of habits, and habits can be drilled.

Here is the budget first, because everything else hangs off it.

## The time budget: what 72 seconds a question actually means

| | Class 6 | Class 9 |
|---|---|---|
| Total time | 150 minutes | 180 minutes |
| Total questions | 125 | 150 |
| Maths questions / marks | 50 questions / 150 marks | 50 questions / 200 marks |
| Maths share of the paper | Half the marks (150 of 300) | Half the marks (200 of 400) |
| Flat average per question | 72 seconds | 72 seconds |
| Fair maths budget | ~75 minutes (90 seconds per maths question) | ~75 minutes (90 seconds per maths question) |

Read that last row again. Ninety seconds per maths question is not a target for geniuses — it is what the paper allows if your child spends time in proportion to marks. And here is the sting: the qualifying rule needs 25% in *each* section and 40% overall. A child who aces language and GK but crawls through maths can still fall short on the aggregate, because maths is half the paper. Speed in maths is not a nice-to-have. It is where the result is decided.

So where do the minutes go? Here are the seven traps, in the order they usually strike.

## The 7 traps

**1. The long calculation.** Your child multiplies 47 × 36 by hand, digit by digit, on the question paper — when the options are 1,692, 1,682, 1,702 and 1,692 is the only one ending in 2 that fits a quick units-digit check. The fix is not "calculate faster". It is: estimate first, eliminate two options, and only then calculate. A 10-second elimination beats a 60-second multiplication.

**2. The second reading.** The child reads the word problem, does not understand it, reads it again from the top, and then reads it a third time while solving. The fix: on the first read, underline the numbers and the question being asked — what is actually wanted, in one line — before touching a pencil. Reading with a pen is one read. Reading without one is three.

**3. The stuck question.** Question 12 is hard. Your child sits on it for six minutes, determined not to be beaten by it, while questions 13 to 20 — four of which they could have solved in two minutes each — wait untouched. This is the most expensive trap in the paper. The fix is the **2-minute rule**: if a question takes more than two minutes, circle its number on the question paper, move on, and come back in a second pass. There is no negative marking, so a skipped question costs nothing until the end — but a question never reached because of a stuck one costs everything.

**4. The instant re-check.** Your child solves a question, then immediately solves it again to check, then checks the bubbling, then moves on. That doubles the time of every question. The fix: trust the first pass. Checking belongs at the end, in the time saved by the 2-minute rule — not in the middle of every question.

**5. The one-by-one bubbling.** Answer, bubble, answer, bubble — the hand keeps travelling between the question paper and the OMR sheet, and each trip breaks concentration. The fix: solve five questions, then bubble all five together. It is faster, and it is safer — one common OMR error is bubbling the right answer against the wrong number, and batch bubbling with a quick number check catches it.

**6. The scattered rough work.** Rough work spread across margins, the back of the admit card, and three different corners of the question paper — so when your child needs a number from two minutes ago, it takes thirty seconds to find it. The fix: one rough-work column, top to bottom, question numbers written beside each block. Boring. And worth five minutes a paper.

**7. The printed order.** The paper prints questions in order; your child does them in order, including the hard ones first. The fix: first pass, easy questions only — the ones solvable in under a minute. Second pass, the medium ones. Third pass, the circled hard ones. The paper does not reward doing questions in order. It rewards doing the easy marks first.

## The 4-week speed audit

You cannot fix what you cannot see. For the next four Sundays, run this routine — it is the whole plan:

| Week | Sunday timed paper | Monday error log (30 minutes) |
|---|---|---|
| Week 1 (from 22 Nov) | One full 150/180-minute paper, 2:00 PM slot, phone in another room | List every question that took over 2 minutes; name its trap (1–7) |
| Week 2 | Same, with the 2-minute rule enforced | Count the minutes each trap cost; the biggest number is the week's drill |
| Week 3 | Same, with batch bubbling (5 at a time) | Re-time the five slowest questions from week 1 — they should be faster now |
| Week 4 | Same, full exam-day routine: admit-card check, 1:30 PM "last entry" discipline | If maths is still over 75 minutes, cut one more trap, not more syllabus |

One honest warning: do not measure speed on untimed practice. A child who does sums without a clock is practising accuracy, not speed, and the exam tests both. The Sunday paper is the only practice that counts for speed — and the Monday log is where the speed actually improves.

## FAQs

**How much time does each AISSEE maths question get?**

On a flat average, 72 seconds per question — 150 minutes for 125 questions in Class 6, and 180 minutes for 150 questions in Class 9. Maths carries half the marks in Class 6 (150 of 300) and half in Class 9 (200 of 400), so a fair budget is about 75 minutes for the 50 maths questions, or 90 seconds each. That is arithmetic from the official pattern, not a rule from NTA — your child can spend the time differently, but they cannot spend more time than the paper allows.

**What is the 2-minute rule for AISSEE maths?**

If a question takes more than two minutes, circle it on the question paper, move on, and come back to it in a second pass after finishing the rest. Because there is no negative marking, every unattempted question is a certain zero — the 2-minute rule protects the easy marks from being eaten by one hard question.

**Is there negative marking in AISSEE 2027?**

No. Wrong answers and blank answers both score zero, and nothing is deducted. That is confirmed from the NTA Information Bulletin, and it is why the attempt strategy is simple: attempt every question, and never leave a question blank because you ran out of time on an earlier one.

**What are the biggest speed traps in AISSEE maths?**

Doing long calculations by hand instead of estimating, reading a word problem twice before starting, getting stuck on one hard question, re-checking every answer immediately, bubbling answers one at a time, scattering rough work across the paper, and doing questions in printed order instead of easy ones first. Each one costs minutes; together they can cost a whole section.

**How should my child practise maths speed in the last 73 days?**

One full timed paper every Sunday, in the 2:00 PM slot, with a phone in another room — then a 30-minute error log on Monday where every slow question is timed again. Speed comes from timed repetition with review, not from doing more sums untimed. Our 12-week drill plan has the weekly structure if you want it laid out day by day.

**Does maths decide the AISSEE result?**

Almost. Maths is half the marks in both classes, and the qualifying rule needs 25 percent in each section plus 40 percent overall. A child who is strong everywhere except maths can still miss the aggregate — which is why maths speed, not just maths knowledge, is worth fixing now.
