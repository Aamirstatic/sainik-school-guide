# How to Build Vocabulary for AISSEE English in 90 Days: A Daily Plan That Actually Works

> **Last verified: 29 September 2026.** Exam pattern facts marked CONFIRMED come from the AISSEE 2026 cycle; anything marked EXPECTED follows that pattern and awaits the AISSEE 2027 information bulletin (not released yet).

Every year, a parent tells me some version of the same thing: "My child's grammar is fine, but the English section still eats his marks." Nine times out of ten, the real problem isn't grammar — it's vocabulary. The child understands the passage *almost*, misses the question anyway, and comes home saying English is "too hard." Vocabulary responds beautifully to a daily habit.

Here is the honest calendar: the AISSEE 2027 exam is on **31 January 2027** (CONFIRMED tentative, NTA exam calendar 2026-27). Starting today — 29 September 2026 — you have a 90-day window ending around end-December, with January kept clean for final revision. Ninety days of small daily work, no cramming, no expensive word-list books. Twenty minutes a day. That's the whole pitch.

## Know exactly what vocabulary is worth in your child's paper

Class 6 and Class 9 are genuinely different here.

**Class 6 (CONFIRMED, 2026 pattern):** 150 minutes, 125 questions, 300 marks. The language section is **25 questions for 50 marks** — and your child chooses **English OR Hindi** as the medium for the paper. Writing in English? Then word meanings, synonyms, antonyms, spelling and idioms can be a third of that section.

**Class 9 (CONFIRMED, 2026 pattern):** 180 minutes, 150 questions, 400 marks. English is a **compulsory section — 25 questions for 50 marks** — alongside Maths (50Q/200), Intelligence, Science and SST (25Q/50 each). No medium choice; every aspirant faces it, and the vocabulary load is heavier: passages, contextual meanings, error detection.

Both papers are MCQ on OMR with **no negative marking** (CONFIRMED) — so a known word is a free 2-mark answer. For a Class 9 grammar refresher alongside this plan, see our [AISSEE English grammar topics that matter most](/blog/aissee-english-grammar-topics-that-matter-most/).

## The 90-day phase plan

One mistake families make: treating vocabulary as something you "start properly" in December. Break it into three 30-day phases instead:

| Phase | Days | Mission |
|---|---|---|
| **1: Foundations** | Day 1–30 (29 Sept – 28 Oct) | 8 new words daily in real sentences; master 10 common root words |
| **2: Expansion** | Day 31–60 (29 Oct – 27 Nov) | 10–12 words daily; synonym-antonym pairs; one short passage weekly |
| **3: Exam hardening** | Day 61–90 (28 Nov – 27 Dec) | Revise only — no new lists; timed 25-question English drills; idioms and one-word substitutions |

Phase 3 contains no new word lists — it's pure retrieval: testing, not studying.

## The daily 20-minute routine (the heart of this plan)

Here is the exact routine. Twenty minutes, same time every day — morning works best:

1. **Minutes 0–5: New words.** Learn today's words (8 in Phase 1, 10–12 in Phase 2) — never alone, always in a sentence your child writes themselves. "Abundant" means nothing on its own; "The school library has an abundant collection of storybooks" is unforgettable.
2. **Minutes 5–12: Yesterday's words.** Recall yesterday's batch from memory before checking. This is retrieval practice — the single most powerful memory technique, and almost nobody's child does it.
3. **Minutes 12–17: Root-word connection.** Take one root and generate the family: *port* (to carry) → transport, export, portable, import.
4. **Minutes 17–20: Word journal.** Every word goes in with three things: the word, one sentence, one synonym or antonym. This journal becomes December's revision book.

Missed a day? Just continue the next day. Six days a week of 20 minutes beats two-hour weekend crams nobody sustains.

## Your root-words starter list

Root words are the cheat code nobody teaches early enough. One Latin or Greek root unlocks whole families of words. Start with these ten:

| Root | Meaning | Example words |
|---|---|---|
| bene | good | benevolent, benefit, benign |
| mal | bad | malice, malfunction, maltreat |
| port | carry | transport, export, portable |
| vis | see | visible, vision, supervise |
| cred | believe | credible, credit, incredible |
| bio | life | biography, biology, autobiography |
| geo | earth | geography, geology, geometry |
| chron | time | chronological, chronic, chronicle |
| tele | far | television, telephone, telegram |
| therm | heat | thermometer, thermal, thermostat |

Spend the whole of Phase 1 on these roots alongside your daily words. Teach your child to *attack* an unknown word in a question: strip the prefix, find the root, guess the meaning. On a no-negative-marking paper, an educated guess from a root is always worth taking. Pair this with our [NCERT vs guidebooks comparison](/blog/ncert-vs-guidebooks-aissee-what-works/) when choosing English reading material.

**Tailoring tip:** Class 6 — if your child's Hindi is stronger, pick Hindi as the medium and let English be a bonus, not a stress point. Class 9 — English is compulsory, so from Phase 2 add idioms, phrasal verbs and one newspaper article daily.

## Words in context: the method that actually works

Here is the difference between a child who *knows* words and one who *remembers* them in January: context. The AISSEE rarely asks "what does X mean?" in isolation. Practise the way the paper tests:

- **Sentence method:** every new word gets used in a sentence about the child's own life. "The football match was *exhilarating*" beats any dictionary example.
- **Passage method:** from Phase 2, read one short passage a week (a newspaper editorial for Class 9, a story excerpt for Class 6) and circle every unknown word. Five words from one interesting passage stick better than twenty from a list.
- **Opposite-pair method:** learn antonyms together — *ancient/modern, brave/cowardly, scarce/abundant*. Antonym questions become free marks.
- **One-word substitution:** keep a small separate list (*bibliophile*, *ambidextrous*). These show up in Class 9 papers regularly.

## Mistakes parents make (and how to dodge them)

I've watched hundreds of families do vocabulary prep. These are the five failure modes:

1. **The 50-words-a-day sprint.** Learnt on Monday, gone by Friday. Stick to 8–12 and revise on a cycle.
2. **Word lists without sentences.** A word without a sentence is a phone number without a name — unmemorable.
3. **Skipping revision.** If a word isn't revisited on days 2, 7, and 21, it evaporates. Build revision into the routine, not "when there's time."
4. **Buying three vocabulary books.** One thin notebook your child writes in beats three printed books they don't. See our [daily timetable for Class 6](/blog/aissee-daily-timetable-class-6/) for how the 20 minutes fits into a real school day.
5. **Starting in January.** By January, vocabulary time is over — that's revision season. Starting 29 September is the whole advantage of this plan.

## FAQs

**How many English questions are there in the AISSEE Class 6 paper?**
The Class 6 paper has a language section of 25 questions for 50 marks. Your child chooses English or Hindi as the language medium for the whole paper, so this matters mainly if your family is picking English.

**Is English compulsory in AISSEE Class 9?**
Yes. In the Class 9 paper, English is a compulsory section of 25 questions for 50 marks, alongside Maths, Intelligence, Science and Social Studies. Vocabulary directly feeds reading comprehension and grammar questions here.

**Can my child really build enough vocabulary in 90 days?**
Yes, with a daily routine rather than cramming. Twenty focused minutes a day over 90 days builds a working vocabulary of roughly 700 to 900 words in context — more than enough for the AISSEE's word-meaning, synonym and antonym questions.

**Should my child memorise a dictionary word list?**
No — memorising long alphabetical lists is the fastest way to forget everything. Learn 8 to 12 words a day in real sentences, revise them on a fixed cycle, and connect them through root words. Retention comes from use, not from lists.

**Do spelling and word-meaning questions have negative marking in AISSEE?**
There is no negative marking anywhere in the AISSEE. If your child is unsure of a word-meaning answer, they should always attempt it — a guess can only add marks, never take them away.
