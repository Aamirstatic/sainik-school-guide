## A PTM at a boarding school is a different animal

At a day school, a parent-teacher meeting is mostly about marks. At a Sainik School, the teacher in front of you is often also the person who sees your child at morning PT, in the dining hall, on the games field and in the dormitory at night. That changes the conversation completely. You are not just asking "how is he doing in maths?" — you are asking how a child who lives away from home is actually coping, and the honest answer rarely fits in one subject's grade.

Parents walking into their first PTM usually expect a report card and a signature. What they actually get is a fuller picture: academic progress, yes, but also conduct, participation, health, sleep, friendships and homesickness. The parents who get the most out of the meeting are the ones who arrive with three or four specific questions, not a general "how is my child?"

## What actually gets discussed

**Academics — but in context.** Teachers will talk about subjects, but listen for the pattern behind the marks. Is your child weak in one subject or drifting across all of them? Is the problem understanding, or speed, or careless errors, or simply not revising? A child who scores well in class tests but poorly in full-length papers usually has a stamina or exam-technique problem, not a knowledge problem — and that distinction changes what you do at home during holidays.

**Conduct and participation.** In a residential school, conduct is not a footnote. House masters notice things parents never see: whether a child volunteers, leads, follows instructions, handles losing a match, or helps a younger cadet. Ask about it directly. A child who is quiet in class but active in sports or NCC drill is not "doing nothing" — the school sees a different side of him than the marksheet shows.

**Health, food and sleep.** This is the section parents skip and later regret skipping. Boarding life runs on a fixed routine, and small problems — poor sleep, skipped breakfast, recurring stomach trouble, an injury not fully healed — show up as poor concentration weeks later. Ask whether your child eats properly in the mess, sleeps on time, and has visited the infirmary, and for what.

**Settling in, especially in the first year.** For a new Class 6 cadet, the first PTM is mostly about adjustment. Homesickness is normal and usually fades; what matters is whether it is fading or deepening. Ask the house master, not just the subject teacher: how is your child with his dormitory mates, does he write or call home regularly, and does he seem settled or withdrawn?

**The plan, not just the verdict.** A good PTM ends with a plan: what the child will work on before the next term, what support the school will give, and what parents should reinforce during holidays. If the meeting ends with only "he is fine" or "he needs to work harder," you have not yet had the real conversation — ask one more question.

## The questions worth asking

| Area | Ask this | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Academics | "Which one subject needs the most work, and what exactly is going wrong in it?" | Turns a vague "work harder" into one fixable problem |
| Study habits | "Does he revise on his own, or only when told?" | Self-study is the skill boarding school is supposed to build |
| Conduct | "How does he behave when he is not being watched?" | House and field behaviour reveal character the classroom hides |
| Health | "Has he been to the infirmary, and is he eating and sleeping properly?" | Small health issues masquerade as academic problems |
| Friendships | "Who does he spend time with, and is he settled in his house?" | A settled child learns better; an isolated one does not |
| Next step | "What should we practise with him during the next holidays?" | Gives parents a job instead of a worry |

## Questions that waste everyone's time

Asking "is my child the best in class?" helps nobody. So does comparing your child with another cadet by name in front of a teacher, or demanding a rank the school does not publish. Teachers answer hundreds of parents in a day; the specific, child-focused question gets the specific, useful answer. The competitive question gets a polite non-answer.

Similarly, do not use the PTM to relitigate admission, fees or school policy. Those have their own channels. A PTM is fifteen or twenty minutes about one child — spend it on that child.

## Before, during and after: a simple checklist

- **Before you travel:** Read the last report card properly and note the two subjects with the biggest gap between effort and marks. Write your questions down — parents forget half of them in the corridor queue. Carry your child's recent letters or call notes; a pattern you noticed at home ("he sounds tired every Sunday") is useful evidence for a teacher.
- **During the meeting:** Start with the house master if you can, then subject teachers. Listen before defending: if a teacher reports a problem, ask for an example before explaining it away. Note names — knowing which teacher said what matters when you follow up.
- **After you get home:** Write down the two or three action points the same day, while they are fresh. Share them with your child without turning it into a lecture: "your maths teacher says the problem is speed, not understanding, so we will practise timed sums in the holidays" lands very differently from "your teacher complained about you." Check progress after a few weeks through the next call home, not by waiting for the next PTM months away.

## If you cannot attend in person

Distance is a real constraint — Sainik Schools draw cadets from across a state, and not every parent can travel for every meeting. In that case, do not simply skip it. Write to the school in advance, ask whether a phone conversation with the house master is possible, and send your questions in writing. A short, specific letter ("please tell us about his maths progress and his health this term") gets a far better reply than silence followed by worry. And if an elder sibling, relative or local guardian attends in your place, brief them with your questions — a stand-in who only collects the report card adds nothing.

## The honest summary

A Sainik School PTM is not a verdict on your parenting, and it is not a prize-giving. It is the one structured moment in the term when the people who live with your child tell you, plainly, how he is doing as a whole person. Go with questions, listen for patterns rather than single marks, and leave with a plan. That is what the meeting is for — and it is worth the journey.

## FAQs

**How often do Sainik Schools hold parent-teacher meetings?**

The exact schedule varies by school and is set in each school's own calendar and circulars, so check your school's notice rather than assuming a fixed national pattern. Most schools hold them a few times a year, often around term results.

**Should both parents attend a Sainik School PTM?**

It is not a rule, but it helps when both can attend, since the meeting covers academics, discipline, health and hostel life together. If only one parent can travel, that parent should carry the other's questions written down.

**Can parents raise a complaint at a PTM?**

Yes, but a PTM is best for your own child's progress. A general complaint about school policy is better raised in writing through the school's proper channel, so it gets a recorded reply.

**What should I do after the PTM?**

Write down the two or three action points the same day, agree them with your child, and follow up after a few weeks. A meeting with no follow-up changes nothing.

**Is a PTM different for a new cadet's parents?**

Yes. For a first-term cadet the conversation is mostly about settling in: homesickness, routine, food, sleep and friendships, rather than marks alone.
