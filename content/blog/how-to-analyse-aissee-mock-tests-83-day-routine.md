## Solving papers is not preparation. Analysing them is

Last year, a parent told us his son had solved "twenty mock papers" and the score had not moved in a month. Twenty papers. Same score. He was baffled; we weren't. The boy was practising his mistakes, not fixing them — solving a paper, glancing at the answer key, and moving on to the next one with the same gaps intact.

Here's the thing: a mock test is a diagnostic, not a rehearsal. The paper itself teaches almost nothing. The thirty minutes after the paper — spent properly — teach everything. With 80 days left to the AISSEE 2027 exam on 31 January, your child has time for about 10 to 12 full papers. That's plenty, if each one is analysed. It's nowhere near enough if they aren't.

## The 11-week mock calendar

The routine changes as the exam approaches. Early papers are for finding gaps; late papers are for exam-day behaviour — timing, bubbling, staying calm at 2 PM.

| Phase | Dates | Full papers | What the analysis focuses on |
|-------|-------|-------------|------------------------------|
| **Find the gaps** | 12 Nov – 30 Nov | 1 per week (3 total) | Which chapters are weak? Which mistake type dominates? |
| **Fix the gaps** | 1 Dec – 31 Dec | 1 per week (4 total) | Is the weak section improving? Are silly slips falling? |
| **Exam behaviour** | 1 Jan – 24 Jan | 2 per week (6 total) | Timing per section, OMR bubbling, 2 PM stamina |
| **Taper** | 25 – 31 Jan | 1 final paper, 4–5 days before | Light review only; no new paper the day before |

That's 13 papers at most — and honestly, 10 done well is better than 13 rushed. Every paper is taken at 2 PM, the real exam time, with the phone in another room and the OMR sheet bubbled as you go, not at the end. If your child has never bubbled answers under time pressure, the first paper will be a shock. Good — better now than on 31 January.

## The 30-minute review (same evening, no exceptions)

Analysis delayed is analysis abandoned. The paper is reviewed the same evening, in three 10-minute blocks:

**Minutes 1–10: sort every wrong answer into a bucket.** There are only four buckets, and the bucket matters more than the question:
- **Didn't know** — the concept was never studied. Fix: study the chapter this week.
- **Knew but rushed** — the method was right, the arithmetic or reading wasn't. Fix: slow down on that section; this is a habit, not a knowledge gap.
- **Silly slip** — bubbled the wrong option, misread "not" as "is", copied a number wrong. Fix: a personal checklist, read twice on the last 10 questions.
- **Ran out of time** — questions left blank at the end. Fix: a time plan per section (see below).

**Minutes 11–20: re-solve the fixable ones.** Not all of them — the "knew but rushed" and "silly slip" ones. If your child can't re-solve it correctly with the answer key closed, it moves to the "didn't know" bucket.

**Minutes 21–30: update the tracker and pick next week's targets.** One line per paper, in a notebook or a sheet on the fridge. This is the whole system in miniature.

## The score tracker that tells the truth

A single total score hides everything. What you need is section-wise, paper by paper. Here's a worked example — illustrative numbers for a Class 6 child, to show what the pattern looks like:

| Paper | Date | Maths /150 | Language /50 | Intelligence /50 | GK /50 | Total /300 |
|-------|------|-----------|--------------|------------------|--------|------------|
| 1 | 15 Nov | 82 | 30 | 28 | 26 | 166 |
| 2 | 22 Nov | 88 | 32 | 30 | 27 | 177 |
| 3 | 29 Nov | 95 | 31 | 33 | 28 | 187 |
| 4 | 6 Dec | 101 | 34 | 35 | 30 | 200 |

Read it the right way: the total climbed from 166 to 200, but the real story is in the columns. Maths jumped 19 marks — the drills worked. GK crawled from 26 to 30 — four papers, almost no movement. That child doesn't need "more hard work" in general. They need two weeks of GK drills, specifically static GK and the defence/sports facts that repeat every year. Without the tracker, the parent sees "200, good" and misses the one section that will decide the merit list.

And note the qualifying bar while you're at it: 25% per section means at least 38 in Maths, 13 in each 50-mark section, and 120 overall for Class 6. Qualifying is the floor, not the goal — seats go by merit — but any section below its 25% line is a red alert, whatever the total says.

## When the score is stuck

If the total hasn't moved in three papers, don't add more papers. Do this instead:

1. **Check the buckets.** If "didn't know" dominates, the syllabus isn't finished — stop mocking and go back to chapters for two weeks. If "silly slip" dominates, the fix is a routine, not more study: a 2-minute answer-sheet check at the end of each section.
2. **Check one section at a time.** A flat total with a rising Maths score and a falling GK score is not a plateau — it's a leak. Fix the leaking section with chapter-wise drills, not full papers.
3. **Check the clock.** If the last 15 questions are always rushed or blank, the paper needs a time plan: for Class 6, roughly 60 minutes for Maths, 25 for Language, 25 for Intelligence, 25 for GK, with 15 minutes kept for bubbling and review. Adjust to your child's strengths, then keep the plan fixed for three papers before judging it.

One more thing, and it's the most important line in this article: **AISSEE has no negative marking.** A blank scores zero and a wrong answer scores zero. So the end-of-paper rule is simple — attempt everything, guess sensibly on the leftovers, never leave a blank. Children who have been trained by school exams to skip uncertain questions lose 10 to 15 marks a paper to this habit alone. Break it now, while there's time.

## The final week

The last full paper happens about 4 to 5 days before the exam — around 26 or 27 January. After that: formula sheets, the error log's greatest hits, the admit card, and rest. A child who walks into the hall on 31 January having slept well and revised lightly will outperform a tired child who solved one more paper the night before. The work is done by then. Trust it.

## FAQs

**How many mock tests should my child solve before AISSEE 2027?**
About 10 to 12 full papers across the 80 days left — one a week from mid-November, then two a week in January. Fewer papers, each properly analysed, beat twice as many solved and forgotten.

**How long should mock test analysis take?**
About 30 minutes on the same evening as the paper: 10 minutes to sort every wrong answer into a mistake bucket, 10 minutes to re-solve the fixable ones, and 10 minutes to update the score tracker and pick next week's targets.

**My child's mock score is stuck around the same number. What is wrong?**
Usually one section is leaking marks while the others are stable. Check the section-wise tracker: if Maths is flat but GK is rising, the problem is not effort, it is method. Target the flat section with chapter-wise drills for two weeks before judging again.

**Should Class 6 children attempt every question in AISSEE?**
Yes. AISSEE 2027 has no negative marking, so a blank scores the same as a wrong answer — zero. The strategy is to attempt everything, guessing sensibly on the ones left at the end, never leaving blanks.

**When should my child stop taking full mock tests before the exam?**
Take the last full paper about 4 to 5 days before 31 January 2027. The final days are for light revision, formula sheets and rest — not for a new paper that could shake confidence.

**What is the qualifying score in AISSEE 2027?**
25 percent in each section and 40 percent overall — 75 out of 300 for Class 6 and 160 out of 400 for Class 9. The rule does not apply to SC/ST candidates. But remember, qualifying is only the floor; seats go by merit list.
