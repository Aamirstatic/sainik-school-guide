## The one-line answer

For most families, **Class 6 is the better bet** — not because the competition is thinner (it isn't; far more parents target Class 6), but because the exam is lighter, your child gets the language choice, and the intake is the big main door into the school. Class 9 is a real but much smaller door, built for families who discovered Sainik Schools late or whose child only became ready later. Here is the honest breakdown.

## Why Class 6 is usually the better bet

**1. The paper is lighter, and it matters.** 300 marks over two and a half hours, with Maths at half the weight, is a fundamentally more forgiving exam for a 10- or 11-year-old than 400 marks of English-only Class 8-level content. A child who is average in English but strong in Hindi or a regional language gets to play on home turf in Class 6 — in Class 9, that option simply does not exist.

**2. The seat math favours it.** Sainik Schools build their cadet strength from Class 6 upward. Class 9 entry exists to fill vacancies — when a cadet leaves, that seat opens. Logically, the main intake is always the larger door. The 2026 cycle had 33 established schools plus 69 new schools taking Class 6 admissions, but only 19 new schools taking Class 9 — and even in the older schools, Class 9 intake is a fraction of the Class 6 intake.

**3. Timing favours early entry.** A cadet who enters in Class 6 gets three full years of the Sainik School system — drill, routine, English-medium instruction, senior-junior culture — before Class 9. A Class 9 entrant walks into an already-formed hierarchy mid-stream. It is absolutely doable (many do it well), but the adjustment load is heavier.

## When Class 9 is actually the right call

Don't treat Class 9 as the consolation prize. It is the right door if:

- **You discovered Sainik Schools late.** Many parents only hear about AISSEE when their child is already in Class 7 or 8. A targeted attempt is infinitely better than waiting and losing the age window.
- **Your child matured late.** Some children — especially boys — simply aren't ready for boarding discipline at 10. A 13-year-old who can handle the English-medium paper and the hostel routine on his own terms can outperform a reluctant 10-year-old.
- **The English base is strong.** Class 9 rewards children already comfortable studying Science and SST in English. If your child is in an English-medium school and doing well, the paper format actually suits them.

## The decision checklist: which door is YOUR child's?

Work through these honestly before you start preparing:

- [ ] **Check the birth date first.** The age window decides eligibility — a child born between 1 Apr 2015 and 31 Mar 2017 fits Class 6; 1 Apr 2012 to 31 Mar 2014 fits Class 9. (PROJECTED — confirm against the 2027 bulletin when released.)
- [ ] **Can the child handle English-only?** If yes comfortably → Class 9 is viable. If English is shaky → Class 6's language choice is the safer door.
- [ ] **How much syllabus gap is there?** Class 6 prep = solid Class 5 NCERT + reasoning practice. Class 9 prep = Class 8 Maths, Science, SST from scratch if the school didn't cover them well.
- [ ] **Is boarding the real goal, or just the exam?** If the child is homesick-prone or very young for their age, Class 6's earlier separation is a genuine factor to weigh with the school counsellor, not just the parent.
- [ ] **One attempt per year, not both.** The windows don't overlap, so the real question is only which single door to aim the year's preparation at.
- [ ] **Don't park a Class 6-ready child for Class 9.** Waiting three years "to be more prepared" almost never helps — the competition rises with the syllabus, and the seat count shrinks.

## A note on competition — the honest version

Parents often ask which has "less competition." Here's the truth: Class 6 has far more applicants **and** far more seats. Class 9 has fewer applicants **and** far fewer seats. The ratio that matters — seats per serious, well-prepared applicant — is kinder at Class 6, because a serious Class 6 candidate mainly competes against other 10-year-olds on a lighter paper, while a Class 9 candidate competes for a handful of vacancies against teenagers who have had two extra years of coaching. Aim where the door is widest and your child is strongest, not where the crowd looks smallest.

## FAQs

**Which is easier: AISSEE Class 6 or Class 9?**
Class 6 is the easier entry in practical terms — the paper is lighter (300 marks over 150 minutes with a language choice), qualifying is 25% per section plus 40% aggregate, and far more seats are filled in Class 6 than in Class 9. Class 9's paper is heavier and the intake is much smaller.

**What is the age limit for AISSEE 2027 Class 6 and Class 9?**
PROJECTED: Class 6 applicants must be born between 1 April 2015 and 31 March 2017 (10 to 12 years on 31 March 2027); Class 9 applicants between 1 April 2012 and 31 March 2014 (13 to 15 years). The 2027 bulletin is not out yet, so confirm when NTA releases it.

**Is there negative marking in AISSEE?**
No. AISSEE uses pen-paper OMR with multiple-choice questions and no negative marking — wrong answers and blanks both score zero.

**Can I apply for both Class 6 and Class 9 in the same year?**
No — eligibility is by age. A child fits exactly one entry window per cycle based on their date of birth, so the choice is made for you unless your child's birthday sits near a boundary year, in which case check the bulletin carefully.

**What subjects are in the AISSEE Class 9 paper?**
The Class 9 paper is 400 marks over 180 minutes: Mathematics 50 questions for 200 marks, plus English, Intelligence, Science, and Social Science at 25 questions and 50 marks each. The entire paper is in English.

**If I miss the Class 6 window, is Class 9 worth attempting?**
Yes, but go in with open eyes: Class 9 intake fills only the vacancies that open up when cadets leave, so it is a much smaller door. Prepare seriously for the 400-mark English-medium paper rather than treating it as a fallback.
