## The score is not the news. The split is.

Seventy days out, most AISSEE families have the same Sunday ritual now: a full mock paper in the morning, a score by lunch, and then — too often — a quiet afternoon where nobody quite knows what to do with the number.

Here is the thing most parents miss. The total score is the least useful number on the sheet. A child can score 190 out of 300 and be in real trouble, while another scores 165 and is nearly safe. The difference is never the total. It is where the marks sit — and whether the child is above the floor in every section, or quietly failing one.

AISSEE has a rule that turns a good total into a trap: your child must clear **25% in every section and 40% overall**. Miss one section floor and the total does not matter. So the first job of any mock analysis is not "how much did we score" but "did we clear every floor, and by how much?"

## The three numbers that actually matter

**1. The section floors.** This is the pass/fail line, and it is lower than most parents think — which is exactly why it gets ignored until it bites.

| Paper | Section | Section total | Floor (25%) | Paper total | Overall floor (40%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Class 6 | Maths | 150 | 37.5 | 300 | 120 |
| Class 6 | Language | 50 | 12.5 | 300 | 120 |
| Class 6 | Intelligence | 50 | 12.5 | 300 | 120 |
| Class 6 | GK | 50 | 12.5 | 300 | 120 |
| Class 9 | Maths | 200 | 50 | 400 | 160 |
| Class 9 | English | 50 | 12.5 | 400 | 160 |
| Class 9 | Intelligence | 50 | 12.5 | 400 | 160 |
| Class 9 | Science | 50 | 12.5 | 400 | 160 |
| Class 9 | Social Science | 50 | 12.5 | 400 | 160 |

In practice, treat a floor of 37.5 as 38 — marks come in whole numbers. And note the rule does not apply the same way to SC/ST candidates, for whom the section and aggregate floors are waived. If your child is in the general, OBC-NCL or defence category, the floors are the first thing to check after every mock.

**2. The weakest-section gap.** Take the section furthest below its floor — or, if all sections clear, the section with the smallest margin above it. That gap, in marks, is the single most honest measure of where the next two weeks should go. A 6-mark gap in GK is a fortnight of daily current-affairs revision. A 20-mark gap in Maths is a different job entirely, and pretending otherwise wastes the fortnight.

**3. The silly-error count.** Go through the paper once more and mark every question your child knew how to solve but still got wrong: the misread question, the wrong bubble, the calculation done right and copied wrong. Count them. In our experience with the families we guide, this number is usually between 8 and 15 marks on a Class 6 paper — and it is the cheapest score in the whole exam, because no new learning is needed to recover it. Only attention.

## A worked example — read it like a doctor reads a report

Imagine a Class 6 scorecard. This is an illustrative example, not a real child's paper:

| Section | Scored | Out of | Floor | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Maths | 96 | 150 | 37.5 | Comfortable |
| Language | 30 | 50 | 12.5 | Comfortable |
| Intelligence | 34 | 50 | 12.5 | Comfortable |
| GK | 11 | 50 | 12.5 | **Below floor** |
| **Total** | **171** | **300** | **120** | Passes overall, fails GK |

Total 171 — well above the 120 aggregate. And yet this child fails, because GK is 1.5 marks short of its floor. The parent who only sees "171, good score" celebrates. The parent who reads the split sees the truth: two weeks of GK, nothing else, until that section is at 20 or above. Then the total takes care of itself.

Now flip it. A child scores 132 overall with every section just above its floor. That child is safer than the 171 above — because the exam's rule is a floor in every room, not a high average. Boring, steady, above the floor everywhere: that is what a qualifying scorecard looks like.

## The three fixes that move a score fastest

**Fix one: the error log, kept honestly.** After every mock, your child writes down each wrong answer in one line — question number, what went wrong, in five words. Three columns only: "didn't know", "knew but slipped", "ran out of time". After three mocks, one column will be visibly fatter than the others, and that column is the study plan. If you only do one thing from this article, do this. Our [mock-test analysis method](/blog/aissee-mock-test-analysis-error-log-method/) walks through the same log in detail.

**Fix two: one section, first hour, every day.** Whatever the weakest section is, it gets the first hour of the study day — not the leftover hour, when the brain is tired and the easy subject has already eaten the best of the day. Two weeks of first-hour work on one section moves it more than two months of evening revision. And keep it narrow: if GK is the gap, the work is 25 questions a day from the last six months of current affairs plus static GK, not a new guidebook.

**Fix three: rehearse at 2 PM.** The Class 6 paper runs from **2:00 to 4:30 PM** and the Class 9 paper from **2:00 to 5:00 PM**. A child who only ever practises at 10 in the morning is training a different brain than the one that will sit the exam. One full paper a week, at the exact exam time, phone in another room, with the OMR sheet and the same pens — that rehearsal is worth more than three untimed practice sets. If a mock score is [stuck around 200](/blog/aissee-mock-scores-stuck-around-200-break-plateau/), the usual cause is not knowledge. It is pace, and pace only improves under real timing.

One last thing, and it matters more than the method. Last week a parent told us their child had started dreading Sundays — not because of the paper, but because of the silence after the score. If that sounds familiar, change the ritual, not the child. Announce one improvement and one repair after every paper, and nothing else. Seventy days is a long time to carry dread, and a very short time to fix a 6-mark gap. Use it for the second job.

## FAQs

**How many mock tests should my child take before AISSEE 2027?**
There is no official number, and no coaching centre's count is a rule. Our guidance is one full timed paper a week from now until mid-January, plus short section tests on weak areas — roughly 8 to 10 full papers in the 70 days left. A paper only helps if you spend as long analysing it as your child spent writing it.

**My child scored low in a mock test. Is that a bad sign at 70 days out?**
No — a low score 70 days out is information, not a verdict. What matters is where the marks went: below a section floor, lost to silly errors, or lost to questions your child had never seen. Each cause has a different fix, and all three are fixable in 70 days.

**Should my child attempt every question in AISSEE 2027?**
Yes. AISSEE has no negative marking — wrong answers and blanks both score zero, so nothing is deducted for a wrong answer. Attempt every question, but answer the sure ones first and return to the guesses at the end, so a hard question never eats the time of five easy ones.

**What if one section is below the qualifying floor in every mock?**
That section becomes the first hour of every study day until it crosses the floor. The floor is 25 percent of the section total, so the gap is usually small — a few marks, not a new syllabus. Work only that section for two weeks, then re-test it alone before judging again.

**How do I stop my child from fearing mock tests?**
Stop announcing the score and start announcing the fix. After every paper, name one thing that improved and one thing to repair next week — nothing more. Children fear mocks when the score feels like a judgment; they use mocks when the score feels like a map.
