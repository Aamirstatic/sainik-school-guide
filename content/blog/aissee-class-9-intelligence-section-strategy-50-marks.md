## The 50 marks most children leave on the table

Ask a Class 9 aspirant which section scares them and you will hear the same answer every time: Mathematics, 200 marks, half the paper. Fair enough — [our Class 9 Maths strategy](/blog/aissee-class-9-maths-section-strategy-200-marks/) exists for exactly that reason. But ask the same child about Intelligence and you usually get a shrug. It feels like the easy section. And that shrug is why it costs marks.

Here is the thing: Intelligence is 25 questions for 50 marks — one-eighth of the paper — and it is the only section where a child who has never seen a coaching classroom can match a child who has. There is no syllabus to memorise, only pattern recognition, and pattern recognition is a habit, not a talent. Fifteen minutes a day for four weeks, and your child will finish the section in under half an hour on 31 January, calmly.

And there is a hard reason to take it seriously. The qualifying rule is **25% in each section and 40% in aggregate** (it does not apply to SC/ST candidates). Twenty-five percent of 50 marks is 12.5 — and since every question carries 2 marks, your child needs **at least 7 of the 25 questions right** to clear the section bar. A child who rushes Intelligence and gets 5 right has failed the section even if their Maths score is strong.

The paper runs **180 minutes, 2:00 PM to 5:00 PM**, in English only, on an OMR sheet — and there is **no negative marking**. That shapes the whole strategy: every question must be attempted, and no question should eat more than about a minute.

## The 7 question types — and what each one is really testing

The official syllabus (Appendix III of the bulletin) lists the Intelligence topics: **analogies, patterns, classification, visual and logical reasoning, series, critical thinking, and family relationships**. Here is what each one looks like in practice. The examples below are our own, for practice — not questions from any past paper.

### 1. Analogies — "A is to B as C is to ?"

The classic. *Book is to Reading as Fork is to… Eating.* Name the relationship before looking at the options — part-to-whole, cause-to-effect, tool-to-use. Children who jump straight to the options get trapped by the one that *looks* related but has the wrong relationship. If your child cannot name the relationship in one short sentence, they are guessing.

### 2. Series — find the next number, letter or figure

Number series reward the boring checks first: difference between consecutive terms, then ratio, then alternating patterns. *3, 6, 11, 18, 27…* — differences 3, 5, 7, 9, so the next is 38. Most Class 9 series questions fall to the difference check alone. The mistake is to stare; the method is to write the differences down.

### 3. Classification — the odd one out

Four words or figures, three belong together, one does not. Find the grouping that covers three items with a single clean rule — "all are prime numbers", "all are fruits" — and distrust a rule that needs an exception.

### 4. Visual reasoning — rotated, folded, mirrored

Figures rotated, reflected, or with a piece cut out. This is the type children improve fastest at, because it is pure practice: ten minutes a day with a printed set, and within two weeks the mirror-image questions stop being scary. Cover the options, picture the answer first, and only then look.

### 5. Logical reasoning — short arguments, one conclusion

A few lines of text, four conclusions, one that actually follows. The discipline is strict: **only what the passage says counts**. Children import outside knowledge and pick the option that sounds true in real life instead of the one the passage proves. Underline the facts. Ignore everything else.

### 6. Critical thinking — the "what is wrong with this argument" type

A smaller share of the section, but the one that separates the top scorers: a short scenario, a claim, and a question about what would weaken or strengthen it. Ask "what would have to be true for this claim to hold?" If the answer is an assumption the passage never makes, the claim is weak.

### 7. Family relationships — the coded blood-relation puzzles

"A is the brother of B. B is the mother of C…" Draw the tree. Every time. Children who try to hold the chain in their head lose track at the third link; children who sketch three boxes and two lines get them all. The most mechanical type in the section, and therefore the most reliable marks — once the drawing habit is fixed.

## The 30-minute attempt plan for exam day

Intelligence should be attempted in **one clean pass of about 25–30 minutes**, and it should not be the first section — Mathematics deserves the freshest mind. Second, after Maths, is the sweet spot in [our paper-attempt strategy](/blog/aissee-paper-attempt-strategy/). First sweep: answer everything solvable in under a minute and circle the rest (~18 minutes). Second sweep: return to the circled ones, at most 2 minutes each (~8 minutes). Final sweep: guess everything still blank — no negative marking means a guess is free (~4 minutes). Never let one question hold you hostage: a stubborn analogy is worth the same 2 marks as the next question, which might take twenty seconds.

## A 4-week drill routine that fits around school

Fifteen minutes a day, after homework, is enough. The routine rotates the seven types so nothing goes stale. (If a younger sibling is preparing too, [the Class 6 Intelligence strategy](/blog/aissee-class-6-intelligence-section-strategy-50-marks/) builds the same habits at a simpler level.)

| Week | Daily 15 minutes | Weekend (45 minutes) |
|---|---|---|
| Week 1 | 10 analogy + 10 classification questions from a previous-year paper | One full timed set (25 questions, 30 minutes) |
| Week 2 | 10 series + 10 visual reasoning questions | Timed set + review every error in the error notebook |
| Week 3 | 10 logical reasoning + 5 family-relation puzzles, drawn as trees | Timed set; re-attempt the questions missed in Week 1 |
| Week 4 | Mixed 15 questions, any type, under 15 minutes | Two timed sets back to back, then a rest day |

Two habits make the routine work. First, the **error notebook**: every wrong answer gets one line — the question type, the trap, the correct rule. By Week 3 the notebook shows the child's real weakness. Second, **always practise on an OMR-style sheet**. Intelligence is fast, and the most common lost marks are answers bubbled against the wrong question number.

## The mistakes that cost the 50 marks

- **Treating it as a "free" section and never practising it.** The qualifying bar is 7 correct answers. Overconfidence is how children score 5.
- **Solving in your head instead of on paper.** Series differences, family trees, mirror images — all belong on the rough sheet.
- **Spending 5 minutes on one question.** Two marks is two marks. Move on.
- **Leaving questions blank.** No negative marking. A guess has a 25% chance; a blank has zero.
- **Practising only one type.** Children love series and avoid visual reasoning. The paper does not ask what you love.

One last thing, and it is the honest part. Intelligence does not need a coaching centre, a thick book, or a paid course. It needs fifteen minutes a day and a parent who checks the error notebook once a week.

## FAQs

**How many marks is the Intelligence section in AISSEE Class 9?**

25 questions, 2 marks each, for 50 marks out of 400. The paper is 150 questions in 180 minutes, and there is no negative marking.

**What question types come in the AISSEE Class 9 Intelligence section?**

Analogies, patterns, classification, visual and logical reasoning, series, critical thinking and family relationships — as listed in the official syllabus in Appendix III of the NTA bulletin.

**How many Intelligence questions must a Class 9 candidate get right to qualify?**

The qualifying rule is 25 percent in each section, which is 12.5 marks out of 50 — so at least 7 of the 25 questions must be correct, since each question carries 2 marks.

**How much time should a Class 9 candidate spend on Intelligence in AISSEE?**

About 25 to 30 minutes of the 180-minute paper. It is the fastest section to attempt once the patterns are familiar, and the saved time can go to Mathematics, which carries 200 marks.

**Is there negative marking in the AISSEE Class 9 Intelligence section?**

No. The AISSEE 2027 bulletin states there is no negative marking for a wrong response, so every question should be attempted.

**Can Intelligence be prepared without coaching?**

Yes — it is the section least dependent on coaching. Fifteen minutes of daily pattern practice from previous-year papers and a simple error notebook is enough for most children.
