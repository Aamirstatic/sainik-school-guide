## Why English vocabulary is worth 15 minutes a day

Parents usually rank English last in AISSEE preparation. Maths is half the paper, so it gets the hours; GK feels easy to revise; English gets whatever time is left. That costs marks in two places.

First, English is worth **50 marks** — 25 questions at 2 marks each — in both Class 6 (as the Language section) and Class 9 (as the English section). Inside that section sit synonyms and antonyms, the vocabulary questions, alongside one unseen comprehension passage and grammar. A child who knows the words reads the passage faster, answers the synonym questions without guessing, and finishes the section with time to spare.

Second, for Class 9 the stakes are higher still: **the entire paper is in English only**. A child who struggles with English words reads the Maths and Science questions more slowly and misunderstands them more often. Vocabulary is not just a 50-mark subject — it is a multiplier on the whole paper.

The good news: vocabulary is the most predictable part of the exam to improve. It needs no talent — just a small daily habit, repeated for 80 days. Here is the plan.

## The 83-day plan in three phases

| Phase | Dates | Focus | Daily time |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Foundations | 10 Nov – 7 Dec (4 weeks) | Everyday words from NCERT books; word families | 15 minutes |
| 2. Expansion | 8 Dec – 4 Jan (4 weeks) | Synonyms, antonyms, root words; words from mock papers | 15 minutes |
| 3. Revision | 5 Jan – 30 Jan (4 weeks) | No new lists; revise the word bank, test weekly | 10 minutes |

Notice what the plan does *not* do: it never asks a child to memorise a 1,000-word list. Five words a day adds up to more than 400 words by the exam — and 400 words a child actually knows beat 1,000 they once glanced at.

## The daily 15-minute routine

**Minutes 1–5: meet five new words.** Take them from one place only: the child's NCERT English book, or yesterday's mock paper. For each word, write down three things — the meaning in simple words, one synonym, and one antonym. That is the whole lesson. No long lists, no dictionary marathons.

**Minutes 6–10: use each word in a sentence.** The child writes one sentence per word, about their own life: "Brave" becomes "My grandfather was brave in the army." A word used in a sentence the child invented is remembered far longer than one copied from a list.

**Minutes 11–15: revise yesterday's five words.** Cover the meanings and try to recall them; any word the child cannot recall goes back into tomorrow's list. This is the part most families skip, and the part that makes the plan work: a word met once is forgotten in a week, a word met three times stays.

## Learn words in families, not one by one

English words travel in families, and AISSEE asks about the whole family. When your child meets "happy", the same sitting should also cover "happiness", "happily", "unhappy" and "glad". When they meet "brave", add "bravery", "bravely" and "coward". One root word learned this way is worth four words learned separately — and synonym-antonym questions become much easier, because the child already knows the word's neighbours.

The same trick works with opposites: every new word gets its antonym the same day — hot–cold, victory–defeat, ancient–modern. The exam's vocabulary questions are very often exactly this: pick the synonym, pick the antonym.

## Where to find the words (and where not to)

The best source is the one the exam itself uses: **the NCERT English books** — Class 5 level for Class 6 entry, Class 8 level for Class 9 entry. Every difficult word in those books is fair game, and each comes with a sentence showing how it is used. Start with the child's own textbook and pull out the words they do not know.

The second-best source is your child's own mistakes. Every synonym or vocabulary question they get wrong in a mock test goes straight into the word bank, with the correct answer beside it. A word that has already cost marks once is a word worth learning properly.

What *not* to use: random "500 difficult words" lists from the internet, and words from books far above the child's level. AISSEE tests school-level English — a Class 5 child needs the words in their own books, known properly.

## Three mistakes that waste the effort

**Learning words without sentences.** A child who can recite "courage = bravery" but cannot use "courage" in a sentence will freeze when the question gives them a sentence to complete. Meaning without use is half a word.

**Cramming before a test, then stopping.** Vocabulary learned in one big session fades within days. Fifteen minutes every day beats two hours on Sunday. If a day is missed, just do the next day's five words — do not try to catch up by doing ten.

**Ignoring the passage.** The comprehension passage in the English section is also a vocabulary test: children who know more words read it faster and answer more accurately. So the daily reading habit matters as much as the word list. Ten minutes of storybook reading a day, in English, is part of the vocabulary plan — not extra work on top of it.

## The weekly test that makes it stick

Every Sunday, give the child a 10-minute test: 10 words from the week's list, five to match with synonyms, five with antonyms. Score it out of 10 and write the score in a notebook. Below 7 means the daily routine needs its revision step done properly; and watching the number climb week by week is the motivation that keeps a 11-week plan alive.

Keep the notebook. By the last week of January it holds the child's entire word bank, and the final revision is simply reading it, a few pages a day. No new words in the last week — the exam rewards words known cold, not words met yesterday.

## FAQs

**How many marks is English worth in AISSEE 2027?**
50 marks in both classes: 25 questions worth 2 marks each. In Class 6 it is the Language section; in Class 9 it is the English section. Vocabulary — synonyms and antonyms — is part of that section, along with one unseen comprehension passage and grammar.

**How many new words should my child learn each day?**
Five new words a day, learned properly, beats twenty skimmed and forgotten. Over 80 days that is 400 words — and words learned in families and used in sentences are the ones that survive until exam day.

**Does vocabulary matter for subjects other than English?**
Yes, especially in Class 9. The entire Class 9 paper is in English only, so a child who cannot read the Maths and Science questions comfortably loses marks outside the English section too.

**What is the best source for AISSEE vocabulary?**
The NCERT English books of the child's own class, plus a children's dictionary. The AISSEE paper is built on NCERT Class 5 level for Class 6 entry and NCERT Class 8 level for Class 9 entry — words from those books are the closest to what the exam actually asks.

**My child forgets new words within a week. What is wrong?**
Usually the word was only read, never used. A word needs three encounters to stick: see it in a sentence, say or write a sentence with it, and meet it again in a test a few days later. Reading a list once is not learning.

**Should we start vocabulary now, with 80 days left?**
Yes — now is the right time. Vocabulary is the slowest subject to improve because it depends on repetition over weeks. Starting in January would be too late; starting in November gives every word time to be revised three or four times before 31 January.
