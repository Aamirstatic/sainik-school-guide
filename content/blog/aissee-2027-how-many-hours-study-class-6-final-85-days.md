## The question every parent asks — and the answer nobody likes

"How many hours should my child study?"

It is the first question in almost every AISSEE conversation I have. And here is the honest answer, with 80 days left to the 31 January 2027 exam: **2.5 to 3 focused hours a day on a school day, and about 4 hours on a Sunday.**

Not six. Not eight. Not the 10-hour timetables you see in coaching-centre posters.

I know that number feels small. When the exam is 80 days away and the syllabus still has gaps, 3 hours a day sounds like not trying. But here is the thing — your child is 10, 11, maybe 12 years old. They already spend 5 to 6 hours in school. Add homework, meals, travel and a little play, and the day is nearly gone before AISSEE study even starts. The hours that remain are precious precisely because there are so few of them. What matters is not how many hours your child sits at the desk. It is how many of those hours are real work.

## What "focused hours" actually means

A focused hour is 50 minutes of work and 10 minutes of break — pencil in hand, questions being solved, answers being checked. A distracted hour is a child staring at a Maths book while thinking about cricket, or "watching" a concept video while scrolling a phone under the table.

Here is a test I give parents. At the end of a study session, ask your child one question: "What did you solve today?" If they can show you 20 solved questions, a page of notes, or a mock-test score — that was a real hour. If they can only say "I studied Maths" — it was desk time, not study time. Three focused hours beat six distracted ones. Every time.

## The weekly hours, by subject

The Class 6 paper is 300 marks: Maths 150, Language 50, Intelligence 50, General Knowledge 50. Your child's weekly hours should follow the marks, not the chapters they enjoy most.

| Subject | Marks | School-day minutes | Sunday minutes | Weekly total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mathematics | 150 | 60 | 90 | ~7.5 hours |
| Language (English/Hindi) | 50 | 25 | 30 | ~2.75 hours |
| Intelligence / Reasoning | 50 | 25 | 30 | ~2.75 hours |
| General Knowledge | 50 | 20 | 30 | ~2.25 hours |
| Full-length mock test (2 PM) | — | — | 150 + review | ~3 hours |
| **Total** | **300** | **~2.2 hours/day** | **~5.5 hours** | **~18 hours/week** |

Two things to notice. First, Maths gets half the time because it carries half the marks — and because Maths is the one subject where daily short drills beat one long weekly session. Second, the Sunday mock is sacred. The real exam starts at 2:00 PM, so the full-length Sunday mock should start at 2:00 PM too, with the same lunch beforehand. In the last two weeks, do every full mock at 2 PM so your child's brain is trained for those exact hours.

## A school-day plan that actually fits a real day

Most timetables fail because they are written for a child who does not exist — one with no homework, no school, no tiredness. Here is one built for a real school-going child:

- **4:00 – 4:30 PM:** Home, rest, snack. No books. A tired child learns nothing.
- **4:30 – 5:30 PM:** Maths — 30 minutes of new practice, 30 minutes of revision of yesterday's mistakes.
- **5:30 – 5:45 PM:** Break. Play, walk, anything that is not a screen.
- **5:45 – 6:30 PM:** One other subject — Language, Intelligence or GK, rotating by day.
- **6:30 – 7:00 PM:** School homework (it has to fit somewhere, and it is better done before dinner than at 10 PM).
- **7:00 PM onwards:** Dinner, family, and rest. The books are closed.

That is 2.5 hours of AISSEE work, plus homework, inside a day that still has a childhood in it. On Sundays, the morning is free and the 2 PM mock anchors the day.

## The burnout checklist — read this every Sunday

If any of these show up, your child is studying too much, not too little:

- Falling asleep in class or at the study table
- Crying over small mistakes, or refusing to sit down to study at all
- Mock scores falling week after week despite more hours
- Headaches, lost appetite, or no interest in play
- Saying "I can't do this" — and meaning it

The fix is never more hours. It is fewer hours, more sleep, and one honest conversation about what is actually hard — usually one chapter, not the whole exam. Cut the weakest hour first, keep the Maths drill, and protect sleep ruthlessly in January. A rested child outperforms an exhausted one on exam day.

## The last-month rule

In January, hours rise slightly — to about 3 to 3.5 a day. But the extra time should go into mock tests and mistake revision, not into new chapters. No new chapter in the last 15 days. None. A half-learned chapter in January costs more marks than it earns, because it steals revision time from the chapters your child already knows.

## What to do tonight

Sit with your child and count this week honestly — not the hours at the desk, the hours of real work. If the number is under 10, add one Maths drill a day and you are on track. If it is over 25, cut something tonight. Then build the week around the table above, and protect the Sunday 2 PM mock like it is the real exam. Because in 80 days, it will be.

## FAQs

**How many hours should a Class 6 student study for AISSEE daily?**

About 2.5 to 3 focused hours a day on a school day, and about 4 hours on a Sunday — with 80 days left to the 31 January 2027 exam. More than that at age 10–12 usually buys fatigue, not marks.

**Is 1 hour a day enough for AISSEE preparation?**

Not with 80 days left. One hour was fine a year ago, when the child was building a base. Now your child needs at least 2 hours of real work a day, because the syllabus has to be finished and revised before the 31 January exam.

**Can my child study 5–6 hours a day for AISSEE?**

They physically can, but they should not. A 10–12-year-old already spends 5–6 hours in school. Adding 5–6 more home hours exceeds what a child's brain can absorb and risks burnout before January. If a coach prescribes 6-hour days for an 11-year-old, question the coach, not your child.

**What is the best time of day for a child to study for AISSEE?**

Late afternoon to early evening — roughly 4:30 to 7:30 PM, after rest and a snack — works best for most school-going children. Since the AISSEE 2027 exam starts at 2:00 PM, the Sunday full-length mock should also start at 2:00 PM so the brain is trained for those hours.

**Should study hours increase in the last month before AISSEE 2027?**

Slightly — to about 3 to 3.5 hours — but through weekly full-length mock tests at 2 PM, not longer chapter reading. Protect sleep ruthlessly in January; a rested child outperforms an exhausted one on exam day.

**Does screen-based study count toward daily AISSEE hours?**

Only if it is active — solving questions on screen, or watching a concept video and pausing to practise. Passive video-watching counts for far less than its clock time suggests. For an 11-year-old, paper-and-pen work remains the most reliable hour-for-hour option.
