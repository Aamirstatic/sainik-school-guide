## Why this plan is different from the September version

We published a 30-day revision plan in September. It was honest about its own limits: the exam date was still tentative, pulled from NTA's exam calendar, and the plan said so — shift it with the date, whenever NTA fixed it.

NTA has now fixed it. The AISSEE 2027 information bulletin, released on **3 November 2026**, confirms the exam on **Sunday, 31 January 2027**, and the application window closes on **2 December 2026**. That changes the plan from a template into a calendar. The last 30 days are no longer "roughly January" — they are **Friday, 1 January to Saturday, 30 January 2027**, with four Sundays in between and the exam the next morning.

So this is not a re-skin of the old article. The structure is the same — closure, rehearsal, calm — but every date is now real, the mock Sundays are named, and the December handover is built in, because the plan only works if December does its job first.

## First, December has to finish the syllabus

The 30-day plan starts on 1 January. That means the syllabus must be finished by **31 December**. Not "mostly finished" — finished, including the chapters your child keeps avoiding.

December is not a quiet month. The JNVST exam is on **28 November**, the AISSEE form closes on **2 December**, and then the winter break arrives in the last week of December — the single best revision window of the whole year, because school is off and the house is quiet. If your child uses the winter break the way our [winter-break sprint guide]({{< relref "aissee-2027-winter-break-10-day-revision-sprint.md" >}}) describes — ten focused days, one weak section per day — January starts with closure instead of panic. If December drifts, no January plan can rescue it. That is the honest part. The rest of this article assumes December did its job.

## The four weeks, with real dates

| Week | Dates | Focus | The one thing that must happen |
|---|---|---|---|
| Week 1 — Close | Fri 1 – Thu 7 Jan | Finish every half-done chapter; no new topics after 7 Jan | Error log started; every mistake from December mocks listed in one notebook |
| Week 2 — Rehearse | Fri 8 – Thu 14 Jan | Two section drills a day; first timed full paper in exam conditions | Sunday 10 Jan mock at 2 PM, analysed the same evening |
| Week 3 — Sharpen | Fri 15 – Thu 21 Jan | Weak-section drills only; GK current affairs frozen at 15 Jan | Sunday 17 Jan mock; score compared with 10 Jan, not with classmates |
| Week 4 — Calm | Fri 22 – Sat 30 Jan | Light revision, formula sheet, error log only; nothing new | Sunday 24 Jan mock (last one); Sat 30 Jan full rest; exam Sun 31 Jan, 2 PM |

Look at the shape of it. The work gets lighter as the exam gets closer, not heavier. That is deliberate. A child who peaks on 20 January and collapses on 28 January does not need more effort — they needed a plan that tapered.

## The four Sunday mocks — and the rule that makes them work

Four mocks: **3, 10, 17 and 24 January**, all on Sundays, all starting at **2 PM** — the real exam time. Class 6 finishes at 4:30 PM, Class 9 at 5 PM. Sit the whole paper in one go, phone in another room, OMR sheet bubbled properly, no pausing for a snack mid-paper.

Here is the rule that decides whether mocks help or hurt: **the mock is worthless without the evening analysis.** The same evening, go through every wrong answer and every question that took too long, and sort them into three piles — did not know, knew but rushed, knew but misread. The first pile gets one hour of study the next morning. The second and third piles are the ones that actually win marks, because they are free marks — the knowledge was already there. Our [maths speed guide]({{< relref "aissee-maths-speed-7-traps-that-eat-your-minutes.md" >}}) lists the seven traps that eat minutes; most children lose more marks to those traps than to chapters they never studied.

And one more rule: no fifth mock. A mock on 30 January, the day before the exam, is the most common self-inflicted wound in this whole process. A bad score the day before destroys a month of confidence; a good score teaches nothing new. Stop at four.

## The daily rhythm: two sessions, not one marathon

Three focused hours a day for Class 6, four for Class 9. Split them: a morning session (90 minutes, the weakest section) and an evening session (90 minutes, mixed revision plus the error log). Between them, real life — school, play, food, sleep. The exam is at 2 PM, so the brain needs to be sharp in the afternoon, which is exactly why the Sunday mocks are at 2 PM and why the [2 PM exam-aligned daily timetable]({{< relref "aissee-2027-daily-timetable-2pm-exam-aligned-plan.md" >}}) shifts the heavy work to the afternoon in the final weeks.

If your child is studying five or six hours a day in January and the mock scores are falling, the hours are the problem. Cut an hour, add sleep, and watch the score. I have seen that trade work more often than any new guidebook.

## What parents should do — and stop doing — in January

Do this: keep the house calm, keep meals and sleep on schedule, and ask one question each evening — "what did you fix today?" Not "how much did you study?" Fixed is the unit that matters in the last month.

Stop doing this: comparing your child with the neighbour's child, buying a new book in the third week of January, and discussing cutoffs at the dinner table. None of it adds a mark. And if the pressure is showing — tears over small mistakes, sleep trouble, saying they want to quit — read our [December pressure plan]({{< relref "aissee-2027-exam-pressure-december-73-days-plan.md" >}}) before you add another hour of study. Pressure in January is usually exhaustion wearing a different name.

One small scene, because it happens every year: a father told me his son had revised the same maths chapter four times in the last week and still got its questions wrong in the mock. The problem was not the chapter. It was that the boy had never once written the formulas from memory on a blank page. Re-reading feels like learning. It is not. In January, every revision session should end with the book closed — write it, solve it, say it. If it cannot be reproduced with the book closed, it is not revised.

## The last three days, hour by hour

- **Friday, 29 January:** one hour only — formula sheet and error log, book closed. Pack the bag: admit card (download it the moment it appears in mid-January and check the name, photo and centre the same evening), school ID, two black pens, water bottle. Check the route to the centre once.
- **Saturday, 30 January:** rest. No mock, no new chapter, no "just one more test." A walk, a favourite meal, an early night. The exam starts at 2 PM tomorrow — there is no advantage in arriving tired.
- **Sunday, 31 January:** normal breakfast, reach the centre early, and remember the one rule that no negative marking buys you — attempt every question. A guessed answer costs nothing; a blank answer scores the same zero. But guess only after the first pass is done: easy questions first, hard ones second, guesses last.

## Your January checklist

- [ ] Syllabus closed by 31 December — nothing new after 7 January
- [ ] Error log in one notebook, updated after every mock
- [ ] Four Sunday mocks booked: 3, 10, 17, 24 January, all at 2 PM
- [ ] Admit card downloaded and checked the day it appears (expected mid-January)
- [ ] Formula sheet written from memory, not copied, by 20 January
- [ ] Saturday, 30 January kept completely free
- [ ] Exam day: 31 January, 2 PM, admit card + school ID + two black pens

January is not the month to find a new strategy. It is the month to execute the one you already have, on dates that are now fixed. The plan above is short on purpose — a tired parent and a nervous 11-year-old should both be able to follow it without a spreadsheet.
