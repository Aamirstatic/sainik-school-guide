## Seventy days is exactly when it gets hard

Here is the thing nobody tells you about the last ten weeks before AISSEE. The first months of preparation are exciting — new books, new routine, a goal to talk about. The last stretch is different. The syllabus is mostly done, the mocks have started, and every mock score now feels like a verdict. Your child is 11. Old enough to understand exactly what is at stake — a seat at a Sainik School, the first real competition of their life — and too young to have the emotional toolkit to carry it.

If your child has changed in the last two weeks, you are probably not imagining it. A child who loved maths in September and now says they hate it. A child who cries over a spelling mistake. A child who suddenly wants to sleep at 7 PM, or cannot sleep at all. At 11, stress rarely arrives as words. It arrives as behaviour — and the parents who catch it early are the ones whose children walk into the exam hall steady instead of shaky.

## Read the signals, then act the same day

Do not wait for your child to say "I am stressed." They will not — not in those words. Use this table instead. If you spot a row, do the thing in the third column tonight, not next week. Last year, a mother called us because her son had stopped eating breakfast — not a hunger strike, just no appetite, every morning, for a week. She thought it was a stomach bug. It was the exam, sitting in his stomach. She moved breakfast to after a 15-minute walk together, no books at the table, and the appetite came back in three days. Small fixes.

| Signal | What it usually means | What to do tonight |
|---|---|---|
| Stomach ache or headache before study, fine at play | Anxiety, not illness — the body complains where words cannot | Drop the hardest topic for today; start tomorrow with their strongest subject instead |
| Tears over a small mistake | The score has become their self-worth | Say the score out loud yourself, calmly: "42 out of 50. That is a good paper." Then ask what one question taught them |
| "I don't want to study" with no reason | Exhaustion, not laziness — or fear of the next mock | Shorten the session to 30 minutes of easy revision; a short win beats a long battle |
| Cannot sleep, or sleeping far too much | The exam is running in their head at night | No study after 8 PM this week; a walk, a bath, a story — boredom is the cure, not more revision |
| Comparing themselves to a friend or cousin | Someone, somewhere, is keeping score out loud | Find the sentence that did it and kill it gently: "Your paper is yours. His is his." |
| Suddenly slow in mocks they used to finish | Fear of the clock, not lack of knowledge | Do one untimed paper this week — no clock at all — and let them remember they know the material |

If a signal lasts more than two weeks, or comes with panic symptoms, skip the table and talk to the school counsellor or a child psychologist. That is not an overreaction. It is the same instinct as taking a fever to a doctor.

## Five calming tools your child can actually use

Forget the poster advice. An 11-year-old will not meditate for twenty minutes. These five work because a child can do them alone, in under five minutes, without feeling silly.

**1. The 4-4-4 breath.** In for 4 counts, hold for 4, out for 4. Repeat four times. CBSE's own stress guide for students recommends deep breathing to calm a racing heart before exams, and counsellors say two to three minutes of focused breathing before a paper genuinely lowers anxiety. Teach it now, during a calm evening — not on exam morning, when everything new feels like one more thing to fail at.

**2. The worry window.** Give worry a time and a place: ten minutes after dinner, every day, when your child may say every fear out loud and you just listen. No fixing, no "don't worry." When the ten minutes end, the window closes until tomorrow. Children who have a place to put their fear stop carrying it into the study hour.

**3. The easy win first.** Every study session should start with five minutes of something your child already does well — five known maths sums, one known grammar rule. Confidence is a warm-up, not a pep talk. A child who starts with a win studies longer than a child who starts with their weakest chapter.

**4. The movement break.** Ten minutes of walking, skipping, or just going outside between study blocks. CBSE's guidance is blunt about this: exercise reduces fatigue and improves concentration. It is not wasted study time. It is what makes the next forty minutes work.

**5. The "one thing I know" card.** Before every mock, your child writes one sentence on a scrap of paper: one thing they know cold. "I know the area of a rectangle." They carry it into the room. When panic comes — and it will, for about thirty seconds — they read their own sentence. It is a small anchor, and it works because it is theirs.

## The 7-day calm-home plan

Pressure is not fixed by one conversation. It is fixed by a week of small changes at home. Try this, starting tomorrow:

| Day | One change | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Cut one study hour | Replace it with play — not a reward for studying, just play |
| Tuesday | Kill one comparison | No mention of any other child's marks, all day, by anyone in the house |
| Wednesday | Teach the 4-4-4 breath | Practise it together at bedtime, twice, like a game |
| Thursday | Move breakfast | Eat together with no books on the table; talk about anything except the exam |
| Friday | One untimed paper | No clock, no score announced — just the paper, finished calmly |
| Saturday | A full evening off | A film, a walk, a relative's house — the exam does not exist after 6 PM |
| Sunday | The worry window starts | Ten minutes after dinner, every day from now on; you listen, you do not fix |

If the house feels different by Sunday, you have your answer: the pressure was never only in your child. It was in the air at home. And the air is the one thing you control completely.

## What to stop saying — and what to say instead

Most pressure at this age does not come from the exam. It comes from sentences adults say without thinking.

- Instead of **"Only 70 days left"**, say **"Seventy days is plenty for what we have planned."** The number is the same. The feeling is the opposite.
- Instead of **"Your cousin scored 280"**, say nothing about the cousin at all. Praise effort, not rank — counsellors are unanimous on this one, because comparison is the fastest way to make a child stop trying.
- Instead of **"If you don't clear this…"**, say **"Whatever happens on 31 January, we will have a plan."** A child who believes there is life after the exam performs better in it. That is not softness; it is strategy.
- Instead of **"Why did you get this wrong?"**, say **"Show me how you thought about this one."** The first question punishes. The second teaches.

And one more, the hardest one: do not let your own anxiety become theirs. Children read their parents' faces better than any mock-test report. If you are lying awake worrying about the form, the fee, the centre — deal with it away from your child. Your calm is their calm.

## The last word: the exam is 150 minutes, not a life sentence

AISSEE Class 6 is a 150-minute paper, 125 questions, 300 marks, no negative marking. A wrong answer costs nothing. A blank costs nothing. That is the whole design — the exam itself is kinder than the fear around it. Remind your child of that, once, the week of the exam, and then stop reminding them.

## FAQs

**What are the signs of exam stress in an 11-year-old?**
A: At 11, stress rarely arrives as words. Watch for sleep trouble, stomach aches or headaches with no medical cause, tears over small things, avoiding study without explanation, and sudden loss of appetite. Behaviour is the signal — listen to it before you lecture about it.

**How can I calm my child down the night before a mock test?**
A: Keep the evening normal: a light dinner, no new topics, a 10-minute revision of something easy they already know, and bed on time. Two minutes of slow breathing — in for 4 counts, hold for 4, out for 4 — works better than a pep talk at this age.

**Should I stop my child from studying when they seem stressed?**
A: No — stopping study entirely usually raises anxiety, because the syllabus then feels even further away. Shorten the session instead, switch to an easy revision topic, and add a real break. Structure calms children more than avoidance does.

**Is it normal for my child to cry before the AISSEE exam?**
A: Yes — tears before a big exam are normal at this age and do not predict failure. Stay calm, listen without fixing, remind them of one thing they do well, and get them to the exam centre early so nothing feels rushed.

**How many hours should an 11-year-old study daily in the last 70 days?**
A: There is no official prescription, but counsellors generally suggest 2 to 3 focused hours on school days for Class 6 aspirants, in 40 to 50 minute blocks with real breaks — plus play, sleep and family time. Three sharp hours beat ten exhausted ones.

**When should I seek professional help for my child's exam anxiety?**
A: If anxiety brings frequent headaches or stomach pain, persistent sleep disturbance, withdrawal from friends and family, or panic symptoms, talk to a school counsellor or a child psychologist early. Early support prevents long-term distress — it is not an overreaction.
