# AISSEE 2027 Final Months Strategy: September to January (Class 6 & 9)

> **🟢 Last verified: 26 September 2026** — ✅ **CONFIRMED** = from official source. 🔶 **EXPECTED** = pattern-based projection.

Roughly four months to go. The AISSEE 2027 exam is on **31 January 2027** (✅ confirmed tentative, NTA calendar), and if you're reading this in late September, your child has about 120 days. That's not a crisis — it's actually a very workable window — but only if you stop studying "everything" and start studying strategically. The parents whose children clear this exam aren't the ones with the biggest stack of guidebooks. They're the ones with a plan that survives contact with real life: school, homework, festivals, exams, and all.

## The Big Picture: How the Remaining Time Splits Up

| Phase | Period | Focus |
|-------|--------|-------|
| Phase 1 — Consolidate | Late Sept → Oct | Finish syllabus, strengthen weak topics |
| Phase 2 — Practise | Nov → Dec | Full-length mocks, speed building, OMR practice |
| Phase 3 — Sharpen | Early → mid Jan | Revision only, light mocks, exam temperament |
| Exam week | 24–31 Jan | Admit card, logistics, rest — no new topics |

Here's the thing most families get wrong: they treat all four months the same way. September should look very different from December, which should look very different from the last two weeks of January. Let me break each phase down properly.

## Phase 1 (Now – October): Finish the Syllabus, Fix the Weak Spots

This is the last month where "learning new things" is the priority. After October, you're practising, not learning.

**Class 6 priorities:** Maths is half the paper — 50 of 125 questions, 150 of 300 marks (✅ 2026 pattern, expected repeat). That's not a suggestion to focus on Maths, it's arithmetic. Within Maths, the highest-yield topics are fractions and decimals, percentages, ratio, simple mensuration, and basic geometry. For Language: reading comprehension and grammar rules (tenses, articles, prepositions). Intelligence is pattern-based — series, coding-decoding, odd-one-out — and it's the most trainable section in a short time. GK should be current-affairs-light and NCERT-heavy: capitals, rivers, national symbols, basic science facts.

**Class 9 priorities:** Same Maths-first logic — 50 questions for 200 of 400 marks (✅ 2026 pattern). Algebra, geometry, and mensuration dominate. English: grammar + unseen passages. General Science: NCERT Class 8 level, heavy on physics basics and life processes. Social Science: history and civics chapters from NCERT Class 8.

A scene I keep replaying: a mother told us last November that her son had "finished the syllabus" — but when we asked him to solve ten fraction problems, he got four wrong. Finishing isn't the same as knowing. Phase 1's real job is honest diagnosis: for every chapter, your child should be able to say "I can solve these" — not "I've read these."

**Daily routine for Phase 1** (adjust around school):
- 1.5–2 hours: one subject deep-dive, weak topics first
- 30 minutes: daily Maths practice — non-negotiable, every single day
- 20 minutes: GK/current affairs reading (a newspaper page, or one GK chapter)
- Sunday: one topic-wise test + error notebook review

## Phase 2 (November – December): Mock Tests Become the Syllabus

Honestly, most guidebooks overcomplicate this phase. The rule is simple: from November, your child should solve **one full-length paper every Sunday** under real conditions — 150 minutes for Class 6, 180 for Class 9, phone in another room, an OMR sheet if you can print one. No pauses. No "let me just check this formula."

Then the crucial part most people skip: **spend Monday analysing it.** Not glancing at the score — analysing. For every wrong answer, write down *why* it went wrong: concept gap, silly mistake, or time pressure. Keep an error notebook. By December, this notebook is more valuable than any guidebook, because it's a personalised map of exactly what your child gets wrong.

**Speed targets** (Class 6, 150 minutes for 125 questions): about 1 minute per question, leaving 20–25 minutes for review. Class 9: about 1 minute per question too, leaving ~30 minutes spare. If your child can't finish a paper in time by mid-December, the fix isn't more studying — it's attempting strategy: easy questions first, Maths last (it's the heaviest but also the slowest), and never spending more than 2 minutes stuck on one question. Remember, **there's no negative marking** (✅ 2026 pattern) — so in the last 10 minutes, attempt everything, even by educated guess.

**December specifics:** Two mocks per week now (Sunday + Wednesday evening). Shorten revision loops — revise the error notebook twice a week. And don't start new reference books in December. It sounds obvious, but every year parents panic-buy in December. The books don't matter anymore. The papers do.

## Phase 3 (January): Stop Learning, Start Peaking

The first three weeks of January have one job: keep your child sharp and calm.

- No new topics. None. If a topic isn't done by January 1, it stays undone — the return on new learning in January is worse than the return on consolidating what's already known.
- One mock per week, lighter analysis. The goal is rhythm, not discovery.
- Daily: 30 minutes of formula/grammar/GK rapid revision. Flashcards work well here.
- Sleep matters more than study now. An 11-year-old running on 6 hours of sleep in January will underperform their own ability by a visible margin. Protect the sleep schedule like it's part of the syllabus — because it is.

**Exam week (24–31 January):** Download the admit card the day it releases (expected mid-to-late January 🔶), visit the exam centre if possible, pack the exam-day bag the night before. Two light revision sessions max. No full mocks in the final 3–4 days — they only create anxiety now.

## The Daily Timetable That Actually Works

| Slot | Duration | Activity |
|------|----------|----------|
| Morning (before/after school) | 30–40 min | Maths practice set |
| Evening block 1 | 60–75 min | Main subject of the day (rotate) |
| Evening block 2 | 30 min | Second subject / error notebook |
| Night | 15–20 min | GK + formula revision |

Total: roughly 2.5 hours on school days, 4–5 on Sundays/holidays. That's enough. More than this for a 10-year-old leads to burnout, and a burnt-out child in December is worse than a slightly under-prepared one.

## Three Opinions You Won't Find in Guidebooks

1. **Skip the expensive crash course.** At this stage, structured self-practice beats any 30-day crash course. The money is better spent on printed mock papers and a quiet study corner.
2. **Parents should track effort, not marks.** Asking "how many did you get right?" after every mock teaches your child to fear the score. Ask "what did you learn from the mistakes?" instead. The marks follow.
3. **Protect one rest day a week.** No studying on that day. A rested brain consolidates; a tired one just accumulates fatigue. This isn't softness, it's strategy.

## FAQ — Final Months Preparation

**Is four months enough for AISSEE 2027 preparation?**
Yes — if the syllabus is mostly covered and the remaining time goes into practice and revision. If your child is starting from zero in late September, it's tight but doable for Class 6 with a strict daily routine; prioritise Maths and Intelligence, which are the most scoring.

**How many mock tests should my child solve before 31 January?**
Aim for 15–20 full-length papers between November and mid-January. Quality of analysis matters more than quantity — one mock properly reviewed beats three rushed ones.

**Which subject should get the most time?**
Maths, for both classes — it's 50% of the Class 6 paper and 50% of the Class 9 paper by marks. After Maths, Class 6 students should prioritise Intelligence (highly trainable), and Class 9 students should prioritise General Science.

**My child makes silly mistakes in every mock. What do I do?**
That's exactly what the error notebook is for. Most "silly mistakes" are actually patterns — misreading questions, calculation slips, OMR bubbling errors. Once your child sees their own pattern written down, they start self-correcting. Budget 5 minutes at the end of every paper for a deliberate re-check.

**Should we stop school studies to focus on AISSEE?**
No. The AISSEE syllabus overlaps heavily with NCERT anyway, and dropping school creates stress without adding much study time. Two to three focused hours a day is plenty alongside school.

**What should the last week look like?**
Light revision only. Admit card downloaded and checked, centre located, bag packed the night before. No new topics, no full mocks in the last 3–4 days, normal sleep. Boring is the goal.
