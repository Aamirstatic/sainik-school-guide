There are three phases in any AISSEE campaign. The long build (months of syllabus), the 60-day sprint, and the 30-day revision taper. Parents obsess over the first and panic through the third — but it is the middle phase, the sprint, that decides how many of the marks a child *knows* actually land on the OMR sheet.

A sprint is not "study harder." It is study *differently*: syllabus completion stops being the goal, and mark conversion becomes the goal. If the exam is held on 31 January 2027 as expected, day 60 falls around **2 December 2026** — which means December and January are sprint months. Here is how to run them.

## The 5 ground rules of the sprint

1. **No new chapters after day 40.** Anything not started by then gets, at most, a formula-sheet glance. New chapters in the final weeks cost more marks in confusion than they ever add.
2. **Every study session is timed.** The Class 6 paper allows 72 seconds per question; Class 9 allows the same. If practice isn't timed, it isn't sprint practice.
3. **Mocks are sacred — and reviewed, not just taken.** A mock without a same-day error-log review is entertainment, not preparation.
4. **Protect the sectional cutoff.** You need roughly 25% in *every* section and 40% overall. A sprint that ignores the weakest section is a sprint that fails at the cutoff.
5. **Sleep is part of the plan.** A tired 11-year-old makes bubbling errors that cost real seats. Fixed sleep timings start from day 60, not the night before the exam.

## The week-by-week plan

The table below assumes roughly 2.5–3 hours of AISSEE work daily on school days and 4–5 hours on weekends — realistic for a Class 5/8 child, not a fantasy timetable.

| Week (days left) | Theme | Daily focus | Mock cadence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1–2 (60–47) | Close the syllabus | Finish remaining chapters; one diagnostic full mock to map weak topics | 1 full mock (diagnostic) |
| 3–4 (46–33) | Topic sprints | 2 weak topics repaired per week; section-wise timed sets (Maths 60 min / others 30 min) | 1 full mock per week |
| 5–6 (32–19) | Exam simulation | Full papers at 10:00 am (actual exam slot); error-log drills on repeat mistakes | 2 full mocks per week |
| 7 (18–12) | Weakness surgery | Only error-log topics; formula and fact sheets; one Language/GK booster round | 2 full mocks |
| 8 (11–0) | Taper | No new material; light revision; sleep discipline; exam-day rehearsal | 1 easy mock, mid-week |

Notice what increases and what decreases: mocks go *up* from week 5, while new learning goes *down* to zero by week 7. That crossover is the entire philosophy of the sprint.

## Class 6 vs Class 9: where the sprint differs

The structure is identical; the emphasis is not.

- **Class 6:** Mathematics is 150 of 300 marks — half the paper. Roughly 50% of sprint time belongs to Maths: speed drills, the 60-minute section budget, and OMR bubbling practice. The other three sections (Language, Intelligence, GK) share the rest, with GK getting a weekly current-affairs top-up.
- **Class 9:** Five sections and a longer paper (180 minutes, 400 marks). The sprint must protect **English** deliberately — the Class 9 paper is English-only, and children from Hindi-medium backgrounds often discover this gap too late. Split sprint time across all five sections in proportion to marks, and never let Maths (200 marks) cannibalise the other four.

## The error log: the sprint's real engine

Buy a thin notebook. This is the most valuable study material your child will own in December. After every mock or timed set, log each wrong or guessed question in this format:

- [ ] **Question source** (mock name + question number) — so it can be revisited
- [ ] **What went wrong** — concept gap, silly error, time pressure, or OMR mistake
- [ ] **The correct method in one line** — not the full solution, just the trigger ("LCM of denominators first")
- [ ] **Revisit date** — exactly 7 days later

**The weekly error-log ritual (30 minutes, every Sunday):** re-attempt every logged question from the past 7 days without looking at the correction. Any question missed twice gets a star — starred questions are the *only* syllabus the child revises in week 7. This single habit typically recovers 15–25 marks that would otherwise leak on exam day, because AISSEE rewards the elimination of repeat errors more than the accumulation of new tricks.

## The taper week (days 7–0): what to do and what to avoid

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| One easy full mock, mid-week, at 10:00 am | Take a brutal mock 2 days before the exam |
| Revise formula sheets and starred error-log items | Open any new chapter or book |
| Fix sleep: in bed by 10 pm, up by 6:30 am | Change the routine — new foods, new schedules |
| Pack admit card, photos, stationery the night before | Leave logistics for exam morning |
| Light physical activity daily | Cram until midnight |

The taper week's job is to deliver a fresh, confident child to the exam hall. Marks are not made in the last 7 days; they are *protected* there.

## FAQs

**When should the 60-day AISSEE sprint start for AISSEE 2027?**
If the exam is held on 31 January 2027 as the NTA calendar indicates, day 60 lands around 2 December 2026. Start the sprint then, with your syllabus at least 80 percent complete.

**How many mock tests should a child take in the last 60 days?**
A realistic cadence is 1 full mock per week for the first 4 weeks, 2 per week for weeks 5 to 7, and 1 easy mock in the final taper week, for a total of 12 to 13 full mocks. Each mock must be followed by a full error-log review.

**Should new topics be started during the 60-day sprint?**
No new chapters after day 40. The sprint is for converting known syllabus into marks through timed practice, weak-topic repair and exam temperament, not for opening fresh chapters.

**What is the taper week before AISSEE?**
The final 7 days: no new topics, no difficult mocks, one easy full paper at the actual exam time, light revision of formula sheets and error logs, and fixed sleep timings. The goal is to arrive fresh, not exhausted.

**Is the 60-day sprint the same for Class 6 and Class 9?**
The structure is the same but the focus differs. Class 6 sprints should overweight Mathematics (150 of 300 marks) and speed, while Class 9 sprints must balance five sections and protect the English section, since the Class 9 paper is English-only.
