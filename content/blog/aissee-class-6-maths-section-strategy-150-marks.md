## The section, in numbers

Here is the thing most parents miss: the AISSEE Class 6 paper is not four equal sections. It is one big section and three small ones.

| Section | Questions | Marks each | Total marks |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mathematics** | **50** | **3** | **150** |
| Language | 25 | 2 | 50 |
| Intelligence | 25 | 2 | 50 |
| General Knowledge | 25 | 2 | 50 |
| **Total** | **125** | — | **300** |

Maths is half the paper. Half. A child who scores 120 in Maths and 100 across the other three sections has 220 out of 300 — a strong score. A child who scores 70 in Maths needs near-perfect marks everywhere else just to catch up, and that almost never happens. This is why the section deserves half of every study day, and it is why this guide exists: the [Language section strategy we published yesterday](/blog/aissee-class-6-language-section-strategy-50-marks/) matters, but Language is 50 marks. Maths is three times that.

The rest of the pattern is straightforward. The exam is on **Sunday, 31 January 2027, 2:00 PM to 4:30 PM** — 150 minutes, pen-and-paper, OMR sheet, multiple-choice questions, **no negative marking**. Your child can attempt the paper in any of 13 languages, including English and Hindi, and the medium cannot be changed after the form is submitted. And there is a floor: under the 2026 pattern, expected to repeat, a candidate needs at least **25% in each section** — that is 37.5 of the 150 Maths marks — and **40% in aggregate**, 120 of 300. The floor does not apply to SC/ST candidates. Notice what that floor means in practice: a child cannot "skip" Maths and pass on the other sections. The paper will not let them.

## Where the 150 marks actually come from

The Maths section is drawn from the **Class 5 NCERT maths syllabus** — not Class 6, and not some secret list. That is genuinely good news, because it means your child already owns the material. The questions cluster into five familiar areas:

- **Numbers and operations** — large numbers, factors and multiples, HCF and LCM, and the four operations done fast and accurately.
- **Fractions and decimals** — comparing, adding and subtracting fractions, converting between fractions and decimals, and simple percentage problems.
- **Geometry and mensuration** — perimeter and area of rectangles and squares, angles, triangles, and the properties of simple shapes.
- **Measurement** — length, weight, capacity, time and money, including unit conversion and the "how much is left" style of word problem.
- **Data handling** — reading tables, bar charts and pictographs, and simple averages.

Here is the honest part. The questions are rarely hard. What makes this section brutal is that it is long — 50 questions — and each one is worth 3 marks, so a single careless slip costs as much as three Language questions. The children who lose Maths marks almost never lose them to a topic they never studied. They lose them to a misread number, a decimal point in the wrong place, or a perimeter calculated as an area. Which is why the rest of this guide is about habits, not chapters.

## The 60-minute split: how to spend the exam

Our [paper attempt strategy](/blog/aissee-2027-paper-attempt-strategy-150-180-minute-plan/) covers the full 150 minutes. For Maths specifically, here is the split that works for most children:

| Block | Minutes | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| First pass | 40 | Attempt every Maths question you can solve in under a minute. Circle the rest and move on — do not wrestle. |
| Second pass | 15 | Return to the circled questions. This is where the marks you almost had get collected. |
| OMR + check | 5 | Bubble check: every answer on the sheet, no skipped rows, no double bubbles. |

That adds up to 60 minutes for Maths, leaving about 25 minutes each for Language and Intelligence, 20 for GK, and 15 for a final review. The first-pass rule is the whole game: a child who spends four minutes on one stubborn question has quietly stolen the time for three easy ones. Teach your child to circle and move on. The paper rewards the child who collects the easy 3-mark questions first.

One more timing point, and it matters: there is no negative marking, so in the last two minutes of the Maths block, your child should answer every remaining question — even by guessing. A guess costs nothing. A blank scores nothing. Make sure they know that before exam day, not during it.

## Five habits that build Maths speed

Speed in this section is not talent. It is repetition, and it is built at home in small daily doses:

1. **One timed 50-question set every week.** Sit it like the real thing — 60 minutes, OMR sheet if you have one, phone in another room. The point is not the score; it is the clock.
2. **Two short drills a week, 15–20 questions each.** One drill on fractions and decimals, one on area and perimeter, alternating with number work. Short and frequent beats long and rare.
3. **The error notebook.** Every wrong answer goes in one notebook with the correct method written beside it. In the final weeks, that notebook is the revision — not a new guidebook.
4. **Mental maths at the dinner table.** Tables to 20, squares to 15, doubling and halving — two minutes a day, out loud. Children who calculate in their heads do not waste exam time on rough work.
5. **Read the question twice.** Half of all "silly mistakes" are misread questions — perimeter asked, area calculated; litres asked, millilitres given. The second read takes three seconds and saves 3 marks.

## Two worked examples — try these tonight

Sit with your child and try these. They are the exact style the section uses: simple ideas, careful reading.

**Example 1 — fractions.** *A shopkeeper has 250 kg of rice. He sells 3/5 of it in the morning. How much rice is left?*
3/5 of 250 kg is 150 kg sold. So 250 − 150 = **100 kg left**. The trap here is stopping at 150 — the question asks what is *left*, and children who rush answer 150. Read it twice.

**Example 2 — area and fencing.** *A rectangular garden is 25 m long and 16 m wide. Find its area, and the cost of fencing it at ₹40 per metre.*
Area = 25 × 16 = **400 square metres**. Perimeter = 2 × (25 + 16) = 82 m. Fencing cost = 82 × 40 = **₹3,280**. Two formulas, one question — and the child who confuses area with perimeter gets both parts wrong. ## Three mistakes that cost these 150 marks

- **Starting with the hardest question.** The paper is not ordered by difficulty. A child who begins at question 1 and fights it for five minutes has already lost the rhythm. First pass: easy ones first.
- **Rough work on the question paper, answers on the OMR — without checking.** The OMR is the answer. One skipped row shifts every answer after it. The last five minutes exist for this check, and only for this check.
- **Leaving questions blank.** No negative marking means a blank is the only guaranteed zero. Guess. Every time.

## A weekly rhythm, not a dated plan

Our [11-week plan](/blog/aissee-2027-last-11-weeks-week-by-week-plan-2026-11-16/) gives the dated schedule; for Maths, the rhythm inside each week is what matters: one timed 50-question set on Sunday morning, two short drills on weekdays, and one error-notebook review on Saturday evening. That is roughly four hours a week for 150 marks — the best return on study time in the whole paper. Keep it going until the last week, then drop the timed sets and keep only the error notebook and the formula sheet. In the final three days, confidence matters more than new practice.

## FAQs

**How many marks is Maths in AISSEE Class 6?**

Maths is 50 questions for 150 marks — half of the 300-mark paper. Each question carries 3 marks. The other three sections (Language, Intelligence, GK) are 25 questions for 50 marks each.

**How much time should my child spend on Maths in the AISSEE Class 6 exam?**

About 60 minutes of the 150-minute paper. That leaves roughly 25 minutes each for Language and Intelligence, 20 minutes for GK, and 15 minutes for a final review and OMR check.

**What is the minimum score needed in Maths to qualify?**

Under the 2026 bulletin pattern, expected to repeat in 2027, a candidate needs at least 25 percent in each section — 37.5 of the 150 Maths marks — and 40 percent (120 of 300) in aggregate. The rule does not apply to SC/ST candidates.

**Is there negative marking in AISSEE 2027?**

No — wrong answers and blanks both score zero; nothing is deducted. Your child should attempt every Maths question, even the ones they are unsure about.

**Which topics matter most in AISSEE Class 6 Maths?**

The section is drawn from the Class 5 NCERT maths syllabus: numbers and operations, fractions and decimals, geometry and mensuration, measurement, and data handling. Fractions, percentage, and area-perimeter questions appear year after year.

**How many Maths questions should my child practise each week?**

One timed 50-question set every week, plus two short topic drills of 15–20 questions. Review every wrong answer the same day and write the mistake in one error notebook — that notebook is worth more than a new guidebook in the final weeks.
