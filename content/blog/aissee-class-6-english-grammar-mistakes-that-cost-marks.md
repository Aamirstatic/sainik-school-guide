## Why English grammar is a quiet marks leak

Parents worry most about maths in AISSEE, and understandably so — it carries 150 of the 300 marks. But English is where prepared children quietly bleed marks they should not lose. The Language section is 25 questions worth 50 marks, and unlike maths, its questions rarely feel hard. A child reads a sentence, it sounds roughly right, and they move on. That "roughly right" is the trap.

Grammar questions in AISSEE Class 6 do not test whether a child knows the names of tenses. They test whether the child notices small breaks: a plural verb with a singular subject, a past-tense verb in a present-tense paragraph, the wrong article before a vowel sound. Each one costs 2 marks, and because there is no negative marking, the real loss is not a penalty — it is the easy marks left on the table while other children collect them.

The good news: the mistakes repeat. Year after year, the same half-dozen error types show up in error-spotting, sentence correction and fill-in-the-blanks. Fix those, and the English section stops leaking.

## The six mistakes that cost the most marks

| Mistake | What it looks like | Why children make it | The fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| Subject-verb agreement | "The group of students are going" | The noun nearest the verb (students) feels like the subject; the real subject is "group" | Find the true subject first, ignore phrases between subject and verb |
| Tense switching | "He went to school and eats lunch" | Children write what sounds natural in speech, mixing past and present | One paragraph, one time frame — unless a time word says otherwise |
| Articles (a / an / the) | "An university", "a hour" | Children apply the vowel-letter rule instead of the vowel-sound rule | Say the word aloud: vowel sound takes "an" (an hour, an honest boy) |
| Prepositions | "Good in maths", "married with her" | Direct translation from Hindi or the home language | Learn prepositions as fixed phrases, not single words: good at, married to, afraid of |
| Homophones | "Their going to school", "its raining" | Spelling by sound, reading too fast | One flashcard per pair, with a sentence each: their = belonging, there = place, they're = they are |
| Punctuation and capitalisation | Missing commas, "i" instead of "I" | Rushed writing, no proofreading habit | Read the sentence aloud once; if you pause, a comma probably belongs there |

## Mistake 1: subject-verb agreement — the biggest single leak

This is the error that hides in plain sight. The sentence "The basket of mangoes are heavy" sounds fine to most Class 5 and 6 children, because "mangoes" sits right next to "are". But the subject is "basket", singular, so the verb must be "is".

The drill that fixes it is boring and it works. Take any sentence, cross out every phrase between the subject and the verb, and read what remains. "The basket (of mangoes) is heavy." Do this ten times a day for two weeks and the habit becomes automatic. In the exam, the same trick works inside error-spotting questions: find the subject, ignore the decoration, check the verb.

Watch out for the classic traps: "each", "every", "either" and "neither" take singular verbs ("Each of the boys is ready"), while "and" joining two subjects takes a plural verb ("Rahul and Priya are friends"). Collective nouns like "team", "class" and "family" usually take singular verbs in AISSEE-level English.

## Mistake 2: tense switching inside one sentence

Children rarely get a tense question wrong when it stands alone. They get it wrong when a sentence mixes times: "Yesterday he goes to the market and bought apples." The first verb should be "went". The fix is a simple question to ask before answering: when is this happening? If the sentence starts in the past, every verb stays in the past unless a word like "now" or "every day" changes the frame.

A useful practice: give your child a short paragraph in the present tense and ask them to rewrite it entirely in the past tense, then back again. Ten minutes, twice a week, and tense consistency stops being a guess.

## Mistake 3: articles — the sound rule, not the letter rule

"An university" and "a hour" are the two most common article errors, and both come from the same misunderstanding. The rule is not about vowels as letters; it is about vowel sounds. "University" starts with a "yoo" sound, a consonant sound, so it takes "a". "Hour" starts with a vowel sound because the "h" is silent, so it takes "an". Same for "an honest man" and "a one-rupee coin".

The fastest fix is saying the word out loud before choosing. If the mouth opens on a vowel sound, write "an". This one habit alone saves marks in fill-in-the-blanks and error-spotting alike.

## Mistake 4: prepositions translated from the home language

Many preposition errors are not grammar errors at all — they are translation errors. A child thinking in Hindi writes "good in maths" because the Hindi construction works that way; English wants "good at maths". Similarly, "married with" should be "married to", "afraid from" should be "afraid of", and "discuss about" should be just "discuss".

The only reliable fix is learning prepositions inside phrases, never alone. Flashcards should say "good at", "interested in", "afraid of", "proud of" — the verb plus its preposition as one unit. Twenty such pairs, revised weekly, cover most of what AISSEE asks.

## Mistake 5: homophones and the apostrophe trap

"There", "their" and "they're" sound identical, so children pick whichever spelling comes to mind first. The same happens with "its" and "it's", "your" and "you're", "to", "too" and "two". AISSEE loves these because they separate careful readers from fast readers.

The fix is meaning, not spelling. "Their" shows belonging (their books), "there" shows place (over there), "they're" is short for "they are". "It's" always means "it is" — if you cannot expand it to "it is", write "its". Teach one pair a day, with the child writing one sentence for each spelling, and the confusion fades within a fortnight.

## Mistake 6: punctuation under time pressure

Punctuation errors rarely appear as standalone questions, but they cost marks indirectly: a child who never proofreads misses the comma that changes a sentence's meaning, or writes "i" in a hurry and loses the benefit of an otherwise correct answer in sentence-correction items. The habit to build is a final 30-second read of every English answer. Read it aloud in your head; wherever you naturally pause, check for a comma.

## A 4-week grammar repair plan

| Week | Focus | Daily drill (20–30 minutes) |
|---|---|---|
| Week 1 | Subject-verb agreement | 10 error-spotting questions; cross out the phrase between subject and verb |
| Week 2 | Tenses + articles | Rewrite one paragraph in a different tense; 10 article fill-in-the-blanks, saying each word aloud |
| Week 3 | Prepositions + homophones | 10 phrase flashcards; one sentence each for a homophone pair |
| Week 4 | Mixed revision + mock | One 25-question English mock, timed at 25 minutes; review every wrong answer the same day |

The plan works because it is narrow. A child who tries to revise "all of grammar" revises none of it. A child who spends one week on one error type can actually feel the improvement — and feeling it is what keeps a ten-year-old practising.

## What not to do

Do not buy a thick grammar book and start at page one. AISSEE English is not a test of grammar terminology; a child who can recite the definition of a participle but cannot spot "their" misused will still lose marks. Do not drill only from guides either — the best error-spotting practice is your child's own writing. Take last week's school essay, circle every grammar slip together, and fix them. That is the exam, in miniature.

And do not panic about English at the cost of maths. English is 50 marks; maths is 150. The point of fixing grammar is not to make English the biggest subject — it is to stop it being the subject where easy marks quietly disappear. ## FAQs

**How many marks is the AISSEE Class 6 English section worth?**

Under the AISSEE 2026 pattern, the Language section carries 25 questions of 2 marks each, totalling 50 marks out of the 300-mark Class 6 paper. The 2027 pattern will be confirmed in the official bulletin, so treat this as the working pattern, not a 2027 guarantee.

**Is there negative marking in AISSEE?**

No. Wrong answers and blank answers both score zero, and nothing is deducted. That means every question is worth attempting, even by elimination.

**Which grammar topic loses the most marks in AISSEE Class 6 English?**

Subject-verb agreement and tense consistency, because they appear in several question types at once — error spotting, sentence correction and fill-in-the-blanks. Articles and prepositions are close behind, since they test feel for English rather than memorised rules.

**How much time should a Class 6 student spend on English grammar daily?**

Twenty to thirty minutes of focused practice, five days a week, beats a two-hour weekend session. Short daily drills — ten error-spotting questions and one short paragraph to correct — build the pattern recognition the exam rewards.

**Does reading storybooks actually improve AISSEE English scores?**

Yes, but slowly. Reading builds the instinct for correct articles, prepositions and word order that grammar rules alone cannot. For exam marks in the next few months, combine reading with targeted error-spotting drills rather than relying on reading alone.

**Are spelling mistakes tested in AISSEE English?**

Yes, through correctly-spelt and incorrectly-spelt word questions and through error-spotting items. Homophones such as their, there and they are a favourite, because they catch students who read fast and spell by sound.
