## The good news first

Two children who both want Sainik School is, honestly, the easier version of this problem. You are not dragging a reluctant child to the study table — you have two volunteers. They can quiz each other, race through mental maths, and keep each other honest on days when motivation dips. Many single-aspirant families would trade for that.

But two aspirants also double everything that is hard about AISSEE preparation: two timetables fighting for one parent's evening, two mock-test scores landing on the same kitchen table, and one risk that single-child families never face — that the children stop competing with the exam and start competing with each other.

## First, the facts that settle half the arguments

Before any planning, get these straight with both children in the room:

- **Each child applies separately.** AISSEE takes one application per candidate, for that child's own class — Class 6 or Class 9. One child's form, fee and admit card have nothing to do with the other's.
- **Each child's result stands alone.** There is no family aggregate, no combined merit, and no rule that both must clear together.
- **There is no sibling quota.** Having a brother or sister already in a Sainik School, or both applying together, gives no preference in admission. Selection runs on the written exam, qualifying marks and counselling merit.
- **They may not even be chasing the same exam.** A Class 5 child and a Class 8 child are preparing for different papers, different syllabuses and different competition pools. Comparing their mock scores directly is meaningless — and children will do it anyway unless you say so first.

Say all four out loud, once, at the start. It removes the fantasy that one child's success can carry the other, and the fear that one child's failure will drag the other down.

## The one rule that governs everything

**Never rank your children against each other.** Not in scores, not in study hours, not in praise. The moment "your brother got 20 more marks" enters the house, preparation stops being about the exam and starts being about beating a sibling — and the child who falls behind stops trying rather than trying harder.

What you compare instead: each child against their own last mock. "You are up 14 marks on your own September paper" is fuel. "You are behind your sister" is poison.

## The timetable problem, solved practically

Two children, one parent, one evening. The usual failure mode is splitting the evening exactly in half and calling it fair — then watching the child who needed help with fractions sit stuck for their whole half while the other child breezes through.| Situation | What actually works |
|---|---|
| Both need you at the same time | Stagger by subject: one child does self-practice (worksheets, reading) while you sit with the other, then swap after 40 minutes |
| One child is much weaker in a subject | Give the weaker subject more of your evening, and tell both children why — need-based help is fairer than equal minutes, and children accept it when it is explained |
| Mock-test Sundays | Never test both children at the same table at the same time; one tests while the other reads or plays, then swap — it stops mid-test score peeking and post-test arguments |
| Different classes (Class 6 and Class 9) | Their papers barely overlap, so treat them as two separate campaigns that happen to share a house, not one campaign with two soldiers |
| Only one study room | Split by time, not by space: early slot for the younger child, late slot for the older one, with the room's door as the boundary |

The principle underneath the table: **fairness means each child gets what they need, not that each child gets the same.** Children understand this far better than adults expect — provided you explain it before the first unfair-looking evening, not during it.## Study together, test apart

Here is the split that works in most two-aspirant houses:

**Together:** morning revision, vocabulary games, map work, current-affairs discussion, and reading aloud. Shared study is where siblings genuinely help each other — the older child explaining a concept learns it twice.

**Apart:** timed mock tests, weak-subject drills, and anything scored. Scored work done side by side becomes a race, and races produce one winner and one child who quietly decides the exam is not for them. Keep scores private between you and each child.

## Money: where two aspirants genuinely save, and where they don't

AISSEE preparation for two children is not double the cost of one, but it is not half either:

- **Genuinely shared:** guidebooks and reference books, a question bank, stationery, and the car trip to the exam centre.
- **Per child, no saving:** application forms, coaching fees if you use them, and any per-student test series.
- **The hidden cost:** your time. Two children need two sets of answer-checking, two error logs, two weak-subject plans. If you are the one doing all of it, that is the real budget to watch — not money.

One practical tip: buy one good book set, not two. Children sharing books learn to negotiate and schedule; children with identical separate sets learn to compare whose book is newer.## The conversation to have before results

This is the most important section of this article, and the one most families skip. Before either result comes out, sit both children down and agree on three things:1. **Whatever happens, it happens to each child separately.** One clearing and one missing does not make one a success and one a failure — it makes two different results.
2. **The successful child's stories stay at school.** Once one child is in a Sainik School, their letters and holiday stories will fascinate the other — and sting, if they arrive as daily reminders. Let the child at home ask about school life, rather than having it narrated at them.
3. **The other child's next goal is ready before results, not invented after them.** Whether that is another attempt next year (if age allows), a different school, or a fresh target entirely, having it ready turns a bad result day into a redirection, not a dead end.

Families that have this conversation early almost never need it on result day.

## A final word for the evening this is published in

If both your children want Sainik School, you already have the hardest ingredient: willingness. Your job is not to manufacture motivation — it is to protect it from the one thing that kills it fastest, which is being measured against a sibling in your own home. Keep their campaigns separate, their scores private, and their progress measured against their own past selves.

## FAQs

**Can two siblings both apply for AISSEE in the same year?**

Yes. Each child submits a separate application and sits the exam for their own class — Class 6 or Class 9. One child's result has no effect on the other's.

**Should siblings study together or separately for AISSEE?**

Both, at different times: together for revision games, quizzes and reading aloud, and separately for timed practice and weak-subject work, where comparison creeps in fastest.

**How do you split study time fairly between two AISSEE aspirants?**

Split adult attention by need, not by the clock: the child with the weaker subject gets more of your evening, and say so openly, so equal minutes never become the argument.

**What if one sibling clears AISSEE and the other does not?**

Prepare for that outcome before results, not after: separate celebrations from consolation, never let the successful child's school stories become a daily comparison, and keep the other child's next goal ready.

**Does having a sibling in Sainik School help the second child's admission?**

No. AISSEE admission is decided by the written exam, qualifying marks and counselling merit. There is no sibling quota or preference in the published admission rules.

**Is it cheaper to prepare two children for AISSEE together?**

Some costs genuinely halve — one set of guidebooks, one mock-test subscription shared for practice, one trip to the exam centre — but coaching fees, forms and travel per child do not.
