There is a particular silence that falls over a house when one child is preparing for AISSEE and the other is not. The older one gets the new study lamp, the extra hour of your evening, the hushed conversations about mock-test scores. The younger one gets told to play quietly — again — and starts keeping score in a way no marksheet ever will.

This is not a small problem. In counselling conversations with defence-aspirant families, the sibling dynamic comes up almost as often as the syllabus does. Children do not measure love in rupees; they measure it in attention. And an exam season visibly moves attention from one child to another. Handle it well, and the non-preparing sibling becomes the family's best supporter. Handle it badly, and you win an exam while losing something at home that no rank can buy back.

## Why comparisons wound more than they motivate

Every parent has said it at least once: "Look at your brother, he studies for three hours." It feels like motivation. To the child hearing it, it lands as a verdict — *you are the lesser one*.

Comparison does two kinds of damage. First, it ties the child's worth to someone else's performance, which they cannot control. Second, it quietly pits the siblings against each other: every praise of one becomes a small demotion of the other. Over an AISSEE season — four to six months of preparation — that steady drip can corrode a relationship that has to last a lifetime.

The rule is simple and non-negotiable: **compare each child only to their own yesterday.** "You finished your homework without being reminded today" builds a child. "Why can't you be like your sister" shrinks one.

## The sibling fairness audit

Fairness does not mean identical treatment — the AISSEE child genuinely needs more study hours right now. It means no child feels like an afterthought. Run this audit honestly:

| Situation in your home | The risk | The fair alternative |
|---|---|---|
| All evening conversation is about mock tests and scores | The other child learns their life is not newsworthy | Dinner-table rule: ten minutes of AISSEE talk, then everyone shares something |
| The younger one is shooed away every evening | They start resenting the exam, not just the noise | Fixed quiet hours on a visible timetable — outside those hours, the house is normal |
| New books, new lamp, new bag for one child | The other child reads it as favouritism | Mark the occasion, not the price tag: a small "exam-season buddy" gift for the supporter too |
| Relatives ask only about the AISSEE child | The other child performs invisibility at family gatherings | Brief relatives beforehand: ask both children about their lives, or say nothing |
| Only the preparing child's effort gets praised | Effort becomes a competition | Praise each child's effort in their own domain, out loud, in front of each other |

## A weekly rhythm that protects both children

Children accept unequal hours far more easily than unequal regard. What they need is proof, in the calendar, that they matter. A practical rhythm:

- **One protected slot a day for the non-preparing child** — fifteen to twenty minutes of your undivided attention: a walk, a game, helping with their homework. Put it on the timetable next to the AISSEE study blocks so it is visibly equal in status.
- **One evening a week that is exam-free** — no mock-test post-mortems, no syllabus talk. The family does something ordinary together. This single habit does more for sibling warmth than any lecture.
- **Give the supporter a role, not a gag order.** "You are in charge of the 4 PM chai break" or "you quiz him on capitals for ten minutes" turns the younger child from an obstacle into a teammate. Children who feel useful rarely feel jealous.
- **Guard the preparing child's rest too.** A tired, snappy elder sibling is the fastest way to make the younger one hate the exam. Sleep and play are part of preparation, not theft from it.

## The harder conversation: if only one gets selected

Parents plan for the exam; few plan for the result. If your child clears AISSEE and the sibling does not — or was never in the race — the months after the result need as much care as the months before it.

Celebrate the selected child genuinely, but do not let the admission become the family's headline for the rest of the year. And give the other child something that is *theirs* — a sport, a skill, an olympiad, a project — so they are moving forward on their own track, not standing still while their sibling boards a train. If your family is navigating this, our piece on [what to do when a child doesn't want the Sainik School path](/blog/what-if-your-child-doesnt-want-to-join-sainik-school) walks through that conversation in detail.

Two more things worth saying plainly. First, **never force the uninterested sibling to "also prepare, just in case."** A child dragged into AISSEE prep resents the grind, disturbs the serious one's peace, and usually quits mid-season — everyone loses. Second, **discipline is a household culture, not one child's burden** — [building it at home](/blog/building-discipline-at-home-before-sainik-school-admission) lifts both children at once.

## The five-minute daily check-in

If you take one practice from this article, take this. Every day, separately, ask the non-preparing child three questions and actually listen to the answers:

1. What was the best part of your day?
2. What was the hardest part?
3. Is there anything you need from me tomorrow?

Five minutes. It costs nothing, and it tells the child what the exam season keeps trying to unsay: *you are not the audience for your sibling's life — you have one of your own, and I am watching it.*

AISSEE preparation ends in January. Siblinghood does not. When the results come — whichever way they go — the child who felt seen through the season will be the first one cheering at the dinner table. That is the real selection that matters, and it is entirely in your hands. ## FAQs

**Is it unfair that my AISSEE child gets more attention?**
Extra attention for an exam is not unfair — unequal love is. Children accept that exam seasons need more hours; what hurts them is feeling like the second priority as a person. A short daily one-on-one ritual with the other child fixes most of the damage.

**Should I stop my other child from disturbing AISSEE preparation?**
Set quiet hours, not silence orders. A home that goes on lockdown breeds resentment. Fixed study slots with a visible timetable work better than telling the younger one to disappear all evening.

**What if relatives keep comparing my two children?**
Shut comparisons down calmly and in front of the children: "They are on different paths, and both are doing well." Children remember who defended them far longer than they remember the relative's comment.

**What if only one child clears AISSEE and gets selected?**
Celebrate the selected child without making it the family's headline for months, and give the other child a project of their own — a sport, a skill, a goal. Being left behind hurts less when you are also moving forward.

**Should both siblings prepare for AISSEE even if one is not interested?**
No. Forcing a disinterested child into AISSEE preparation helps neither of them — one resents the grind, the other loses study peace. Support each child's own direction instead.
