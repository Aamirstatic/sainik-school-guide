## The mock is not the preparation. The analysis is.

Here's the thing most parents miss. Your child sits a three-hour mock on Sunday morning, you ask "kitne aaye?", the answer is 186 out of 300, and everyone moves on. The paper goes into a drawer. Next Sunday, another mock, another number.

That number tells you almost nothing. A child can score 186 in ten different ways — brilliant at maths and blank in GK, or steady everywhere with twenty silly mistakes. The score is a headline. The analysis is the story. And in the final 80 days before the exam on **31 January 2027**, the story is where the marks are hiding.

So here is a method that takes thirty minutes, needs nothing fancier than a notebook, and works whether your child is in Class 6 or Class 9.

## Step 1: Analyse the same day — 30 minutes, no exceptions

Memory fades fast. An hour after the test, your child still remembers *why* they picked option B in question 47. Three days later, they don't — and the review becomes a re-solve, which teaches far less.

Make it a rule: the mock ends, there's a short break, then **30 minutes of analysis before anything else**. Not two hours — thirty focused minutes beats a long, wandering review every time. If your child is tired, do 15 minutes now and 15 after dinner. But never "tomorrow". Tomorrow's mock is already waiting.

Our [mock test calendar for the final 80 days](/blog/aissee-2027-mock-test-calendar-final-80-days/) sets one full mock a week for exactly this reason — a rhythm that leaves room for the analysis, not just the test.

## Step 2: Score it section by section, against the real bar

First, write down the section scores, not just the total. For Class 6:

| Section | Questions | Marks | Qualifying mark (25%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Maths | 50 | 150 | 38 |
| Language | 25 | 50 | 13 |
| Intelligence | 25 | 50 | 13 |
| General Knowledge | 25 | 50 | 13 |
| **Total** | **125** | **300** | **120 aggregate (40%)** |

For Class 9 the bar is the same shape — 25% in each section and 40% aggregate out of 400 marks — with Maths alone worth 200.

Now look at the pattern, not the total. A child at 190 out of 300 with Maths at 30 out of 150 has a completely different problem from a child at 190 with every section just above the bar. The first child needs maths rescue. The second needs speed and accuracy across the board. Same total, opposite prescriptions — and you only see it when the scores are split.

## Step 3: Sort every wrong answer into four buckets

This is the heart of the method. Take every question your child got wrong or skipped, and put it in exactly one bucket:

| Bucket | What it means | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Silly mistake** | Knew it, misread the question, copied the wrong number, bubbled the wrong circle | A checking habit, not more study |
| **Concept gap** | Didn't know the method or the fact at all | Revisit the chapter, then re-solve |
| **Time pressure** | Knew it but ran out of time, or rushed and guessed | Section-wise timed drills |
| **Blind guess** | No idea, picked an answer at random | Note the topic — it goes on the revision list |

Honestly, most parents are surprised by the result. In my experience, a third or more of a child's lost marks are usually silly mistakes and time pressure — things that have nothing to do with how much the child knows. And those are the cheapest marks in the whole exam to win back.

A quick story: last year, a parent showed me his daughter's mock — 172 out of 300, and he was ready to buy a new maths book. We sorted her 34 wrong answers. Eleven were silly mistakes. Nine were time pressure in the last twenty minutes. Only fourteen were real concept gaps, and eight of those were in one chapter — fractions. One chapter, one checking habit, and her next mock was 204. No new book needed.

## Step 4: Keep a one-page error log

A notebook page per mock. Five columns, nothing more:

| Q. No. | Section | My answer | Correct answer | Bucket + one-line fix |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 47 | Maths | B | C | Silly — read "difference" as "sum" |
| 82 | GK | — (skipped) | A | Concept gap — revise national parks |
| 103 | Intelligence | D | A | Time — guessed in last 2 minutes |

The rule is one line per question. If the fix takes more than a line to write, the concept needs a proper revisit — that question goes on the week's revision list, not in the log.

Then, before the next mock, your child reads only the last two logs. Ten minutes. That's the whole revision of "what I keep getting wrong" — and it compounds. Three mocks in, the same silly mistake simply stops appearing, because the child has seen it written down in their own handwriting twice.

## Step 5: The three-mock trend rule

Never judge progress by one paper. A harder paper, a bad night's sleep, or a new section can drop a score by 20 marks — that is noise, not decline. Judge by the **average of three mocks**:

| | Mock 1 | Mock 2 | Mock 3 | Trend |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Maths (/150) | 84 | 79 | 96 | Rising — keep going |
| Language (/50) | 31 | 33 | 30 | Flat — needs attention |
| Intelligence (/50) | 28 | 34 | 36 | Rising |
| GK (/50) | 22 | 19 | 24 | Flat and low — weakest link |
| **Total (/300)** | **165** | **165** | **186** | **Moving, slowly** |

This is an illustrative example, not any real child's scores — but the reading of it is the point. The total barely moved between mock 1 and mock 2, yet Intelligence was climbing. And GK, flat and low across all three, is the section to rescue — not by buying a GK book, but by giving GK twenty minutes a day for two weeks. Our guide to [balancing static GK and current affairs](/blog/aissee-gk-static-vs-current-affairs-balance-plan/) shows how that time should be split.

If three mocks in a row show the same section weak, change the plan, not the child: cut an hour from the strongest section and give it to the weakest, and switch the weakest section from timed tests to slow, untimed concept work for a week.

## The traps to avoid

- **Re-solving only what went right.** It feels good and teaches nothing. The marks live in the wrong answers.
- **Comparing with toppers.** Your child's only honest comparison is their own last three mocks.
- **Skipping analysis when the score is good.** A 240 with twelve silly mistakes is a warning, not a victory — in the real exam, those same mistakes cost a seat.
- **Two mocks a week with no analysis.** One analysed mock beats three unanalysed ones. If the week is busy, skip the mock, never the analysis.
- **Blaming the child for a flat trend.** A flat trend is a plan problem. Change the plan first.

## Your after-every-mock checklist

1. Score it section by section against the 25% per-section bar.
2. Sort every wrong or skipped question into the four buckets.
3. Write the one-page error log — one line per question.
4. Circle the single weakest section and name one change for next week.
5. Re-solve only the concept-gap questions, from scratch, after revisiting the chapter.
6. Read the last two error logs before the next mock.
7. Record the section scores in the three-mock trend table.

Thirty minutes, once a week, for ten weeks. That is five hours of analysis between now and 31 January — and it is the cheapest marks your child will ever earn.

## FAQs

**How soon after a mock test should my child analyse it?**

The same day, ideally within an hour of finishing, while the paper is still fresh in memory. A 30-minute analysis session right after the test is worth more than a two-hour review three days later, when your child no longer remembers why they picked an answer.

**How many marks should my child target in AISSEE mock tests?**

For Class 6, aim to cross 40% aggregate (120 out of 300) comfortably and 25% in every section — Maths 38 out of 150, and 13 each out of 50 in Language, Intelligence and GK. But the real target is the trend: section scores rising across three mocks, not one lucky paper.

**Should my child re-solve the questions they got wrong?**

Yes — but only after sorting them. A silly mistake needs a checking habit, not a re-solve. A concept gap needs the chapter revisited first, then the question re-solved from scratch. Re-solving without knowing which bucket the error belongs to just repeats the same mistake faster.

**My child's mock scores are stuck at the same level. What should change?**

If three mocks in a row show the same section weak, the study plan — not the child — needs changing. Cut one hour from the strongest section and give it to the weakest, and switch the weakest section's practice from timed tests to slow, untimed concept work for a week.

**How many mock tests should a child take before AISSEE 2027?**

One full-length mock a week in the final 80 days is a sensible rhythm — about 10 to 12 papers — with section-wise practice in between. More than two full mocks a week usually means the analysis gets skipped, and an unanalysed mock teaches almost nothing.

**Is it normal for mock scores to go down sometimes?**

Yes, completely. A harder paper, a bad night's sleep or a new section can drop a score by 20 marks. Judge progress by the average of three mocks, not by any single paper — one dip is noise, three flat papers is a signal.
