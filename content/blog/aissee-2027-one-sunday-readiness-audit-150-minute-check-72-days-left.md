## You know the total. You don't know the problem.

Ask most parents how their child's AISSEE preparation is going, and you get a number. "He got 178 last Sunday." "She is scoring around 200."

Here's the thing — a total tells you almost nothing. The qualifying rule does not care about the total alone: your child needs **25% in every section** and **40% overall**. A child who scores 190 but gets 9 out of 50 in General Knowledge fails. A child who scores 130 with all four sections above the line is still in the race.

So with 72 days left, stop collecting totals. Do one honest audit instead. One Sunday. One full paper. One scorecard that shows you exactly where the marks are leaking — and, just as importantly, *why*.

## Step 1: Run the audit like the real exam

Pick a Sunday — this one, if you can. The audit only works if the conditions are real, because a diagnosis taken in comfort is a lie you tell yourself.

- **The paper:** a full-length mock or previous-year paper with the real split — 125 questions, 300 marks, Maths 50, Language 25, Intelligence 25, GK 25. A paper with the wrong split gives a wrong diagnosis.
- **The clock:** exactly **150 minutes**, 2 PM to 4:30 PM if you want to match the real exam's afternoon feel. No extra five minutes.
- **The room:** phone in another room. Water bottle and rough sheets on the table. No one walking in with a question.
- **The bubbling:** answers marked on a separate sheet, the way the OMR works — bubbling errors are a real, fixable leak, and you will never see them if your child just ticks in the book.

When the 150 minutes end, stop. Mark it the same evening, section by section — the memory of *why* an answer went wrong fades fast, and the "why" is the whole point.

## Step 2: Fill in the scorecard

Write the numbers down in a table. Not in your head — on paper, where you can look at them next to the qualifying lines.

| Section | Questions | Marks | Qualifying line (25%) | Your child's score | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mathematics | 50 | 150 | 37.5 | ___ / 150 | |
| Language | 25 | 50 | 12.5 | ___ / 50 | |
| Intelligence | 25 | 50 | 12.5 | ___ / 50 | |
| General Knowledge | 25 | 50 | 12.5 | ___ / 50 | |
| **Total** | **125** | **300** | **120 (40%)** | **___ / 300** | |

Two columns matter, not one. The "qualifying line" column tells you whether a section is a danger. The score tells you how far from the line you are. A section at 14 out of 50 is above the line but fragile — one bad day and it drops below. A section at 35 out of 150 in Maths is below the line and needs the most hours, because Maths is half the paper.

For Class 9, the same table works with the Class 9 shape: **180 minutes, 150 questions, 400 marks** — Maths 50 questions for 200 marks (line 50), and English, Intelligence, Science and Social Science at 25 questions and 50 marks each (line 12.5 each), with **160 out of 400** as the 40% aggregate.

## Step 3: Read the scorecard — four patterns, four different fixes

This is where most families go wrong. They see a low section and throw more hours at it. But a low score has at least four different causes, and each one needs a different fix. For every wrong or skipped question in the audit, ask one question: *why did this go wrong?*

**Pattern 1: "Didn't know it."** The chapter was never learned, or was learned and forgotten. The fix is study, not practice — one chapter at a time, with the NCERT book open, not a guidebook. In Maths it is usually two or three chapters doing all the damage: fractions, geometry, or time-speed-distance. Fix the chapters, not the whole subject.

**Pattern 2: "Knew it, ran out of time."** The answers were right, but the section was never finished. This is the most common Maths problem and the most fixable — it is a speed problem, not a knowledge problem. The fix is timed drills: 20 questions in 25 minutes, every day, with a stopwatch. Our [Class 6 Maths section strategy]({{< relref "aissee-class-6-maths-section-strategy-150-marks.md" >}}) has a drill routine built for exactly this.

**Pattern 3: "Knew it, marked it wrong."** Silly mistakes — the wrong option bubbled, a question misread, a calculation slip in the last step. If this is more than 5 or 6 questions in a paper, it is not bad luck; it is a habit. The fix is a checking routine: finish each section five minutes early and re-read only the bubbled answers against the question numbers. Keep an [error log]({{< relref "aissee-mock-test-analysis-error-log-method.md" >}}) — children who write down their silly mistakes stop repeating them.

**Pattern 4: "Guessed, and it didn't work."** In GK especially, a child who guesses everything will score around 12–13 out of 50 by luck alone — right at the qualifying line. That is a coin flip, not preparation. The fix is targeted: one page a day of the highest-yield GK — defence, sports, awards, and the static facts that repeat every year.

Write the pattern next to each section on the scorecard. Now you have a diagnosis, not a number.

## Step 4: The two-week fix cycle

An audit without a fix is just a bad Sunday. Turn the scorecard into a plan for the next two weeks — from Monday 23 November to Sunday 6 December, with the second audit on 6 December.

| Priority | What to do | Time per day |
|---|---|---|
| Weakest section (below the line) | One chapter or topic block a day, NCERT first, then 20 practice questions | 45 minutes |
| Second-weakest section | Timed drills only — no new chapters, just speed and accuracy | 25 minutes |
| Strongest section | One short set a week to keep it warm; do not add hours here | 15 minutes, 3 days a week |
| Silly mistakes (all sections) | Error log review every evening; re-do the week's logged questions on Sunday | 10 minutes |

Notice what this plan does *not* do: it does not add hours to the strongest section, and it does not ask for more total study time. Most children in the last 72 days are already studying enough. The audit's job is to move the hours they already spend to the place where the marks are leaking. After two weeks, run the audit again — same conditions, a different paper — and compare the two scorecards. If the weakest section moved up by 8–10 marks, the fix is working; keep it. If it did not move at all, the fix was wrong — change the method, not the child.

## What not to do with an audit

A few honest warnings, because audits can do damage if they are used wrongly.

**Don't audit every Sunday.** Three audits are enough: now, around 20 December, and in the last week of January. Weekly full mocks in between are practice, not diagnosis. A child who is tested every week starts performing for the test instead of learning from it.

**Don't read the total out loud as a verdict.** "You got 160, that's not enough" tells a child nothing useful and makes the next audit scarier. Read the sections: "Maths is 12 marks short of the line, and it's a speed problem — we can fix speed in two weeks." That is a plan, not a judgement.

**Don't compare with anyone else's child, and don't start new books because of one audit.** Another family's 210 means nothing about your child's 160 — the only comparison that matters is your child's last audit against this one. And one bad GK score does not mean a new GK book; it usually means one page a day of the right GK. New books in December are the most expensive way to feel busy.

## The December calendar, honestly

The form deadline is **2 December** — if you have not submitted yet, that comes first. The late window (3–6 December, ₹500 extra) and the correction window (7–9 December) are safety nets, not your plan. The JNVST exam is on **Saturday, 28 November** for children sitting both. Then winter break, then the [30-day January revision plan]({{< relref "aissee-2027-30-day-revision-plan-january-calendar-2026-11-19.md" >}}) takes over, and the exam is on **Sunday, 31 January 2027**.

The audit fits into that calendar exactly once now — this Sunday, before the deadline week eats your attention. Do it, write the scorecard, and let the numbers decide the next two weeks. That is what a diagnosis is for.

## FAQs

**What is the AISSEE readiness audit?**

One full-length paper attempted in real exam conditions — 150 minutes, 125 questions, phone away, OMR-style bubbling — followed by a section-wise scorecard. It shows which section is actually failing and why, instead of a single total that hides the problem.

**What score should my child aim for in the audit?**

The qualifying rule needs 25 percent in every section and 40 percent overall — 37.5 out of 150 in Maths, 12.5 out of 50 in each of the other three sections, and 120 out of 300 in total. But treat the audit as a diagnosis, not a pass or fail. A child at 90 with one section at 8 needs a different fix from a child at 90 with all four sections even.

**Can we use any mock test paper for the audit?**

Yes, as long as it matches the real paper: 125 questions, 150 minutes, 300 marks, with the same section split — Maths 50 questions, Language 25, Intelligence 25, General Knowledge 25. A paper with the wrong split gives a wrong diagnosis.

**My child scored badly in the audit. Should we increase study hours?**

No — not yet. First find the cause. A bad Maths score from slow solving needs timed drills, not more chapters. A bad GK score from guessing needs a different fix from a bad Language score from silly mistakes. More hours on the wrong fix only makes a tired child.

**How often should we repeat the readiness audit?**

Three times is enough: once now, once around 20 December after the first fix cycle, and once in the last week of January as a final check. Weekly full mocks in between are for practice, not diagnosis — the audit is the health check, and doing it too often just burns Sundays.

**Does the audit work for Class 9 as well?**

Yes, with the Class 9 shape: 180 minutes, 150 questions, 400 marks — Maths 50 questions for 200 marks, and English, Intelligence, Science and Social Science at 25 questions and 50 marks each. The qualifying rule is the same: 25 percent per section and 40 percent overall, which is 160 out of 400.
