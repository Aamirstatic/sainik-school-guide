## The question every AISSEE parent should ask

Most parents who come to this site have one plan: AISSEE, then a Sainik School, then — hopefully — the NDA. That plan is worth pursuing. But it is also a plan with a hard edge: AISSEE is a national exam with limited seats, and most children who sit it will not get a Sainik School seat. So the honest question is not "what if my child fails?" but "what is the second residential-school option, and is it actually good?"

This guide looks at the state-run residential schools that parents most often ask about: KGBV schools for girls, Gurukulam schools, and Uttar Pradesh's Atal Residential Schools and JPNSV network. They share one big attraction — they are free. They also share one big limitation — they are not open to everyone, and they are not Sainik Schools by another name.

## First, what AISSEE actually gives you

A quick baseline, so the comparison is fair. AISSEE is a national, merit-based entrance exam run by NTA for admission to Classes 6 and 9 in Sainik Schools and New Sainik Schools. Anyone who meets the age and class criteria can apply, regardless of income or social category — reservation rules then apply within the merit list, including the 67% home-state quota and the 10% girls' quota. Families pay a fee (Rs 850 for General/OBC/Defence and Rs 700 for SC/ST in the 2026 cycle) and, after admission, school fees that vary by school. What you buy with that is a specific culture: military-style discipline, NCC, physical training, leadership roles, and a peer group aimed at the armed forces. No state boarding school below replicates that package.

## KGBV: the girls-only free residential option

Kasturba Gandhi Balika Vidyalayas (KGBVs) are the most established state residential alternative, and the one with the clearest rules. Launched in 2004 and now run under the Samagra Shiksha scheme, KGBVs are residential schools for girls from disadvantaged backgrounds, set up mainly in educationally backward blocks. They cover Classes 6 to 12 in most states (originally Classes 6–8, extended upward over time), and everything is free: tuition, boarding, lodging, textbooks and uniforms.

Admission is where KGBV differs most sharply from AISSEE. It is not an open national exam. Priority goes to girls from SC, ST, OBC and minority communities — around 75% of seats — with the remaining seats prioritised for below-poverty-line families, and special focus on girls who are out of school or at risk of dropping out. In practice, a KGBV place is a lifeline for exactly the girls it was designed for, and not a realistic backup for a general-category family that simply missed the AISSEE cutoff. If your daughter fits the criteria, though, it is an excellent option: free, residential, with self-defence training, bridge courses for late entrants, and vocational components in the higher classes. Admission runs through the state education machinery, so check your district education office for the current process rather than waiting for a national notification that will never come.

## Gurukulam: the tribal-welfare residential network

The word "Gurukul" covers several different things, and parents should not let the name do the thinking. The most concrete network is Andhra Pradesh's APTWREIS, known as Gurukulam: a tribal-welfare residential system that, by its own official account, runs 199 institutions — 78 state-funded residential institutions, 28 Eklavya Model Residential Schools (EMRS), 12 mini-Gurukulams for low-literacy areas, and 81 hostels converted into tribal welfare residential schools. Its stated purpose is the all-round development of tribal children, with a fully residential, learner-centred campus model.

The lesson for parents elsewhere is not "apply to Gurukulam" — that network serves its own state's tribal students. The lesson is that most states run their own welfare residential networks with their own names, beneficiary groups and admission routes. If you live outside Andhra Pradesh, ask your state education department what its equivalent is, rather than searching for a single national "Gurukul admission form" — there isn't one.

## Uttar Pradesh's two newer networks: Atal schools and JPNSV

Uttar Pradesh is worth a separate look because it has built two large free residential networks in recent years, and UP parents ask about both.

**Atal Residential Schools** are the better documented of the two. Named after former Prime Minister Atal Bihari Vajpayee, the network was approved with 18 schools — one per divisional headquarters — each designed for 1,000 students (500 boys, 500 girls) from Classes 6 to 12, following the CBSE pattern on 12–15 acre campuses. They are free, and they are aimed at specific groups: children of registered construction workers, children orphaned during COVID-19, and beneficiaries of the Mukhyamantri Bal Seva Yojana. The Chief Minister has described them as gurukul-inspired, with Indian values and skill development at their centre, and expansion to 57 districts has been announced. The eligibility gate is the point: if your family is not in one of the beneficiary groups, an Atal school is not your backup plan.

**Jai Prakash Narayan Sarvodaya Vidyalayas (JPNSV)** are reported by Outlook Money (July 2026) as a newer UP network of 101 government-run schools offering free residential education from Classes 6 to 12, with hostel, books and competitive-exam coaching, for SC, ST, OBC and economically weaker general-category students, affiliated to CBSE or the UP board. We label this as reported, not confirmed, because it rests on a single source so far. If JPNSV matters to your family, verify it with the UP social welfare department before making any plan around it.

## The honest comparison

| Feature | Sainik School (via AISSEE) | KGBV | State welfare residential (Gurukulam / Atal / JPNSV) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who can apply | Any eligible child, national merit exam | Girls from disadvantaged backgrounds, priority-based | Specific beneficiary groups per scheme, state-run |
| Cost to family | Exam fee + school fees | Free (boarding, books, uniform) | Free (boarding, books, facilities) |
| Classes covered | Entry at 6 and 9 | 6–12 (varies by state) | 6–12 |
| Defence / NDA focus | Yes — the core purpose | No — general academics, retention focus | No — general academics, values and skills focus |
| Admission route | One national exam (AISSEE) | State/district process, eligibility-gated | State process, eligibility-gated |
| Quality consistency | Varies by school, but common AISSEE standard | Varies significantly by school and state | Varies significantly by school and state |

Two rows of that table deserve emphasis. First, quality variance: state residential schools are run school by school, and two schools under the same scheme can feel like different worlds. Visit the actual school and talk to parents of current students before deciding. Second, the NDA row: if the defence career is the goal, a state boarding school does not substitute for a Sainik School. It can still be a very good school — but it is a different product.

## What to actually do

If AISSEE is your main plan, keep it your main plan, and treat state options as a parallel track, not a consolation prize. Check eligibility first: for KGBV, Atal or JPNSV, eligibility decides everything, so find out the district-level process now, not after the AISSEE result. Apply to both tracks where allowed — different bodies, different timelines, one application does not block the other. Visit before you commit: hostel conditions, teacher stability and safety arrangements matter more than any brochure. And keep the defence dream honest: if your child still wants the NDA after a non-Sainik schooling, that route stays open — NDA entry depends on the UPSC exam and SSB, not on which school you attended.

## The bottom line

State boarding schools are worth considering — genuinely worth it, not as a polite fallback line. For a girl from a disadvantaged background, a KGBV place can change a life. For an eligible UP family, an Atal school place is free, residential, CBSE-pattern schooling that would otherwise be unaffordable. But they are eligibility-gated, quality varies by school, and none of them is a Sainik School. Go in with that clarity, and the second option becomes a real plan instead of a disappointment dressed up as one.

## FAQs

**Are KGBV schools really free?**

Yes. KGBV schools provide free residential education, including boarding, lodging, textbooks and uniforms, for girls from disadvantaged backgrounds under the Samagra Shiksha scheme.

**Can a boy apply to a KGBV school?**

No. KGBV schools are for girls only. Boys looking for a free state residential option should look at their own state's schemes, such as UP's Atal Residential Schools or JPNSV, subject to eligibility.

**Do state boarding schools prepare children for NDA like Sainik Schools?**

No. Sainik Schools are specifically designed around defence careers, with NCC, physical training and an NDA-oriented culture. State residential schools focus on general academics and keeping disadvantaged children in school; NDA preparation is not their job.

**Can my child apply to both AISSEE and a state boarding school?**

Yes, in principle. They are run by different bodies with different eligibility rules and timelines, so one application does not block the other. Check your state's current admission process before assuming any date or route.

**Who should not count on a state boarding school as a backup?**

Families who do not meet the eligibility criteria — for example, general-category families above the income or social-category thresholds for KGBV, or families outside the beneficiary groups for Atal schools — should not treat these as a fallback, because admission is gated by eligibility, not by exam merit alone.

**Where do I check the current admission process for my state?**

Start with your state education department or district education office, and the school or scheme's official site. Admission routes, dates and documents vary by state and change year to year, so do not rely on a single blog post, including this one.
