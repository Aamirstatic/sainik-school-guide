## The 50-mark question every Class 9 student gets wrong

Ask a Class 9 AISSEE aspirant how they are preparing Social Science and you will usually hear the same answer: "I am memorising the dates." Ask them a month later and you will hear the same complaint: "I studied so much, but the questions were different."

Both are telling the truth. Social Science in AISSEE Class 9 is 25 questions worth 50 marks out of a 400-mark paper — 12.5 percent of the total, against Mathematics at 200 marks. It is not the section that gets you selected on its own. But it is very often the section that decides whether a borderline student makes the merit list, because the paper has no negative marking and every one of those 25 questions is a free attempt.

The real question is not "dates or concepts?" It is "which kind of knowledge actually converts into marks on an MCQ paper?" And the honest answer is: concepts first, dates as anchors, and facts only where they unlock a concept.

## Why dates alone fail

An MCQ rarely asks "In which year did X happen?" in isolation. What it usually asks is closer to: "Which of the following was a consequence of X?" or "Arrange these events in order" or "Which statement about X is correct?" A student who memorised "X happened in 1920" but never understood what X was, why it happened, or what changed because of it will get all three of those wrong — while a student who knows the story but is fuzzy on the exact year will often get two of the three right by elimination.

Dates still have a job, but it is a narrow one. Learn the dates that anchor a story in time: the year a movement started, the year a constitution or act came into force, the decade a geographical process belongs to. Then learn the sequence — what came before, what came after. Sequence questions are common, and sequence survives even when an exact year slips your mind.

## When concepts score more

Concepts are reusable. Understand federalism once and you can answer questions about the division of powers, about why a subject sits on one list rather than another, and about disputes between levels of government — three differently-worded questions from one idea. Understand why the Earth has seasons and you can answer questions about latitude, the tilt of the axis, and the difference between the Northern and Southern Hemispheres.

That reusability is what makes concepts the better investment. A memorised date answers one question. A understood concept answers a family of questions. And on a paper where Social Science gives you just 25 questions, the student who owns ten strong concepts will outscore the student who owns a hundred loose dates.

Geography rewards this especially well: map sense, climate logic, and resource logic transfer across questions. Civics is similar: once the idea of rights, duties, and democratic institutions clicks, most questions become recognition exercises rather than recall tests. History is where students over-invest in dates — and where cause-and-consequence thinking pays off most.

## A realistic revision-time split

Here is how to divide Social Science preparation in the months before the exam. The split assumes about 45 minutes a day for this section — no more, because Mathematics at 200 marks must stay the priority — our [20 most repeated Class 9 Maths question types](/blog/aissee-maths-20-most-repeated-question-types/) shows where those marks actually come from — and because grammar-style accuracy drills in other sections — like the ones in our [Class 6 English grammar guide](/blog/aissee-class-6-english-grammar-mistakes-that-cost-marks/) — show the same principle: fix the repeatable errors first.

| Revision block | Share of SST time | What you actually do |
|---|---|---|
| Concept building (History causes, Civics ideas, Geography logic) | 50% | Read a topic, then explain it aloud in your own words without the book |
| Anchor dates + sequences | 20% | One timeline per chapter: 8–12 anchor dates, in order, with one line each on why they matter |
| Practice questions + error log | 20% | 10 MCQs a day; every wrong answer gets one line in an error notebook — the concept missed, not just the right option |
| Map work + diagrams | 10% | Rivers, states, climate zones, and key locations on a blank map, twice a week |

Notice what this split does: dates get a fifth of the time, not all of it. And the error log matters more than any single study session, because AISSEE repeats question *types* even when it changes the question — the same trap appears in new clothes.

## The weekly rhythm that holds up

- **Monday to Friday:** one topic a day, 45 minutes, following the split above.
- **Saturday:** a 25-question timed drill — the full Social Science section in 25 minutes, to practise the pace of roughly one minute per question.
- **Sunday:** error-log review only. No new topic. Revisit every question you got wrong that week and write the concept behind it in one sentence.

If a topic keeps producing errors after two weeks, that is a signal, not a failure: it means the concept underneath is missing, and re-reading the same notes will not fix it. Find a different explanation — a teacher, a video, a simpler textbook — and rebuild from the concept, not from the dates.

## The last-month rule

In the final month, stop adding new dates entirely. Your timeline sheets are done; your job is to keep them warm with quick daily reads while spending the freed time on mock papers and error logs. Students who keep cramming new facts in the last month usually report the same experience: the new facts crowd out the old ones, and the paper feels unfamiliar even though they studied more.

Social Science will not win you a Sainik School seat by itself. But 50 marks, attempted with understanding rather than memorised lists, is very often the difference between a rank that gets a call and a rank that waits for the next round. Treat dates as anchors, concepts as the ship, and the error log as the compass — and the section starts paying back.

## FAQs

**How many marks is AISSEE Class 9 Social Science worth?**

Under the AISSEE 2026 pattern, Social Science is 25 questions of 2 marks each, totalling 50 marks out of the 400-mark Class 9 paper. That is 12.5 percent of the paper — small next to Mathematics at 200 marks, but big enough to decide a merit-list place.

**Should I memorise dates for AISSEE Class 9 Social Science?**

Memorise only the handful of dates that anchor a concept — for example, the year a movement began or a constitutional provision was adopted — and learn the sequence of events rather than exact day-month dates. Pure date-recall questions are rare; questions that test what happened, why it happened and what followed are far more common.

**Which part of Social Science scores easiest — History, Geography or Civics?**

There is no fixed split published in the bulletin, but in practice Civics and Geography reward understanding fastest: if you grasp a concept like federalism or a map-based idea like latitude, you can answer several differently-worded questions. History rewards students who know causes and consequences, not those who cram dates.

**How much time should Class 9 students give Social Science daily?**

Forty-five minutes a day is enough if it is split well: twenty minutes on a new concept, fifteen on revision of yesterday's topic, and ten on ten practice questions. Social Science should not eat into Mathematics time, which carries four times its marks.

**Does Social Science matter for tie-breaking in AISSEE Class 9?**

Yes, but late in the order. If total marks are equal, NTA compares Mathematics first, then Intelligence, then English, then General Science, then Social Science, and finally lower age. Social Science only decides ties that survive all four earlier comparisons — which is exactly why it matters for borderline merit-list cases.

**Is there negative marking in AISSEE Class 9 Social Science?**

No. Wrong answers and blank answers both score zero, and nothing is deducted. Attempt all 25 Social Science questions, using elimination on the ones you are unsure about.
