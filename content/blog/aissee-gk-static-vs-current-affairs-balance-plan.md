## The section everyone leaves for last

Ask a parent what their child is revising tonight and you will hear "maths" nine times out of ten. Ask what they are doing for GK and the answer is usually a shrug — "he reads the newspaper sometimes."

Here is the thing: in the Class 6 paper, GK is **25 questions worth 50 marks** out of 300 — one-sixth of the paper, the same weight as the entire Language section and the entire Intelligence section. A child who scores 40 in GK instead of 20 has gained the same ground as a child who jumps a full grade in English. And GK, unlike maths, is the section where a little steady work shows up fastest, because the questions are mostly recall, not problem-solving.

With 80 days to the exam on **31 January 2027**, GK is a scoring opportunity hiding in plain sight.

## First, know what GK actually is in this exam

| | Class 6 | Class 9 |
|---|---|---|
| GK section | ✅ Yes — 25 questions, 50 marks | ❌ No separate section |
| Where GK lives | Its own section | Inside Social Science (25 Q, 50 marks), partly in Science |
| Total paper | 300 marks, 150 min, 125 questions | 400 marks, 180 min, 150 questions |
| Qualifying mark | 25% per section + 40% aggregate | 25% per section + 40% aggregate |
| Negative marking | None — attempt everything | None — attempt everything |

For Class 6, that means at least **13 marks out of 50** in GK just to clear the section qualifying mark — and realistically much more, because the merit list decides admission. For Class 9, there is no GK section to clear, but the same knowledge shows up inside Social Science, so the work still pays.

GK in this exam is really two different subjects wearing one name, and that is where most preparation goes wrong.

## Static GK vs current affairs — the honest split

**Static GK** is the stuff that does not change: states and capitals, rivers and mountains, national symbols, the Constitution's basic facts, important dates, sports, awards, and defence — the armed forces, their ranks, their mottos. Learn it once, revise it, and it stays learned. Our guide to [the GK topics that keep repeating](/blog/aissee-class-6-gk-defence-sports-awards-repeat/) — defence, sports and awards — is a good place to see which static areas come back year after year.

**Current affairs** is the stuff that happened recently: a big national event, a new government scheme, a tournament winner, a major award, an important appointment. It changes every month, it cannot be revised the way static facts can, and — this surprises most parents — it is the *smaller* share of the questions.

So how should 80 days be split? Our recommendation, not an official rule:

| GK work | Share of GK time | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Static GK revision | ~70% | 20–25 minutes a day: one topic area per day, revisited weekly |
| Current affairs | ~30% | 10–15 minutes a day: one children's news page, plus a monthly roundup |
| Weekly quiz | Built into both | 20 questions every Sunday, from the week's own notes |

The mistake to avoid is the reverse split — an hour a day on the news and none on the map. A child who knows every headline of the last fortnight but cannot name the states on India's east coast will lose more marks than the child who did it the other way round.

## A weekly GK routine that fits a school day

Your child already has school, homework and three other sections to prepare. GK does not need an hour. It needs **30 minutes a day, every day**, in this shape:

**Monday to Friday — 30 minutes after homework:**
- 20 minutes: one static topic. Monday — states and capitals. Tuesday — rivers, mountains, national parks. Wednesday — sports and awards. Thursday — the armed forces and defence. Friday — the Constitution, national symbols and important dates. The same five topics repeat every week, so by the exam each one has been covered eleven times.
- 10 minutes: the day's news, from one source only — a children's newspaper page or a short news roundup written for school students. One fact written down in a notebook, in the child's own words. One fact a day is 80 facts by the exam. That is plenty.

**Saturday — 30 minutes:** map work. An outline map of India, no labels. The child fills in states, capitals, major rivers and neighbouring countries from memory, then checks. Map questions are predictable marks, and the most skipped.

**Sunday — 30 minutes:** a 20-question quiz from the week's notes, set by the parent, with the answers discussed out loud. Wrong answers go back into the notebook. This is also the day to attempt a full mock paper — our [mock test calendar for the final 80 days](/blog/aissee-2027-mock-test-calendar-final-80-days/) shows how many to take and how to use each one.

## Current affairs: what counts, and what to ignore

For a Class 6 child, "current affairs" does not mean the daily news cycle. It means the big, durable stories of roughly the last year — the ones that will still matter in January. A practical filter: if a story would still be worth a quiz question six months from now, it counts. If it will be forgotten next week, it doesn't.

Three sources are enough, and one is better than three done badly:

1. **A children's newspaper page or a monthly current-affairs roundup for school students.** Written at the right level, with the noise removed. This is the single best source for this age.
2. **The school assembly or classroom news.** Many schools already discuss one news item a day. Ask your child what it was at dinner — that conversation is revision.
3. **One simple monthly compendium** (a thin magazine or a free PDF roundup), read once a month, not daily.

What to skip: news channels, adult competitive-exam magazines, and any app that promises "daily current affairs for AISSEE." They are written for UPSC and SSC candidates, not for an 11-year-old, and they will drown the child in detail that never appears in this paper. Honestly, most of them overcomplicate this — a child needs the headline fact, not the committee that wrote the report.

## The three mistakes that cost GK marks

**1. Buying three GK books and finishing none.** One book, revised four times, beats four books read once. Pick the NCERT Class 5 and 6 GK pages or one simple general-knowledge book and stay with it.

**2. Treating current affairs as the whole subject.** The news feels urgent, so it eats the time. Static GK is the bulk of the marks and the part you can actually finish. Do static first, news second, every day.

**3. Reading without ever testing it.** GK learned by reading alone evaporates — it feels familiar on the page and blank in the exam hall. One line a day in a notebook, plus the Sunday quiz, is what makes it stick. And since there is **no negative marking**, the exam-day rule is simple: attempt every GK question, even the ones you are unsure about. A guess costs nothing; a blank costs a mark.

## FAQs

**How many marks is General Knowledge worth in AISSEE Class 6?**

GK carries 25 questions worth 50 marks in the Class 6 paper — one-sixth of the 300 total. The paper is 150 minutes long with 125 questions in all: Maths 50 questions (150 marks), Language 25 (50 marks), Intelligence 25 (50 marks) and GK 25 (50 marks).

**Is there a separate GK section in AISSEE Class 9?**

No. The Class 9 paper has no standalone GK section. GK-type questions appear mainly inside Social Science (25 questions, 50 marks) and partly in Science. The Class 9 paper is 400 marks over 180 minutes: Maths 50 questions (200 marks), and English, Intelligence, Science and Social Science 25 questions (50 marks) each.

**How much time should my child spend on current affairs for AISSEE GK?**

About a third of GK time is a sensible split in the final 80 days — roughly 15 to 20 minutes a day on current affairs, and the rest on static GK revision. Current affairs is a smaller share of the questions than most parents assume, and it is also the part you cannot cram in the last week.

**What current affairs should a Class 6 child follow for AISSEE?**

The big, durable news of the last year — major national events, new schemes, sports winners, awards and important appointments — rather than daily headlines. A children's newspaper page or a monthly current-affairs roundup for school students covers it. Avoid anything written for competitive exams for adults.

**Do I need to buy a GK book for AISSEE?**

One simple general-knowledge book or the GK pages of the NCERT Class 5 and 6 books is enough. What matters is not the number of books but the revision: the same facts, revisited three or four times, beat a new book finished once.

**What is the qualifying score in AISSEE?**

A candidate needs at least 25% in each section and 40% in aggregate to qualify — so for Class 6 GK that means at least 13 marks out of 50. No minimum applies to SC/ST candidates in the existing Sainik Schools. There is no negative marking, so attempt every GK question.
