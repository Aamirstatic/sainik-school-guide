Ask any parent what changed in the kitchen during board-exam season or a serious entrance-prep phase, and you will hear the same confession: breakfast got skipped, maggi became dinner, and the child survived on biscuits and chai. AISSEE 2027 is on **31 January 2027** — roughly four months of heavy study from here — and food is the one performance lever most families ignore.

Here is the honest truth nobody tells parents: you do not need superfoods, supplements, or a dietitian. You need **regular food, served on time, with less junk**. The rest is detail. But the details do matter, so let us get them right.

## Why food changes during study season

During heavy study phases, three things go wrong at once. Children skip meals because "there is no time." The meals they do eat get heavier and oilier because stressed families reach for comfort food. And snacking moves from the kitchen to the desk, which means chips, biscuits, and namkeen instead of real food.

The result is predictable: a child who is sleepy at 11 AM (heavy breakfast or none at all), cranky at 5 PM (no afternoon food), and wide awake at midnight (caffeine plus sugar, plus a body clock that never got a proper dinner). None of this is a discipline problem. It is a fuel problem.

## The 7 rules that actually work

### 1. Breakfast is non-negotiable

If you change exactly one thing, make it this. A hungry child cannot concentrate — studies link skipping breakfast with poorer attention in children, and the crash lands right in the middle of morning study. The good news for Indian parents: our everyday breakfasts are already excellent. Vegetable poha, upma with peanuts, moong dal cheela, idli, vegetable dalia, or oats with nuts and fruit — all provide the slow-release energy a child needs. Add protein where you can (an egg, a bowl of dahi, paneer in the cheela), because protein digests slower than plain carbs and keeps a child full longer. This matches what dietitians consistently recommend: protein plus slow carbs at breakfast supports steadier attention through the morning.

### 2. Smaller meals, more often

Three giant meals make a studying child sleepy. Dietitians at Max Healthcare put it bluntly: loading children with large meals redirects blood to the stomach and leaves the brain under-fuelled, so small, frequent, nutritious meals keep a child alert. Practically, this means: proper breakfast, a mid-morning fruit, a normal lunch, an evening snack, a light dinner. Five touchpoints instead of three. The evening snack is the one most families skip — do not. A child coming back from school or a long study stretch at 5 PM needs food before the evening study block, or the whole evening is wasted.

### 3. Protein at every main meal

This is the simplest upgrade with the biggest payoff. Carbs burn fast; protein provides the slow, steady supply of energy that keeps a child going through a two-hour study session. Dal, rajma, chana, sprouts, eggs, paneer, dahi, peanuts, soya — most Indian kitchens already have these. Just make sure they actually appear on the plate, not just in the pantry. A khichdi with extra dal, a cheela with paneer filling, dahi with lunch, a boiled egg or sprout chaat in the evening — small additions, no menu overhaul.

### 4. Water is a study tool, not an afterthought

Children studying indoors, often in air conditioning, feel less thirsty and quietly drink far too little. Dehydration shows up as headaches, restlessness, and an inability to focus — symptoms parents often mistake for laziness or stress. Keep a **visible water bottle on the study desk** and aim for roughly 1.5 to 2 litres across the day, taken as regular sips. If plain water is a battle, nimbu pani, chaas, or buttermilk work just as well. Coconut water is fine too. What does not count: colas and packaged juices, which are sugar delivery systems wearing a fruit costume.

### 5. Cut the sugar spikes

Sugar is not evil, but the pattern of sugar most study-season kids fall into is: biscuits with morning tea, a chocolate bar at 4 PM, a cola while studying at night. Each spike is followed by a crash — drowsiness, irritability, worse concentration than before. You do not need to ban sweets (that just creates smuggling). Instead, **replace the default**: keep a bowl of fruit, roasted makhana, or peanuts where the biscuit tin used to sit. When a child reaches for something while studying, what is within arm's reach is what gets eaten.

### 6. Dinner should be light and early

A heavy, oily, late dinner does two kinds of damage: it ruins sleep quality, and it makes the morning sluggish. During study season, aim for dinner by 8 PM at the latest, and keep it lighter than lunch — dal-roti-sabzi, khichdi, or soup with toast. Save the heavy parathas and biryani for the weekend. Good sleep is when the day's studying actually gets consolidated into memory, and good sleep starts with a dinner that does not fight the stomach all night.

### 7. The exam-week version of all this

In the final week before the exam (and especially the night before and morning of), do not experiment. No new restaurant, no "energy food" the child has never tried, no heavy festive meal. Familiar food, eaten calmly, on time. On exam morning: a proper breakfast eaten at least an hour before leaving — poha, upma, idli, or eggs with toast — plus water. A familiar stomach is a calm mind.

## A sample exam-season day (steal this)

| Time | What | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| 7:00 AM | Vegetable poha/upma + milk or dahi | Slow carbs + protein; carries the morning |
| 10:30 AM | One banana or apple + handful of peanuts | Bridges the gap to lunch, no crash |
| 1:00 PM | Dal, sabzi, 2 rotis, rice, dahi | Full balanced lunch; biggest meal of the day |
| 5:00 PM | Sprout chaat / veg sandwich / soup | Refuels the evening study block |
| 8:00 PM | Light khichdi or dal-roti-sabzi | Early, light dinner for good sleep |
| Through the day | Water bottle on the desk | 1.5–2 litres in small sips |

Adjust the foods to what your family actually eats — a Tamil household's idli-sambar and a Punjabi household's dal-roti are equally good. The structure matters more than the specific dishes.

## FAQs

**Should my child eat differently during heavy exam preparation?**

Yes, in emphasis more than in ingredients. Regular meals at fixed times, a proper protein-rich breakfast, smaller frequent meals instead of heavy ones, plenty of water, and far less junk food. No exotic superfoods are needed — the family's normal healthy food, served on time, does the job.

**Is skipping breakfast really that bad during exam prep?**

It is the single worst food habit during study season. A child who skips breakfast runs out of energy mid-morning, which is exactly when schools schedule the hardest periods. A simple poha, upma or dalia breakfast is enough — the point is eating something with staying power, not cooking a feast.

**Are energy drinks or too much tea and coffee okay for studying late?**

No. Sugary energy drinks cause a quick spike followed by a crash, and too much caffeine in children leads to poor sleep, which ruins the next day's studying. A small cup of milk tea occasionally is fine for older children (caffeine is not recommended for younger kids); anything marketed as an energy drink is best avoided entirely.

**What is the best snack for late-evening study sessions?**

Light and warm beats heavy and fried. A glass of warm milk with a small handful of roasted peanuts or almonds, a banana, or a bowl of vegetable soup keeps a child alert without overloading the stomach. Avoid maggi, chips and heavy parathas right before study time.

**How much water should a studying child drink?**

About 1.5 to 2 litres a day, taken as regular sips rather than large amounts at once. Children studying indoors, often in air conditioning, feel less thirsty and quietly dehydrate — keep a visible water bottle on the study desk as a reminder.
