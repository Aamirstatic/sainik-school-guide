## The school famous for officers also produced founders

Ask most parents what a Sainik School is for and the answer comes quickly: it prepares boys and girls for the National Defence Academy and the armed forces. That is true, and it is the schools' founding purpose. But it is only half the story. Some of the most recognisable names to come out of the Sainik School system never made business their plan B — they made it their life's work, and the habits they picked up in school uniform turned out to be the same habits that build companies.

That matters for parents weighing an application, because the honest question is not "will my child become an officer?" It is "what will this school give my child if life takes them somewhere else?" The entrepreneur alumni are the best evidence available for that second question.

## Captain G. R. Gopinath: the farmer's son who put India on a plane

The most dramatic example is Captain G. R. Gopinath, an alumnus of Sainik School, Bijapur, who founded Air Deccan, India's first low-cost airline. Before aviation, Gopinath served in the Army, then tried farming — and both chapters show up in the airline story. Air Deccan's idea was simple and, at the time, radical: flying should not be a luxury reserved for the rich. By stripping out frills and keeping fares low, it put first-time flyers on planes across India and forced the whole industry to rethink who its customer was.

For a parent, the lesson is not "your child will start an airline." It is that Gopinath's venture depended on qualities a boarding school drills daily: operating on a tight routine, deciding with incomplete information, and persisting when the first attempt fails. He did not go from school to startup in one leap; he went through service, farming and failure, and the discipline survived every change of direction.

## Pawan Munjal: from a school in Kapurthala to the world's largest two-wheeler maker

The second example is quieter but no less instructive. Pawan Munjal, an alumnus of Sainik School, Kapurthala, is the Chairman and CEO of Hero MotoCorp, one of the largest motorcycle manufacturers in the world. He did not found the company — he grew up inside a family business and then led it through the decades when India's two-wheeler market exploded, keeping it at the top of a fiercely competitive industry.

Munjal's story teaches a different lesson. Entrepreneurship is not only about starting something from nothing; it is also about running something large, well, for a long time. That requires consistency, attention to detail, loyalty to a team, and holding standards when nobody is watching — virtues a residential school rewards daily, from morning parade to prep time.

## The smaller, closer example: a retired major who built a business from Kolhapur

Not every alumni business story involves an airline or a listed company, and parents should hear the smaller ones too. Major Sanjay Shinde (Retd), who passed out from Sainik School, Satara, and served in the Army for 12 years, built a manufacturing business in Kolhapur after retiring early — starting with candles and expanding into incense sticks, phenyl, handwash, sanitiser and brooms. His story, reported in 2024, is useful because it is ordinary in the best sense: no investors, no headlines, just Army training applied to a small factory, one product at a time.

That is the realistic shape of most alumni entrepreneurship. It looks less like a startup launch and more like a second career built slowly, with discipline substituting for capital in the early years.

## What the school actually teaches that a founder needs

Strip away the romance and the transferable skills are specific. First, routine: a cadet lives by a timetable, and a founder lives or dies by one too. Second, small-team leadership: house captains, prefects and team captains learn early what it means to be responsible for other people, not just for themselves. Third, coping with setbacks in public: losing a match, failing an inspection, or being corrected in front of peers teaches a child to recover without drama — the single most useful founder trait there is. Fourth, mixing with children from very different backgrounds, which is the closest thing school offers to managing a real workforce.

None of this guarantees a business. Markets, timing, capital and luck matter enormously. What the school does is give a child habits that stay useful whichever career they choose — which is why alumni show up in administration, science, politics, sport and business as well as in uniform.

## The alumni network: the hidden advantage

There is one more practical advantage worth knowing about. Sainik School alumni associations exist at school level and beyond, and the All India Sainik Schools Alumni Meet held in New Delhi in 2026 — bringing together more than 150 alumni — proposed a national association with a directory, mentorship programmes, career guidance, internships, scholarships and, explicitly, encouragement of entrepreneurship and startup collaboration among alumni. For a young graduate starting a business, that kind of network — seniors who have done it before, in the same system — is often worth more than any single lesson.

Parents choosing a school should ask about this, not just about NDA selections: how active is the alumni association, and do old students return to mentor cadets?

## A founder's checklist for parents (and cadets)

| What to build at school | How it shows up later in business |
|---|---|
| A daily routine you keep without supervision | Running a business when nobody tells you what to do |
| Leading a house, team or club | Managing employees and partners |
| Recovering from public failure | Surviving a failed product or a lost client |
| Living with people unlike you | Hiring and keeping a diverse team |
| Saving, budgeting and managing small allowances | Cash-flow discipline in the early years |
| Asking seniors for help | Using a mentor and alumni network properly |

If your child is already in a Sainik School and talks about business, encourage the checklist, not the dream. The dream takes care of itself; the habits need building now. And if your child wants a non-defence career altogether, that is not a failure of the school's purpose — it is one of the outcomes the system has produced for decades. ## FAQs

**Did any Sainik School alumnus found a major Indian company?**

Yes. Captain G. R. Gopinath, an alumnus of Sainik School, Bijapur, founded Air Deccan, India's first low-cost airline. Pawan Munjal, an alumnus of Sainik School, Kapurthala, leads Hero MotoCorp as its Chairman and CEO.

**Does Sainik School prepare students only for the armed forces?**

No. The schools were founded to prepare students for the National Defence Academy, but their alumni also work in administration, corporate leadership, science, politics, sports and business.

**What skills from Sainik School life transfer to running a business?**

Discipline under routine, leading small teams early, coping with setbacks without drama, and working with people from very different backgrounds — all of which founders name as useful in business.

**Should my child join a Sainik School if they want to become an entrepreneur?**

Choose the school for what it teaches — discipline, leadership and resilience — not for any promise of a business career. Entrepreneurship depends on the individual, the idea and the market, and no school guarantees it.

**Is there a Sainik School alumni network that helps entrepreneurs?**

Yes, alumni associations exist at school and national level, and the 2026 All India Sainik Schools Alumni Meet in New Delhi proposed a national association with mentorship and startup collaboration among its aims.
