Here is the thing about English in AISSEE Class 9: nobody fears it. Maths gets the tuition, Science gets the revision charts, and English gets "we'll do it in the last week." Then the result comes, and a child who scored well in Maths is sitting below the qualifying line in English — and that is the end of the run, because the bulletin is blunt about it. You need 25% in *every* section, not just overall.

English is 25 questions, 50 marks, in a 400-mark paper. Small. But it is the only section where the whole paper is in a language your child may not be thinking in all day — Class 9 is English only, no medium choice — and it is the section where careless marks leak fastest. With 76 days left to 31 January 2027, this is the right week to give it a proper plan.

## The section, in numbers

| Fact | Detail |
|------|--------|
| Questions | 25 |
| Marks per question | 2 |
| Total marks | 50 of 400 |
| Exam | Sunday, 31 January 2027, 2:00–5:00 PM |
| Paper | 150 questions, 180 minutes, pen-paper OMR |
| Negative marking | None — a wrong answer scores zero |
| Qualifying floor | 25% in each section (12.5 of 50 in English) + 40% aggregate |
| Medium | English only for Class 9 |

Read that qualifying line twice. 25% of 50 is 12.5 marks — and since every question is worth 2 marks, your child needs **at least 7 correct answers (14 marks)** to clear the English floor. Seven questions. That is a low bar, which is exactly why it is embarrassing to miss — and children do miss it, usually because they spent 100 minutes on Maths and reached English with ten minutes left and a tired brain.

## What the bulletin actually asks

The syllabus sits in Appendix III of the NTA Information Bulletin. It lists ten areas, and they are worth reading as a checklist rather than a vague "do grammar":

- **Comprehension** — one or two unseen passages with questions
- **Grammar** — the working rules of the language
- **Vocabulary** — synonyms, antonyms, word meanings in context
- **Sentence correction** — spot the error, fix the sentence
- **Tenses** — the single most-tested grammar topic
- **Modals** — can, could, may, might, must, should, would
- **Narration** — direct to indirect speech
- **Voice** — active to passive
- **Clauses** — mainly noun, adjective and adverb clauses
- **Sentence formation** — jumbled words into a correct sentence

Notice what is *not* there: no essay, no letter, no long writing. Every mark comes from short, objective questions. That is good news. It means English is the most coachable section in the paper — a child who practises 25 questions a day for six weeks will walk in knowing exactly what each question type looks like.

## Where the marks actually leak

**Tenses.** The classic trap is a sentence where the time word and the verb disagree — "Yesterday he *goes* to school." Children who know the rule still miss it under time pressure because they read the sentence once, fast. The fix is boring and it works: every tense question, read the time word first (yesterday, now, tomorrow, since, already), then check the verb.

**Narration.** Direct to indirect speech is a rules game — tense shifts back, "said" becomes "told", "tomorrow" becomes "the next day". Children lose marks here not because it is hard but because they practise it once and never again. Two narration questions a day, for three weeks, and this stops leaking.

**Voice.** Active to passive follows one pattern: object becomes subject, verb becomes "be + past participle". The trap is the tense of "be" — "She writes a letter" becomes "A letter *is written* by her", not "was written". Again, a pattern, not a mystery.

**Vocabulary in context.** The paper rarely asks for a dictionary meaning in isolation. It asks what a word means *in this sentence*. A child who memorises word lists without sentences will pick the first meaning they know. The habit to build: when you meet a new word, write the whole sentence it came in, not just the word.

**Comprehension rushing.** The passage is usually the easiest 8–10 marks in the section — if your child reads it. The mistake is reading the questions first, then hunting for answers in the passage, which doubles the time and halves the accuracy. Read the passage once, properly, then answer. It is faster that way. Really.

## How to split 25–30 minutes on exam day

English deserves a fixed slot, not whatever time is left over. Here is a split that has worked for the children we hear from:

| Part | Questions (approx.) | Time |
|------|--------------------|------|
| Comprehension passage(s) | 8–10 | 8 minutes |
| Grammar: tenses, modals, clauses | 6–7 | 7 minutes |
| Narration and voice | 4–5 | 5 minutes |
| Vocabulary and sentence correction | 5–6 | 5 minutes |
| Re-checking marked answers | — | 2–3 minutes |
| **Total** | **25** | **25–30 minutes** |

Two rules for the day. First, do English in the middle of the paper, not last — after Maths, before fatigue sets in, or right at the start if your child is a fresh-brain reader. Second, never leave an English question blank. There is no negative marking, so a guess is worth 2 marks and a blank is worth nothing. If a question is stuck, mark the best guess, circle the number on the question paper, and come back only if time remains.

## A six-week plan (16 November – 27 December)

This is a plan, not a timetable from NTA — adjust it to your child's school day. Twenty minutes a day is enough; English rewards a little every day far more than a weekend crash.

| Week | Focus | Daily work |
|------|-------|-----------|
| 1 (16–22 Nov) | **Diagnose** | One 25-question mixed set, untimed. List the topics that went wrong — that list is the plan. |
| 2 (23–29 Nov) | **Tenses + modals** | 10 tense questions + 5 modal questions daily, from the Class 8 NCERT English book. |
| 3 (30 Nov–6 Dec) | **Narration + voice** | 5 narration + 5 voice questions daily. Write the rule next to every wrong answer. |
| 4 (7–13 Dec) | **Vocabulary + sentence correction** | 10 new words a day, each in its own sentence. 5 error-spotting questions daily. |
| 5 (14–20 Dec) | **Comprehension** | One unseen passage a day, timed at 8 minutes. No re-reading the passage for answers. |
| 6 (21–27 Dec) | **Full sections** | One full 25-question English section daily, timed at 30 minutes, on an OMR-style sheet. |

From January, English folds into full-length mock papers — two a week, at the real 2 PM slot, with the same 25–30 minute English split every time. The point of the mocks is not the score. It is that the split becomes a habit, so on 31 January your child does not have to think about it.

## Two practice examples (try these tonight)

These are our own practice questions, written in the bulletin's style — not from any real paper.

**1. Narration.** *Rahul said, "I will finish my homework tomorrow."* Which indirect form is correct?
(a) Rahul said that he will finish his homework tomorrow.
(b) Rahul said that he would finish his homework the next day.
(c) Rahul said that he would finish his homework tomorrow.
(d) Rahul told that he will finish his homework the next day.

Answer: (b). "Will" shifts back to "would" and "tomorrow" becomes "the next day". If your child picked (c), the tense is right but the time word is wrong — that is the trap, every time.

**2. Voice.** *The teacher praised the cadet.* Which passive form is correct?
(a) The cadet is praised by the teacher.
(b) The cadet was praised by the teacher.
(c) The cadet has been praised by the teacher.
(d) The cadet had been praised by the teacher.

Answer: (b). "Praised" is simple past, so the passive keeps simple past — "was praised". Option (a) changes the tense to present, which is the most common error in voice questions.

## The honest summary

English will not win your child a Sainik School seat on its own — Maths, at 200 marks, does the heavy lifting. But English can *lose* the seat, quietly, through the 25% section floor, while everyone was watching the Maths score. Seven correct answers is all it takes to be safe. That is a very small ask for 76 days of twenty-minute days.

Start this week. One mixed set, untimed, tonight — and write down what went wrong. That list is the whole plan.

## FAQs

**How many marks is English in AISSEE Class 9?**
English is 25 questions of 2 marks each, worth 50 marks out of the 400-mark Class 9 paper. The paper has 150 questions in 180 minutes, and there is no negative marking.

**What is the English syllabus for AISSEE Class 9?**
The NTA bulletin's Appendix III lists comprehension, grammar, vocabulary, sentence correction, tenses, modals, narration, voice, clauses and sentence formation. Read the appendix itself rather than any coaching handout, because it is the final topic list.

**What is the minimum score needed in English to qualify in AISSEE 2027?**
For Sainik Schools, a candidate needs at least 25% in each section and 40% in aggregate. For English that means at least 12.5 of 50 marks, so in practice at least 7 correct answers (14 marks). The 25% per-section rule does not apply to SC/ST candidates.

**Is the AISSEE Class 9 paper in English only?**
Yes. Class 9 is English only. Class 6 candidates can choose from 13 languages, but Class 9 has no medium choice, so every section — including Maths word problems — is read in English.

**How much time should my child give English in the AISSEE Class 9 exam?**
Plan 25 to 30 minutes for the 25 English questions, roughly 60 to 70 seconds each. That leaves about 150 minutes for Maths, Intelligence, Science and Social Science, and a few minutes at the end to check the OMR sheet.

**Does English matter if my child is strong in Maths?**
Yes, because of the section-wise qualifying rule. Maths is 200 of 400 marks, but a child who scores below 25% in English does not qualify for a Sainik School seat however high the Maths score is. English is the section most often left to the last week, and that is exactly why it decides merit lists.
