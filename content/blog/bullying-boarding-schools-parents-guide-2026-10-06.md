This is the conversation parents have in whispers, not in open forums. You are about to send your eleven-year-old — your child, who still asks you to kill the cockroach in the bathroom — to live with older teenagers, far from home, in a place where seniors give orders and juniors obey. The question sits in every parent's chest: *what if someone picks on my child?*

Let us answer it properly. Not with reassurance theatre, and not with fear. With what is actually in place, what you should actually watch for, and what you should actually do.

## What is actually in place

Sainik Schools are CBSE-affiliated. That matters here, because CBSE does not treat bullying as an internal matter to be handled quietly. Under **CBSE Circular No. Acad.-17/2015 (dated 9 March 2015)**, the board issued detailed guidelines for the prevention of bullying and ragging in all its affiliated schools. Among other things, the circular provides for an **Anti-Bullying Committee** in the school — comprising the Vice-Principal, a senior teacher, the school doctor, the counsellor, a PTA representative, a management representative, a legal representative and peer educators — with the members' names and contact numbers to be displayed on the school premises.

The circular also lays out a graded ladder of responses: oral and written warnings, suspension, withholding or cancelling results, a fine, transfer to another school — and expulsion or rustication, reserved for the rarest of rare cases. Separately, the **CBSE Affiliation Bye-Laws 2018** require every secondary and senior secondary school to appoint a full-time counsellor and wellness teacher.

In a Sainik School this sits alongside the boarding structure itself: housemasters live with the cadets, and the senior-junior chain of command that worries parents is also the structure the school uses to keep order. The honest summary: the system has guardrails, and they are not decorative. But no system replaces a parent who is paying attention.

## The part schools can't do for you: spotting the signs

Children rarely say "I am being bullied." They say "I don't want to go back after the holidays," or they stop calling, or their marks slide. Boarding makes this harder — you see your child on a screen or hear them on a call — so you have to read patterns, not moments.

| Sign | What it looks like on a call/home visit | When to worry |
|---|---|---|
| Reluctance to return | Dreads the end of vacations, invents reasons to stay | When it appears suddenly, not in the first month |
| Physical marks | Scratches, bruises, torn clothes, damaged belongings | Any unexplained injury — ask, don't assume |
| Withdrawal | Short calls, monosyllabic answers, avoids friends' names | When a chatty child goes quiet for weeks |
| Academic slide | Sudden drop in marks despite same effort | Correlates with other signs above |
| Appetite/sleep | Eating poorly, disturbed sleep, frequent headaches | Persistent, not exam-season noise |
| Money requests | Asks for extra cash without clear reason | Repeated requests that don't add up |

One sign is a phase — homesickness, exam stress, a bad week. **A cluster of three or more, persisting beyond a fortnight, is a signal.** Write down what you observe with dates. If you ever need to escalate, a dated record of what you saw and when you reported it is the most powerful thing a parent can carry.

## Who to contact — in this order

Don't go to social media first. Don't call the most senior person you know. The order matters, because skipping steps usually slows things down.

1. **The housemaster.** The resident staff member of your child's house/hostel. They see daily life up close and can act fastest. Ask for their direct contact at admission time — don't wait for a crisis to hunt for the number.
2. **The school counsellor.** Required full-time under CBSE affiliation rules. Counsellors are trained for exactly this, and children often open up to them more easily than to authority figures.
3. **The Vice-Principal or Principal.** If the housemaster's response is slow or inadequate, escalate in writing — an email creates a record that a phone call doesn't.
4. **The anti-bullying committee.** The circular requires member names and contacts to be displayed on school premises. This is your formal channel if the school is not acting.
5. **Outside the school** — only if the school genuinely fails to act: the district education authorities. This is the last step, not the first, because schools resolve fastest when given the chance to act on their own procedures.

## Prepare your child before admission, not after trouble

The strongest protection against bullying is a child who believes, bone-deep, that telling is not snitching. Build that before the first day:

- **Teach reporting as strength.** "If someone troubles you, you tell the housemaster the same day. Strong cadets report early." Make it a rule, not a suggestion.
- **Name three trusted adults.** Housemaster, counsellor, one favourite teacher. A child who can name them can reach them.
- **Practise assertive responses.** Not aggression — a clear, loud "stop it" and walking away toward people. Bullies test; most back off when the cost of continuing is an audience.
- **Agree on a code phrase.** Scheduled hostel calls can feel monitored. A pre-agreed ordinary-sounding phrase — "How is Dad's back pain?" when Dad has no back pain — lets your child signal distress without saying it aloud.
- **Visit together before admission.** Walk the hostel, meet the housemaster, eat in the mess if allowed. A familiar place is a less frightening place, and a child who has met the housemaster once will knock on that door sooner.

## FAQs

**Is bullying common in Sainik Schools?**
Boarding life is hierarchical — seniors and juniors live together — so friction can happen anywhere. But Sainik Schools are CBSE-affiliated, and CBSE's 2015 guidelines require every affiliated school to run anti-bullying structures. What matters is not whether incidents ever occur, but how quickly and openly the school responds.

**What is the difference between ragging and bullying?**
Ragging is initiation-style harassment by seniors of new students; bullying is repeated aggressive behaviour targeting a weaker or isolated student, physical or psychological. CBSE's Circular No. Acad.-17/2015 covers both in affiliated schools, and schools can escalate from warnings and suspension to rustication in the rarest of rare cases.

**What are the warning signs that my child is being bullied in the hostel?**
Watch for reluctance to return after vacations, sudden grade drops, damaged belongings or unexplained injuries, lost appetite or disturbed sleep, withdrawal from calls home, and requests for extra money without a clear reason. One sign is a phase; a cluster of them is a signal.

**Who should parents contact if their child is bullied in a Sainik School?**
Start with the housemaster (the hostel's resident staff member) and the school counsellor — CBSE affiliation rules require a full-time counsellor in every secondary school. If the response is inadequate, escalate to the Vice-Principal or Principal, and then to the school's anti-bullying committee, whose members' names and contact numbers must be displayed on school premises.

**Can my child call home freely if something goes wrong in the hostel?**
Policies vary by school — most Sainik Schools restrict personal mobile phones for cadets but allow scheduled calls from the hostel. Ask the school for the exact communication policy during the admission visit, and give your child a pre-agreed phrase they can use on calls if they are upset but cannot speak openly.

**How can parents prepare their child against bullying before admission?**
Teach them to report early rather than endure, practise assertive (not aggressive) responses, make sure they can name three trusted adults at school, normalise telling parents everything, and visit the hostel together before admission so the environment is not a shock.
