The entrance is cleared. The admission letter is on the fridge. And then your child disappears behind the school gate for the first time — and the phone call on day three is a crying one.

Welcome to month one. Almost every parent of a Sainik School cadet lives this month, and almost every one of them reports the same arc: panic, doubt, a difficult call to a friend, and then — quietly, around week three — a child who sounds like themselves again.

If you have not yet read our [first-day guide](/blog/first-day-at-sainik-school-what-to-expect-2026-10-07/), start there for reporting-day logistics. This piece is about what happens after: the homesickness curve, the settling of the routine, and the single hardest judgement call — when to step in and when to stay out of the way.

## The homesickness curve: what to expect, week by week

Homesickness is not a flaw in the system; it is the system working. A ten-year-old who has never slept away from home is adjusting to a dormitory, a schedule, and dozens of strangers at once. Here is the pattern that repeats year after year:

| Week | What usually happens | What you should do |
|---|---|---|
| **Days 1–3** | Excitement carries them. Everything is new — the uniform, the dorm, the drill commands. Calls home are cheerful and full of detail. | Enjoy it. Keep calls short and warm. Don't over-promise visits. |
| **Days 4–10** | The low point. Routine fatigue sets in, minor frictions appear (a lost sock, a scolding for being late to PT), and the novelty wears off. This is when most "I want to come home" calls happen. | Stay calm, normalise it, and do not make promises you cannot keep. See "when to intervene" below. |
| **Days 11–21** | The turn. Friendships form — usually the child finds two or three dorm-mates they click with. The routine starts to feel automatic rather than exhausting. Calls get shorter and less emotional. | Celebrate the normalisation. Ask about friends and games, not about missing home. |
| **Days 22–30** | Settled, mostly. The child starts referring to the school as "my school". Parents often report the first unprompted call that is about something funny that happened at school. | This is the payoff of the previous three weeks. Let the attachment transfer happen. |

The timeline is not a rule — some children settle in ten days, a few take six weeks. But the shape of the curve is remarkably consistent, and knowing it exists turns week one's tears from a crisis into a phase.

## The routine: why the exhaustion is normal

The Sainik School day is deliberately full, and for a new student it is a shock. A typical pattern:

- **Early wake-up** (around 5:30–6:00 AM), often with physical training or drill
- **Morning classes** after breakfast
- **Afternoon classes**, then games/sports in the evening
- **Supervised evening prep** (study time) after dinner
- **Lights out** by around 9:30–10:00 PM

In week one, a new student may be getting a full day's exercise after years of none, sleeping less than they are used to, and concentrating harder than ever — all at once. Exhaustion in weeks one and two is a physical fact, not a sign the child cannot cope. It fades as fitness and sleep discipline build, usually by week three.

One practical tip: do not judge how your child is doing from the first Sunday call. Sunday is rest day, which is when they have time to feel everything. Weekday evenings are the better barometer.

## When parents should intervene — and when they should not

This is the crux. Here is the line, drawn plainly:

**Step back when:**
- Your child cries on calls but is eating, sleeping, and attending everything. Emotional adjustment is happening.
- The complaints are vague ("I don't like it") rather than specific.
- You are tempted to call the school every day. The school has handled thousands of first months; daily parental intervention usually prolongs adjustment.
- They ask to come home in week one or two. Nearly all of them do. Nearly none of them mean it in week four.

**Step in when:**
- The distress gets **worse** after week two instead of easing — more tears, not fewer.
- There are **physical symptoms**: persistent loss of appetite, inability to sleep, frequent headaches or stomach pain with no medical cause found.
- Your child reports **specific incidents**: bullying, ragging, being singled out. Do not wait for this to pass — report it formally to the school. (See our [parents' guide to bullying in boarding schools](/blog/bullying-boarding-schools-parents-guide-2026-10-06/) for how to escalate properly.)
- A teacher or house master contacts you with a concern. When the school raises a flag, it means it.

The correct first contact is always the **house master or housemistress** — the staff member who lives with the students and sees them outside class. Going over their head to the principal on day five over homesickness will mark you as a panicking parent; a calm conversation with the house master gets you real information.

## What helps from home (without getting in the way)

1. **Keep calls short and forward-looking.** Ten minutes of "what was the best part of today?" beats forty minutes of "do you miss us?"
2. **Send letters or parcels occasionally.** A letter is something they can re-read on a bad day; it is also proof of home that doesn't reset the adjustment clock the way an unscheduled visit does.
3. **Never bargain.** "Just finish this term and we'll think about it" gives the child a countdown to cling to instead of a reason to settle.
4. **Talk to senior parents.** Every school has families whose children are in Class 8 or 10 — their first-month stories are the best antidote to your own panic.
5. **Take care of your own adjustment.** Parents go through their own curve. A worried parent's tone is contagious on phone calls.

## One month later

By the end of the first month, the children who cried in week two are usually the ones racing their dorm-mates to the mess hall. The routine that exhausted them is now the structure they defend — ask any cadet how they feel about their morning run and watch the pride surface.

Month one is the price of everything Sainik School gives your child in the years after: discipline, independence, and friendships forged in shared early mornings. It is a hard month. It is also, for most families, the last time it is ever that hard.

## FAQs

**How long does it take for a child to adjust to Sainik School?**

Most students settle into the routine within three to four weeks. The roughest patch is usually days three to ten, and phone calls home get noticeably cheerier by the end of week three.

**Is homesickness normal in the first month at Sainik School?**

Yes — it is the expected pattern, not a warning sign. The vast majority of new cadets go through a homesickness curve that peaks in the first two weeks and fades as routines and friendships take hold.

**Should parents visit during the first month at Sainik School?**

Follow the school's visiting schedule. Unscheduled visits in the first weeks usually reset the adjustment clock instead of helping it.

**What is the daily routine like for new Sainik School students?**

An early start, morning physical training, classes, games in the evening, supervised prep time, and lights out. The exact timetable varies by school, but the structure is broadly the same across Sainik Schools.

**What should parents do if their child wants to leave in the first month?**

Take it seriously but don't decide in week one. Talk to the house master first, give the adjustment a full month, and act only if distress persists or deepens rather than eases.

**Does bullying happen in Sainik Schools during the first month?**

Schools have anti-ragging rules and supervision, but any hostel can have friction between new students. If your child reports specific incidents, report them to the school formally rather than waiting to see if it passes.
