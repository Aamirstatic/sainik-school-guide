## The problem is not what your child knows. It is the language the question is asked in.

Your child is in Class 8, studying in Hindi medium. They know what a prime number is. They know the Revolt of 1857. They know what a panchayat does. Ask them in Hindi and they will answer in seconds.

Now put the same question in front of them in English — *"Which of the following is the smallest prime number?"* — and watch what happens. Not confusion about the maths. A pause. A re-read. A second re-read. The answer was always there. The language was in the way.

That is the whole story of the AISSEE Class 9 paper for Hindi-medium children. The paper is in **English only** — all five sections, all 150 questions, all 400 marks. Class 6 is different: it is offered in **13 languages**, including Hindi. But Class 9 has no choice. The bulletin is clear, and it has been clear for years.

So let us be honest about two things. First, this is a real disadvantage, and pretending it is not helps nobody. Second, it is a fixable one — and 72 days is enough to fix it, because your child does not need to learn new subjects. They need to learn the English words for subjects they already know.

## Where the English actually hurts — section by section

Not every section is equally hard in English. Knowing where the damage happens tells you where to spend the 20 to 30 minutes a day.

| Section | Questions | How hard is the English? | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mathematics** | 50 (200 marks) | Easiest to bridge | Numbers and symbols are the same in every language. Only the instruction words change — "find the remainder", "twice the number", "the perimeter of". |
| **Intelligence** | 25 (50 marks) | Easy to moderate | Mostly patterns, series and figures. The verbal analogies need English vocabulary, but the non-verbal half needs almost no language at all. |
| **English** | 25 (50 marks) | Hardest by definition | This section tests the language itself — comprehension, error spotting, narration, voice. It cannot be translated around. |
| **General Science** | 25 (50 marks) | Moderate to hard | The concepts are familiar, but the questions are sentences. A child must read "which of these is a chemical change" and understand every word. |
| **Social Science** | 25 (50 marks) | Hardest after English | Long questions about History, Civics and Geography, with options that differ by one word — "first" versus "only", "before" versus "after". |

The pattern is clear: the more words a question has, the more the language matters. Maths is the safest section for a Hindi-medium child, and it is worth half the paper. Social Science and Science are where the bridge needs the most work. ## The 72-day bridge: 20 November to 31 January

The plan has three phases, and the rule throughout is simple: **learn in the language you understand, practise in the language of the exam.**

| Phase | Dates | What to do every day | Done when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Phase 1 — Words first** | 20 Nov – 10 Dec (3 weeks) | 20 minutes: build the vocabulary notebook. Every subject gets one page: maths words, science words, SST words. Ten new words a day, each with its Hindi meaning and one example question. | 200+ exam words collected; child can read a Class 8 English chapter and understand it without translating every line |
| **Phase 2 — English practice** | 11 Dec – 10 Jan (4.5 weeks) | 30 minutes: one full section a day in English — alternate Maths, Science, SST and English. Previous-year papers and mock sections only, always in English, timed. Wrong answers go into the error notebook in English. | 25+ English sections attempted; the child stops translating questions in their head and starts reading them directly |
| **Phase 3 — Full papers in English** | 11 Jan – 30 Jan (3 weeks) | Full 180-minute papers in English, one every Sunday at 2 PM — the real exam time — plus error analysis the same evening. Weekdays: revise the vocabulary notebook and the error book only. | 3 full English papers done; the child finishes within 180 minutes without language panic |

The winter break, in the last week of December, is the best window in this plan. School is off, the house is quiet — use it for Phase 2's heaviest week. Our [winter-break sprint guide]({{< relref "aissee-2027-winter-break-10-day-revision-sprint.md" >}}) shows how to structure those ten days; give English practice a slot every single day of it.

## The vocabulary notebook: the one tool that matters most

Buy a thin notebook today. Divide it into four pages: Maths, Science, SST, English. Every time your child meets an English word they did not know in a question, it goes in the notebook — the word, its Hindi meaning, and the question it came from.

Start with the words that appear in almost every paper. A few to begin with — and your child will collect the rest themselves:

- **Maths:** remainder, quotient, twice, thrice, perimeter, area, volume, fraction, decimal, ratio, proportion, average, simple interest, square root, factor, multiple
- **Science:** friction, reflection, refraction, combustion, reproduction, micro-organism, adolescent, hormone, conservation, pollution, eclipse, current
- **Social Science:** constitution, parliament, franchise, democracy, panchayat, resources, agriculture, industry, colonial, revolt, reform, movement
- **English:** comprehension, synonym, antonym, narration, voice, tense, error, improvement, passage

Two hundred words sounds like a lot. It is ten a day for three weeks — and each one is a word your child already knows in Hindi. They are not learning new ideas. They are putting English labels on ideas they own.

## Five habits that build the bridge

**1. Read the question in English first — always.** From today, no Hindi translation of a question before attempting it. Read it in English, twice if needed, then answer. The first week will feel slow. By the third week it will feel normal. That change alone is worth more than any book.

**2. Do every mock in English, even the ones from Hindi books.** If a practice book gives Hindi questions, translate only the ones your child gets wrong — and write the English version in the error notebook. The exam will not offer a Hindi version. Practice must match the exam.

**3. Read one page of English every day — not a textbook.** A children's newspaper, a simple English storybook, the captions under a diagram in the NCERT English book. Fifteen minutes of easy English reading builds the habit of thinking in English faster than any grammar drill. The Class 9 English section has a comprehension passage every year; a child who reads English daily walks into it calm.

**4. Say the answer in English before writing it.** For Science and SST, ask your child to explain the answer aloud in English — one or two sentences. "The panchayat is the local government of a village." If they can say it, they can read it in a question. If they can only say it in Hindi, the English question will still slow them down.

**5. Never let English practice eat subject practice.** The bridge is 20 to 30 minutes a day, on top of normal study — not instead of it. A child who spends two hours on English grammar and skips Maths revision has made a bad trade. Maths is 200 marks and the easiest section to bridge. Protect it.

## The traps to avoid

**Trap 1: "My child is weak in English, so Class 9 is not for them."** Wrong. The paper tests Class 8 subjects in English, not English literature. A child who knows the syllabus and can read simple English can clear it. The English section is only 50 of 400 marks — and the 25% qualifying rule needs just 12.5 marks there.

**Trap 2: Switching every book to English at once.** A child who suddenly cannot understand their own textbooks will lose confidence and marks in every subject. Learn the chapter in Hindi if that is faster, then do its questions in English. The exam tests answers, not the language of your notes.

**Trap 3: Translating word by word in the exam hall.** This is the slowest way to read a paper, and the paper is 180 minutes for 150 questions. Phase 2 of this plan exists precisely to kill the translation habit before January. By the third full mock, your child should be reading questions directly, not converting them.

**Trap 4: Ignoring the qualifying rule.** Every section needs **25%** — 12.5 marks out of 50 in English, Science and SST. A Hindi-medium child who focuses only on Maths can fail on a section they never practised in English. Our [guide to the 25% rule]({{< relref "aissee-2027-qualifying-marks-25-percent-rule-worked-examples.md" >}}) works through the exact arithmetic.

## A small story, then the checklist

A father called us last month. His son was in Class 8 in a Hindi-medium school in a small town, scoring 90% in school exams — and 40% in his first AISSEE mock. The father was ready to give up on Class 9 entirely. We looked at the mock together: the boy had left 18 questions blank, not because he did not know the answers, but because he had not finished reading them. We did nothing fancy — 20 minutes of English questions a day, the vocabulary notebook, one English section every evening. Four weeks later his mock was 68%. The syllabus had not changed. The child had not changed. Only the language had stopped being a wall.

That is what 72 days can do. Start the notebook tonight.

## FAQs

**Is the AISSEE Class 9 paper really in English only?**

Yes. The Class 9 paper is in English only — all five sections, all 150 questions. Class 6 is different: it is offered in 13 languages, including Hindi. A Class 9 applicant cannot choose Hindi as the paper medium.

**My child studies in Hindi medium. Can they still clear AISSEE Class 9?**

Yes — the syllabus is the same NCERT Class 8 syllabus they already study, only the language of the questions changes. What they need is 72 days of practice reading and answering in English, not a new syllabus. The plan in this article builds that bridge section by section.

**Which section is hardest in English for a Hindi-medium child?**

Usually Social Science and General Science, because their questions are sentences, not numbers — a child must understand the English to find the answer. Maths is the easiest to bridge, because numbers and symbols are the same in every language; only the instruction words change.

**How much English practice does a Hindi-medium child need every day?**

About 20 to 30 minutes a day, on top of normal study — one English mock section or one chapter read in English, plus a small vocabulary notebook. That is enough over 72 days, because the child already knows the subjects; they are only learning the English words for what they know.

**Should my child switch to English-medium books for AISSEE Class 9?**

For practice, yes — at least for mock papers and previous-year questions, which must be attempted in English. For learning a new chapter, the Hindi book is still fine; read the chapter in Hindi, then do its questions in English. Switching everything to English at once usually slows the child down.

**Can my child choose Hindi for the AISSEE Class 6 paper instead?**

Yes. Class 6 candidates can choose from 13 languages, including Hindi. But the choice is made on the form, before the 2 December 2026 deadline, and it cannot be changed later — so decide before you submit.
