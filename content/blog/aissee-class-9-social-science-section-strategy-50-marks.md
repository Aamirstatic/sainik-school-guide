Ask a Class 9 aspirant what they are revising for AISSEE 2027 and you will hear the same answer every time: maths. Then science. Social Science almost never comes up — and that is exactly why it decides so many results.

Here is the thing. The Class 9 paper is 400 marks, and Mathematics alone is 200 of them. Everyone knows that. But Social Science is 50 marks — 25 questions, 2 marks each — the same weight as English, the same as General Science, the same as Intelligence. One-eighth of the paper. And unlike maths, it does not need a formula sheet or a coaching class. It needs a child who has actually read their Class 8 Social Science book instead of skimming it the night before the exam.

## The section, in numbers

| Section | Questions | Marks | Share of paper |
|---------|-----------|-------|----------------|
| Mathematics | 50 | 200 | 50% |
| English | 25 | 50 | 12.5% |
| Intelligence | 25 | 50 | 12.5% |
| General Science | 25 | 50 | 12.5% |
| **Social Science** | **25** | **50** | **12.5%** |
| **Total** | **150** | **400** | **100%** |

The exam runs 180 minutes, pen-and-paper on an OMR sheet, in **English only**, with **no negative marking** — so every Social Science question your child attempts is a free shot at 2 marks. But there is a catch that makes this section more dangerous than it looks: your child must score at least **25% in each section** and **40% in aggregate** to qualify (the rule does not apply to SC/ST candidates in the 33 Sainik Schools). A child who scores 170 in maths and 10 out of 50 in Social Science doesn't just lose 40 marks. The child fails the paper.

Last year, a parent called us in a panic because her son's mock scores were "good" — 270 out of 400 — and yet the coaching centre had marked him as not qualified. The maths was fine. The Social Science score was 11 out of 50. One section, one blind spot, and the whole paper was gone. That is the story of this section.

## The four strands — and what each one actually asks

The syllabus in Appendix III of the bulletin has four strands. They are not equal in how children experience them, so study them differently.

| Strand | What it covers | How to study it |
|--------|---------------|-----------------|
| **History** | Beginnings of Indian civilisation, cultural roots, early cities and states, the rise of empires, the Gupta era, the Marathas, the colonial era | Timelines, not paragraphs. Make one line per era — who, when, what changed — and revise the line, not the chapter. Dates and sequences are what get asked. |
| **Geography** | Geographical diversity of India, resources and their conservation, weather and climate, landforms and life, locating places on the earth, oceans and continents | Maps, not memory. Keep an atlas open while you read. If your child cannot point to the Deccan plateau or the Thar on a blank map, the chapter is not done. |
| **Civics** | Types of government, the Constitution of India, universal franchise and the electoral system, the Parliament system, grassroots democracy | Logic, not rote learning. If your child understands *why* we have a Parliament and what a panchayat actually does, the facts follow. If they only memorise articles, the questions that ask "why" will beat them. |
| **Economics** | From barter to money, markets, economic activity around us | Everyday examples. A question about markets is really a question about the shop your child walks past every day. Connect the chapter to real life and it sticks. |

One honest warning: some coaching portals list extra topics — modern Indian history, the freedom struggle, the Revolt of 1857 — that belong to older syllabi or to general preparation material. The bulletin list above is the official one. Use NCERT Class 8 Social Science as the base book and tick each bulletin topic off against its index. If a topic is not in the NCERT book, it is still in the syllabus — but the NCERT book is where the questions come from.

## The 11-week plan: from tonight to 31 January

Eleven weeks is exactly enough for this section, because it is a reading subject, not a practice subject. It does not need 200 problems a week. It needs steady, short sittings.

| Weeks | Focus | What "done" looks like |
|-------|-------|------------------------|
| 1–3 (16 Nov – 6 Dec) | History — all of it, one era at a time | A one-page timeline your child can redraw from memory in 10 minutes |
| 4–6 (7 – 27 Dec) | Geography — diversity, resources, climate, maps | Your child can locate 20 places on a blank India map and explain two climate zones |
| 7–8 (28 Dec – 10 Jan) | Civics + Economics — Constitution, Parliament, panchayats, markets | Your child can explain, in their own words, how a law is made and what a panchayat does |
| 9–10 (11 – 24 Jan) | Previous papers + weak-strand revision | Two full Social Science sections from past papers, timed at 30 minutes each, with every wrong answer written down and re-learned |
| 11 (25 – 31 Jan) | Light revision only | Timeline + map + one page of Civics notes, read twice. No new topics. |

Thirty minutes a day, five days a week, is enough. This is not a section that rewards cramming — it rewards the child who has seen the material three times by exam day.

## Exam-day tactics for these 50 marks

**Do Social Science early, not last.** It is the fastest section to finish when the reading is done. Put it second or third in your child's attempt order, right after the section they are most confident in. A quick 25–30 minutes here banks marks and builds momentum for the long maths stretch.

**Read the question, then the options — twice.** Social Science questions are the easiest to misread in the whole paper, because two options often look nearly identical and differ by one word: "first" versus "only", "before" versus "after". The child who rushes this section loses marks they already knew.

**Attempt everything.** There is no negative marking, and the 25% qualifying minimum means a blank question is a guaranteed zero. A guessed answer in Social Science is worth more than a blank one — and in this section, an educated guess from a half-remembered chapter is right more often than children think.

**Do not carry Class 6 habits into Class 9.** In Class 6, General Knowledge is a separate 50-mark section and the paper is in your child's chosen language. In Class 9, Social Science replaces it, the questions go deeper, and the whole paper is in English. Children from Hindi-medium schools should practise reading History and Civics in English now — not in January.

## Three mistakes that cost these 50 marks

1. **Treating it as a "general knowledge" section.** It is not. It is a syllabus section with a fixed topic list. Random GK reading does not help; the NCERT book does.
2. **Leaving it for the last month.** History timelines and map work need spaced repetition. A child who starts Geography in January will know the chapter names and nothing else.
3. **Ignoring the qualifying rule.** Fifty marks is 12.5% of the paper, but 25% of 50 is just 13 marks — the minimum to stay in the race. A child who scores 12 in Social Science fails even with 300 marks elsewhere. That is the arithmetic that ruins good candidates.
