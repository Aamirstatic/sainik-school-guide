There is a scene playing out in lakhs of homes right now, probably including yours. The child gets home from school around 3, eats, rests a bit, and then the evening becomes a negotiation. School homework on one side. AISSEE practice on the other. The parent wants both done. The child wants neither done. By 9 PM everyone is tired and slightly annoyed, and the prep book is still closed.

Look, the problem was never laziness. The problem is arithmetic: a school day genuinely doesn't have room for two full study shifts. So stop trying to fit two shifts in. What works — for real families, not in theory — is one evening with two *short* blocks, protected like appointments.

## The daily rhythm that actually fits

Forget the 4-hour study plans floating around parent WhatsApp groups. Here's a school-day structure that survives contact with reality:

| Time | Block | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 4:00 – 5:30 PM | Homework slot | School homework only. Phone in another room. When it's done, it's done — no "just one more worksheet." |
| 5:30 – 7:00 PM | Play + dinner | Non-negotiable break. Tired brains don't retain anything; this block protects the next one. |
| 7:00 – 7:40 PM | AISSEE block 1 | One focused set: 25 maths questions, or one comprehension + grammar set. Timed, always. |
| 7:40 – 8:00 PM | Break | Walk, music, talk. No screens if you can manage it. |
| 8:00 – 8:45 PM | AISSEE block 2 | Revision only: formulas, vocabulary, mistakes notebook from the week's mocks. |
| 9:00 PM | Wind down | Reading for pleasure counts. Sleep by 9:30–10 for a 10–12-year-old is not optional — it's study strategy. |

Two things make this work where grander plans fail. First, homework gets a *fixed* slot with a hard stop — it can't sprawl across the evening. Second, AISSEE prep is split into two short blocks because a child's focus genuinely expires after about 40 minutes. One long 90-minute prep session looks productive on paper and produces about 45 minutes of real work.

On weekends, add one longer block: a full timed paper on Sunday morning (150 minutes for Class 6, phone in another room), reviewed the same evening. That's the engine of the whole week.

## The rule that saves the plan: school exams always win

Twice or thrice a term, your child's school will hold unit tests or exams. During those weeks, flip the priority completely: school first, AISSEE shrinks to a 20-minute daily habit — revise formulas, glance at the mistakes notebook, done.

This is not "losing" prep time. A child who bombs a school test because they were secretly doing AISSEE maths walks into January carrying doubt. A child topping their class test walks in carrying confidence. And confidence, honestly, is worth more than any extra chapter. The 20-minute habit keeps the thread alive so restarting after the school exams takes a day, not a week.

## When the week explodes: the cut-first checklist

Some weeks are just impossible — guests at home, a school project, a cold. Don't abandon the plan; shrink it in this order:

- [ ] Cut passive extras first: extra tuition sheets, random mock tests, scrolling through study apps
- [ ] Cut AISSEE block 2 (the revision block) — keep block 1 alive
- [ ] Shrink block 1 to 20 minutes of pure revision, no new questions
- [ ] Never cut: sleep, school homework, the Sunday timed paper (move it to Saturday if needed)
- [ ] After the bad week, don't "compensate" with a marathon — just resume the normal rhythm

Parents usually do this backwards: they protect the extras and sacrifice sleep. Sleep-deprived children forget faster, get irritable, and start hating the books. Protecting sleep *is* protecting the AISSEE score.

## Using the Diwali break wisely

Diwali falls on 8 November this year, right in the middle of the final stretch. Most schools give a few days off. Here's how to use them without burning the child out:

1. **One full timed paper** early in the break, reviewed the same day.
2. **One revision sweep** — formulas, vocabulary, GK notes. No new chapters.
3. **Genuine rest.** Festivals, family, sleep. A rested child in mid-November outperforms an exhausted one every time.

That's it. Three jobs for the whole break. The parents who turn Diwali week into a boot camp usually spend December repairing the damage.

## What to say when your child says "it's too much"

They will say it. When they do, don't argue and don't lecture — shrink the plan *with* them. Try: "You're right, this week is heavy. So until your school test is over, we do only 20 minutes of revision a day. You pick the time." A plan the child helped shrink is a plan they'll follow. A plan imposed on a tired child becomes the reason they start hiding the prep book.

And here's something worth remembering at 9 PM on a hard day: the AISSEE rewards steady children, not heroic ones. One hundred and seven days of the rhythm above beats three weeks of heroic effort followed by burnout. You're not behind. You're exactly where a planned, rested child should be.

## FAQs

**How do I fit AISSEE preparation into a school day with homework?**
Use two short focused blocks: about 30 to 40 minutes before dinner and 40 to 60 minutes after dinner, plus longer weekend blocks. Keep school homework in its own fixed slot first so it never bleeds into prep time or bedtime.

**Should my child reduce school homework to study more for AISSEE?**
No. Homework reinforces the same maths and language fundamentals the AISSEE tests, and falling behind in school damages confidence and marks. During school exams, school takes full priority and AISSEE prep shrinks to a light daily revision habit.

**How many hours of AISSEE study does a Class 6 child need daily?**
Roughly 1 to 1.5 focused hours on school days is enough for most Class 6 aspirants, with longer blocks on weekends. Class 9 students, with a wider syllabus, usually need some extra weekday depth. Consistency across months beats any single marathon session.

**What should we cut first when the week gets too packed?**
Cut passive extras first: extra tuition sheets, random mock tests, and scrolling through study apps. Never cut sleep or school homework. Tired children and school backlogs destroy preparation faster than any missed practice set.

**How should we use the Diwali break for AISSEE preparation?**
Treat the Diwali holidays as a focused revision block, not a full study camp. One timed full paper, one round of formula and vocabulary revision, and real rest. Children who study through every festival arrive at January exhausted.

**What do I say when my child says there is just too much to do?**
Agree with them first, then shrink the plan together. Say: you're right, this week is heavy, so we will do only 20 minutes of revision a day until your school test is over. A plan the child helped shrink is a plan they will actually follow.
