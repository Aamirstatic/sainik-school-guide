Picture the scene: it is Sunday evening, the house feels too quiet, and on the weekly phone call your 11-year-old's voice cracks on the second sentence. He is fine — he just misses home. You hang up and spend the night wondering whether you have made a terrible mistake.

You have not. And he is not the only one. Nearly every cadet who joins a Sainik School in Class 6 — most of them between 10 and 12 — goes through some version of this. Homesickness is not a sign that your child is weak or that boarding school was the wrong call. It is the normal, predictable response of a child adjusting to a genuinely big life change. What matters is what you do next.

## The first six weeks: what actually happens

Counsellors who work with boarding schools describe a fairly consistent adjustment curve. It helps to know it in advance, so you can read the signs instead of fearing them.

| Week | What you may see | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | Excitement mixed with evening tears; "I want to come home" on phone calls | Normal — the routine is new and everything feels big |
| 3–4 | Calls get shorter; complaints shift from "I miss home" to "the food is boring" | Good sign — missing home is being replaced by daily-life concerns |
| 5–6 | Friends' names start appearing in conversation; calls become chatty | Settling — the dorm is becoming "his place" |
| 7+ | Still emotional after a bad day, but recovers by morning | Normal long-term rhythm of boarding life |

The important thing: the child who was crying in week two and laughing in week six is not two different children. That is one adjustment curve doing its work. If your school has a [settling-in or visiting routine](/blog/how-often-can-parents-visit-sainik-school/), follow it — the schedule exists because decades of boarding experience say it works.

## The hard truths parents get wrong

**"He is crying, so he is not suited for this."** Crying in the first weeks says nothing about suitability. What predicts who settles is not toughness — it is routine, friendships, and time.

**"If I visit more, he will feel better."** This one is counter-intuitive, and schools will tell you the same: frequent unscheduled visits in the first term can *restart* the homesickness clock. Every visit is a reunion, and every reunion is followed by another goodbye. Short, predictable phone calls and the school's scheduled visiting days (see our [guide on visiting rules](/blog/how-often-can-parents-visit-sainik-school/)) work better than surprise appearances.

**"I should tell him boarding school is punishment."** Never frame the struggle as his failure. If anything goes wrong, make sure he hears: *"This feeling is normal. It is supposed to feel hard at first. That does not mean you are doing anything wrong."* Children who believe homesickness means something is wrong with them hide it, and hidden sadness is the one that festers.

**"The school will handle everything."** The school handles a lot — housemasters, wardens and senior cadets are genuinely good at this — but you are still the parent. Your job is steady, warm, boring consistency: the same calm voice, the same "I'm proud of you," every single call.

## What actually helps: a parent's checklist

- [ ] **Normalise it before it starts.** Before the joining day, say plainly: "You will probably feel like coming home for a few weeks. Almost everyone does. It passes." A child who expects it is not ambushed by it.
- [ ] **Keep calls short and scheduled.** Ten to fifteen minutes of cheerful, predictable contact beats an hour of tearful goodbyes. End on an up note — even if you have to fake it.
- [ ] **Ask about his day, not his feelings, first.** "Who did you sit with at dinner?" builds a life. "Are you sad?" builds a case file. He will tell you he misses home anyway; let the conversation be about the new life too.
- [ ] **Send small, permitted things.** A note in the laundry bag, a family photo for the locker — check the [hostel life rules](/blog/sainik-school-hostel-life/) for what is allowed. Tangible home beats verbal home.
- [ ] **Never promise an early exit to stop the tears.** "Just one more month" as a bargaining chip turns every week into a countdown. If withdrawal is ever truly on the table, decide it calmly with the housemaster — not on a tearful phone call.
- [ ] **Practise separations before joining.** Even for current cadets, this applies to terms after holidays: a week of sleeping at a grandparent's house, a school camp — small rehearsals make the real thing less strange.
- [ ] **Talk to the housemaster, not around him.** If you are worried, call the school. Housemasters see the child all day — their read on "settling" vs "struggling" is far more accurate than your read from one phone call.

## When to worry — the real red flags

Normal homesickness fades. Escalating distress does not. Call the housemaster and the school counsellor — promptly, not after another month — if you notice any of these:

1. **No improvement after six weeks.** The adjustment curve should be visibly bending upward. Flat or worsening is a signal.
2. **Withdrawal.** Stopped making friends, eats alone, skips games or house activities.
3. **Body keeping the score.** Frequent headaches, stomach aches or loss of appetite with no medical cause — children carry emotional distress in their bodies.
4. **Grades collapsing.** Homesick children can keep up for a while, but sustained sadness leaks into concentration.
5. **Any mention of self-harm, or "I don't want to live like this."** Take it at face value every single time, and involve professionals immediately.

None of this means the school failed, and none of it means your child failed. It means the two of them need a plan together — and schools have counsellors precisely for this.

## One more thing only a parent can do

Before the joining day, read our [readiness checklist](/blog/is-your-child-ready-for-sainik-school-boarding-life/) with your child — not as a test he must pass, but as a conversation. The homesick phase passes for almost everyone, and most cadets later describe it as the thing that made them tougher. Your steady voice on the other end of that Sunday call is a large part of why.

## FAQs

**Is homesickness normal in Sainik School?**
Completely. Nearly every cadet, especially Class 6 joiners around age 10–12, goes through it in the first weeks. It typically fades as routines settle, usually within four to six weeks, and it is not a sign the child is unsuited for boarding life.

**When should parents worry about homesickness?**
Worry when it does not improve after six weeks, or when you see signs beyond tears: withdrawal from friends, falling grades, loss of appetite, physical complaints with no medical cause, or any talk of self-harm. In those cases, speak to the housemaster and the school counsellor promptly.

**Should parents visit often when the child is homesick?**
Counter-intuitively, frequent early visits can make homesickness worse — the child resets to square one each time. Follow the school's visiting rules, keep phone calls short and predictable, and let the house staff set the rhythm in the first term.

**What can parents do before joining to reduce homesickness?**
Practise small separations, build sleep and routine discipline, teach basic self-care skills, and talk about homesickness openly before it happens — normalising it in advance takes away its power.

**Can homesick children be pulled out of Sainik School?**
Yes, parents can withdraw their child, but it is worth waiting through the first six weeks and talking to the housemaster and counsellor first. Most children who struggle in week two are settled and happy by week six — the adjustment curve is real.
